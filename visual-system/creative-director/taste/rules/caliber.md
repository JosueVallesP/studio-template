# Caliber

**The rule: the brand shows the real level of the person's work, no higher and no lower.** The proof lives in `brief.md` under "Proof of caliber." Every direction and every piece is judged on whether it carries that proof to a stranger.

## Why it matters

People judge depth fast, from surface signals, before they read a word. A person with deep work and a brand that looks like a template gets read as a template. A person with thin work and a brand full of status symbols gets read as a hustler once anyone looks closer. Both cost them the people they most want to reach.

## Signals that tend to read as depth

These are possibilities to weigh against the brief, not a checklist.

- **Specificity.** Their own words, their own frameworks named, real numbers, real places, real work shown.
- **Proof shown rather than claimed.** The work itself on the page (the book, the diagram, the result) instead of adjectives about it.
- **Restraint.** Fewer elements, each doing a job. Space used on purpose.
- **Consistency over time.** The same small set of shapes, type and color across years of work, so the body of work adds up.
- **Craft in the details.** Type set with care, alignment that holds, images chosen rather than grabbed.
- **A point of view.** Something in the look that someone in their field might disagree with.

## Overselling

The brand claims more than the person can back up, or claims it in a way their audience distrusts.

- Superlatives and status props: luxury stock, private jets, stages, "world-class."
- Numbers on the page the brief can't support.
- A look borrowed from a far bigger or flashier brand than the person's work.
- Loud for its own sake: effects, motion or color that the audience reads as selling.

## Underselling

The brand hides or flattens the person's real level.

- The default look of whatever tool made it: default fonts, default layouts, default icons.
- Generic stock imagery standing in for their real work.
- Proof buried, missing or crammed in small type.
- Cramming: so much on one canvas that nothing reads as important.
- A look that would suit someone ten years earlier in their career.

## Calibrate to the audience

Caliber is read by someone. Check `brief.md` for who that is and what they need to believe. A thesis-driven investor, a technical founder and a faith-tech operator read the same signals differently. When the audience and the person's taste pull apart, the audience wins on anything they need to trust, and the person's taste wins everywhere else.

## Questions a reviewer asks

1. Which line in "Proof of caliber" does this piece carry? If none, why does it exist?
2. Would someone who knows this person's work say it looks like them at their best, or like someone smaller or louder?
3. Is anything on the page claimed rather than shown?
4. Would the audience in the brief trust this on first look?
