# Provenance: dsn-palette

Everything about where the knowledge in `SKILL.md` came from. Nothing here tells you what to do; it tells you how much to trust each instruction and who said it.

**Blanket caption warning for this topic.** Auto-captions destroy colour information specifically. Colour names are substituted, hex codes are transcribed as word fragments, and the vocabulary of colour ("hue", "tone", "shade", "tint") is interchanged almost at random. **No hex value anywhere in these six transcripts is recoverable, and no colour name in any of them can be trusted.** Sample colours from the artefact, never from a transcript. Specific manglings are listed per source.

## The six sources

### 1. Creative Arts Workshop, "Albers' Color Class"
`fdZlIwyZSHo`, 67m, ~55k views. Fritz Horstman, education director at the Josef and Anni Albers Foundation.

**The canonical source and the spine of this skill.** A live workshop running the first exercise from Albers' *Interaction of Color* (1963) with cut paper, on camera, including the attempts that fail. Uniquely supplies:

- colour relativity demonstrated rather than asserted: one colour made to appear as two, with the proof flap [00:05:12, 00:05:55]
- the factual fact against the actual fact, meaning the hex against what a viewer sees [00:06:50]
- the three channels of contrast, hue, saturation and value, defined and separated [00:24:29 to 00:28:04]
- value as light reaching the eye, and therefore observer-dependent and legitimately arguable [00:32:03]
- strong colours resist being pushed, weak ones move easily [00:51:11, 00:52:08]
- soft edge against hard edge, where a colour close to its surround nearly disappears [00:35:36]
- the hue-eating effect: a blue on pale blue washes to flat grey while the same blue on red stays blue [00:39:22]
- temperature shift as a subtle hue shift [00:38:27]
- optical mixing at small scale, from the one demonstration that failed: a fine red mesh blended with its background instead of shifting [00:48:15]
- the eye-fatigue workaround, reading both fields at the edge of vision from a central fixation [00:22:46]
- family resemblance as a way to talk about hue distance, with two blues "speaking the blue language" [01:00:16]
- the method itself: cut paper rather than paint, so that dozens of combinations can be tried in an hour [00:12:51, 00:43:18, 00:43:56]
- a participant's observation, confirmed on air, that when inside and outside share a hue the inside loses its own [00:59:28 to 01:00:16]

**Caption warning:** the subject's own name is rendered "al Burruss", "Elmer's", "Elly Albers" and "aalverson". The 1953 student's name, the two living painters named at [01:03:45], the two Albers students named at [00:44:43], and "Johan Wolf Gang Von Gerta" (Goethe) are all unrecoverable. Every colour word in the demonstration passages is suspect. **Do not cite a name or a colour from this transcript.**

### 2. Chris Oatley, "Interacting With Color: Insights From Josef Albers"
`R2sCpXbE8R4`, 32m, ~52k views. The same Fritz Horstman, interviewed about his book.

**Same lineage as source 1, so it corroborates nothing.** It is kept because it states the method's rationale in a way the workshop does not, being talk rather than demonstration. Uniquely supplies:

- colour relativity named as the first idea in the book, with the yellow/green/blue thought experiment [00:03:38, 00:04:20]
- Albers' hostility to colour theory, placed in line with Goethe: there is no theory of colour, look only to the blue of the sky [00:02:59, 00:03:38]
- **intervals,** meaning the distance between two colours, named as central to Albers' teaching, with the musician analogy: someone who cannot hear which note is higher should probably not make music [00:18:10, 00:20:50]
- two training exercises: the grayscale gradation, and the illusion of transparency as the first genuinely applicable payoff [00:16:56, 00:19:33]
- the taste warning: students reach for the colours they are wearing, and naming that is the point [00:25:44]
- "they look good together" refused as an answer, with "well tell me how" as the required follow-up [00:26:25]
- colour altering perceived size, from an Albers assignment where identical grid holes read larger on yellow than on blue [00:21:31]
- a high tolerance for ambiguity named as the useful artistic skill, since there is no right answer here [00:26:25]

**Caption warning:** "Albert" throughout means Albers, "bow house" means Bauhaus, "Micah" means MICA, "B trup" means Bottrop, "Gerta" means Goethe. The two artists discussed at [00:28:40] are garbled past recovery.

### 3. Flux Academy, "How To Select Colors: Step By Step"
`Co75kmQtbaA`, 41m, ~273k views.

**The procedural source,** and the only one with a stated end-to-end process. Uniquely supplies:

- the four brief questions asked before any software: constraints, existing guidance, desired action, desired feeling [00:00:44]
- the three-act structure of brief, selection, review, with review as explicit questions about function, appeal and brief compliance [00:35:03]
- hue defined usefully as the answer a two-year-old would give [00:01:28]
- the self-undercutting on psychology: "just using yellow doesn't make something optimistic", and purple does not make a thing regal on its own [00:03:04]
- culture and locality as a veto on hue, with political and sporting associations named [00:03:04]
- one colour plus black and white as a complete and effective scheme [00:10:26]
- the harmony rules walked through in Adobe Color: monochromatic, analogous, complementary, split complementary, double split [00:12:31 to 00:16:02]
- tones matching as the thing that makes a set hang together, demonstrated by rejecting wheel output whose tones clash with the existing blue [00:22:50, 00:23:34]
- the worked accent-inflation pair: a skincare interface that repeats every packaging colour and becomes noise, against a guitar retailer reduced to black, white and one red so the products can be the colour [00:27:27, 00:28:57]
- usage rules as a deliverable, so a brand's secondary does not become its primary by accident [00:34:21]
- the 60/30/10 ratio [00:32:55]

**Caption warning:** "turn on the situation" means turn down the saturation, "primary shoe" means primary hue, "mink"/"karma" are colour and mood words lost in transit. Brand names are mangled ("into milan", "defender guitars", "bert's chris", "pills no longer"). **Every colour name in this transcript is unreliable.**

### 4. Universal Tech Fort (UTF), "Industry-Standard Color System"
`met6BVkv0Ec`, **200m, 302 views.** See the verdict below before using anything from it.

A Figma screencast building a Material Design 3 colour system by hand, then again with a plugin. Supplies:

- the three-category split of key colours: brand or identity, semantic, background or surface [00:03:35]
- **perceived brightness:** yellow, cyan and magenta read lighter than red, green and blue at identical HSB saturation and brightness [00:27:13 to 00:30:37]
- the neutral variant: a grey carrying a small tint of the brand hue, so the whole grey ramp belongs to the brand, with the warm and cool grey consequence [01:06:33, 01:10:55]
- the role-token idea itself, meaning a named slot ("on primary", "primary container") that a specific ramp step fills, and the pairing discipline it enforces [01:36:13 onward]
- competitor inspection as a route to a category hue [00:08:13]

Asserted without justification, by this source alone: halve the primary's brightness to get the secondary [00:21:27]; set the tertiary's brightness between the two [00:24:32]; rotate hue 5 to 8 degrees for the secondary and 20 to 30 for the tertiary [00:42:28, 00:46:51]; 10% saturation for the neutral variant [01:03:50].

**Caption warning: the worst in any block so far.** "Brown color" means brand color throughout, "team" means theme, "lighting" and "darking" mean light and dark theme, "rows" means roles, "sheet" and "sheets" mean shade and shades, "tune" and "tuna palette" mean tone and tonal palette, "two tip" means tooltip, "CSS paper" means CSS Peeper. Hex codes arrive as "0005 AFF", "0058F", "006 DF" and "f into six places". **Nothing numeric or chromatic in this transcript is quotable.**

### 5. Maddy Beard UX, "How to Build a Color Palette for Product Design"
`Eel2E7uVklE`, 13m, ~27k views.

**The densest source per minute in the set,** and the one that solves relativity operationally. Uniquely supplies:

- the primary and secondary role structure: brand colour or colours, plus a light and a dark neutral chosen to recede [00:00:42, 00:01:20]
- never true white and especially never true black, with a little of the brand hue mixed into both [00:01:20, 00:02:00]
- **two tuned variants of the brand colour, one for light backgrounds and one for dark,** because the same colour does not read the same on both; their team names them like times of day [00:03:56]
- the 4.5:1 contrast floor, with the requirement that at least one neutral must pass against the brand colour [00:03:22]
- the alert colour chosen when the palette is built rather than defaulted to later [00:04:32]
- category research so a product reads as its category without copying a competitor's exact colour [00:02:39]
- mid-tone saturated backgrounds ruled out, because nothing reads on them [00:08:05]
- colour blindness at roughly 4.5% of the population, with simulation named as a routine check [00:06:36]
- opacity as a way to extend a small palette without adding colours [00:12:11]

No caption warnings worth recording beyond the blanket rule. Tool names survive intact.

### 6. Spotlight by Teachable, Erik Kennedy live workshop

A live redesign, roughly a third of it colour. Uniquely supplies:

- the picker read as a graph, brightness on one axis and saturation on the other, so any pattern you can see you can state as three numbers [00:38:41]

- the squint test as a hierarchy check [00:20:01]
- a contrast tool that returns the nearest passing shade rather than only a pass or fail verdict [00:28:26]

## Corroboration analysis

**Taught independently by several channels, so treat as real:**

- **Work in HSB and move one channel at a time.** Flux [00:04:37], Maddy [00:05:15], Kennedy [00:21:50], UTF [00:15:01]. Four channels with no shared lineage. The best-corroborated procedure in the set.
- **Tint the neutrals toward the brand hue.** Maddy [00:02:00], Kennedy [00:41:36], UTF [01:06:33]. Three channels reaching it from product UI, from visual design and from a token system. The best-corroborated structural move.
- **Ration the accent; the field around a vivid subject stays quiet.** Flux [00:28:57], Maddy [00:10:07], Kennedy [00:46:17], UTF [00:56:31]. Four.
- **Something must precede the colour picker.** Flux's brief, Kennedy's adjectives, Maddy's category research, UTF's competitor inspection. All four put a written input first, though they disagree about what it is.
- **Contrast as a measured threshold rather than a judgement.** Maddy [00:03:22] and Kennedy [00:27:15], independently, both naming an external standard. UTF claims accessibility repeatedly and never measures once.
- **Colour carries part of a brief and not the whole.** Flux [00:03:04] and Kennedy [00:11:38].

**Note on the two Albers sources:** sources 1 and 2 are the same speaker on the same book. **They count as one source, not two.** Colour relativity has one direct teacher here. It is treated as canonical anyway, because it is the only claim in the block demonstrated live with a falsifiable setup, including a demonstration that failed and was reported as failing [00:48:15], and because Kennedy reaches a compatible finding from a completely different direction [00:23:13]. That convergence is the strongest evidence in the set.

**Single-channel, so treat as house style until corroborated:**

## Where the sources disagree, and the decision rule

### 1. Whether a colour can be chosen out of context

The central conflict. Albers: appearance is produced by the surround, so you rotate backgrounds under candidates and look [00:12:51, 00:43:56]. Flux and UTF: open a wheel or a plugin, apply a rule, read off a set [Flux 00:12:31, UTF 01:14:31].

**Decision rule: a generator enumerates and a render decides.** This is not a split between an old way and a new way. The generator operates on coordinates and appearance is not a coordinate, so the generator is structurally incapable of judging its own output, whatever year it was written. Use it for candidates and never for approval.

Two things make this rule safe to state strongly. Flux's *practice* already follows it even though his stated method does not, since he keeps testing candidates against the real wordmark and says why [00:06:13, 00:08:21]. And UTF, who is furthest from Albers, confirms the mechanism accidentally: his neutral variant exists purely because a grey reads differently depending on what hue is mixed into it [01:10:55].

### 2. Whether colour theory is worth learning

Albers' lineage calls theory antithetical and puts experience first [Oatley 00:02:59]. Kennedy calls it "largely impractical advice" that "couldn't make a bad design look better" [00:05:51]. Flux calls learning the language of design directly valuable [00:13:48]. UTF builds his whole system on a published framework.

**Decision rule: Albers and Kennedy are attacking different targets and both are right against theirs.** Albers rejects theory as a substitute for looking. Kennedy rejects it as a substitute for a procedure. Neither rejects the vocabulary, and both use hue, saturation and value constantly. So: keep the vocabulary, which is what lets you say what you see; drop the harmony rules as a method of approval; add the procedure Albers never supplies.

### 3. What "tone" means

Flux uses tone for tint and shade together, "like adding gray" [00:22:50]. UTF uses tonal value for a position on a 0 to 100 lightness ramp. Albers uses value. These are three different things under one word.

**Decision rule: the word is unusable across this set.** Say hue, saturation or value, and say which one you moved. This is also why `SKILL.md` makes the interval statement a test rather than a note.

### 4. Whether pure black and pure white belong in a palette

Maddy: never, especially black, because it strains the eye [00:01:20]. UTF: tonal value 0 is pure black and 100 is pure white by construction, and he manually forces the endpoints to those values [01:23:37].

**Decision rule: they are talking about different objects.** UTF's endpoints are the mathematical ends of a generated ramp. Maddy's prohibition is about colours you assign to elements. Keep the ramp endpoints if your generator needs them; never assign them.

### 5. Whether the brand colour should come from the ramp

UTF raises and answers this himself, which is the best moment in that source: Material's tokens say the primary role takes ramp step 40, but if a brand already has a colour, taking step 40 instead silently changes the brand [01:46:22, 01:49:58]. **His answer is correct and worth keeping:** key colours override generated tokens for the brand roles. No other source poses the problem.

## Numbers: convention versus derivation

- **4.5:1 contrast for body text.** The only trustworthy number in the set, because it is an external published standard rather than anyone's preference, and two sources cite it independently [Maddy 00:03:22, Kennedy 00:27:15]. Maddy calls it a rule of thumb; Kennedy notes it is contractually required for some clients.
- **60/30/10: convention.** Asserted once, explicitly as something established elsewhere, then fitted retroactively to sites already praised [Flux 00:32:55, 00:33:37]. Nobody derives it, measures it, or offers a range. What it crudely proxies is a weight budget, not an area budget.
- **Material's tone numbers** (40 for the brand role, 100 and 30 for text-on roles, 90 for containers, plus 92, 94, 95, 96 and 98 for surface layers): published Google tokens, read off the documentation [UTF 01:43:16 onward]. Not derived by anyone here, and the claim that the pairs are accessible by construction is demonstrated in that source only by eye, with "you can see there's no issue with contrast" standing in for a measurement [UTF 02:11:21].
- **UTF's derivation tips** (halve the brightness, rotate 5 to 8 degrees, rotate 20 to 30 degrees, 10% saturation): asserted, single-source, no justification offered for any of the four. They produce workable results on his one example and nothing establishes that they generalise. Treat as one person's defaults.
- **Roughly 4.5% colour blindness** [Maddy 00:06:36]. Approximately right for a mixed population, though the figure is normally quoted per sex. Safe to use as "about one viewer in twenty".
- **Kennedy's sample of "almost 100 of the best sites"** [00:22:33]. Self-reported, no list, no method. His finding is useful and its evidence is an assertion about a sample nobody can see.
- **How far a colour shifts in a given surround: no number from anybody.** This is the largest gap in the mechanics and it is deliberate on Albers' side rather than an oversight. It is why the instrument throughout `SKILL.md` is a render.
- **How many colours is too many: no number either.** Flux offers "the fewest amount necessary" [00:09:45], which is a direction and not a limit.

## Canon rules the skill leans on

From the studio's design canon, not the six videos.

- **Cheap iteration is the medium's structural advantage** [Maeda, craft design.md rule 24]. The closest canon match to Albers' method: cut paper rather than paint, specifically so that trying thirty combinations costs nothing [Albers 00:43:18]. The two arguments are the same argument sixty years apart.
- **Scale is a relationship, not a dimension** [craft design.md, discount list]. Appearance is a relationship too, not a coordinate, which is the formal reason a hex cannot be approved and a generator cannot judge.
- **Review order is semantics, then syntax, then understandability, then visual power** [Vignelli, craft design.md rule 5]. Colour sits in syntax and visual power, so a palette review that runs before the piece's meaning is settled is running the list backwards.
- **One dumb thing plus variations, and the recognition-without-the-mark test** [Scher, craft art-direction.md rule 11]. The argument for fixing one palette across a body of work: only a repeated colour set accumulates recognition.
- **Write tolerance bands as prohibitions, not aspirations** [Scher, craft art-direction.md rule 11]. How usage rules should be written: not "use blue primarily" but "the brand colour is never used for body text".
- **Aptness is the named worst model capability** [craft art-direction.md, needs-a-human]. Why an agent can shortlist a hue from category convention and cannot pick one.

## Not covered by these sources

- **Print, ink, CMYK, spot colour and gamut.** All six are screen-only. A palette approved by everything in `SKILL.md` can still be unprintable.
- **Display variance and colour management.** Albers notes that two viewers at different angles see different values [00:32:03] and nobody follows the thought to calibration, profiles or device difference, which is the modern form of the same problem.
- **Sampling a palette from a photograph.** Half of Josué's post work involves an image, and no source here derives a palette from one. Kennedy comes closest by choosing imagery to match a palette, which is the reverse operation.
- **Dark mode as a design problem.** UTF covers it as a token-set problem, which is not the same thing. Nobody discusses what changes conceptually when the field inverts.
- **Gradients.** One exercise [Oatley 00:16:56] and one example [Flux 00:38:14]. No mechanics.
- **Any measurement of anything colour is claimed to achieve.** Trust, calm, appetite, youthfulness and premium feel are all asserted from examples. The one measurable claim in the set, contrast, is about legibility rather than effect.
- **Data visualisation palettes.** Sequential, diverging and categorical sets have requirements that cut directly against several rules here, including the tinted-neutral move. Do not apply this skill to a chart.
- **Non-Western colour association,** beyond Flux's single caution that associations are local [00:03:04].
- **Colour in motion and video.**
- **A model for how many colours a system needs,** or for when a palette has become a design system.
