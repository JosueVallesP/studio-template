# dsn-grid: provenance

Where everything in `SKILL.md` came from, how well corroborated it is, where the
sources disagree, and what none of them reach.

## Corroboration summary

Five sources across five independent channels, plus a canon tier of two talks.
One of the five reviews the discipline's primary printed reference
(Müller-Brockmann), which connects the set to the literature rather than leaving
it as five people's house style. **This is the best-corroborated skill in the
plugin**, and `SKILL.md` says so, because it is the one place a reader can lean
hard on the content.

What several independent channels teach, and is therefore safe:

- Margin as the boundary for information, not for ink
- Grid type taxonomy (modular, column, hierarchical, and the alternatives)
- Column measure governed by reading comfort
- Composite column counts, for the number of splits they yield
- Gutter sized by how separate adjacent things must read
- Pencil sketching before software
- A shared grid is what makes a set read as a set
- The grid as retired decisions rather than as decoration

What rests on a single channel, and is flagged inline in `SKILL.md`:

- Choosing a grid from the audience's task (Satori only)
- Column count derived from the spans the content needs (Satori only)
- Span assignment by priority (Satori only)
- Comping at the exact finished ratio (Jectives only; Flux sketches but does not
  state the ratio rule)
- Shoot and crop to the module, and the three repairs (Jectives only)
- The static-versus-asymmetric repair, three and two (Flux only)
- The explicit sub-guide break inside a column (GraphiqVibe only)

## The sources

**Jectives, "Grid Systems in Graphic Design: Book Review and Flip-Through"**
(`MNMTjffhRcU`, 44 min). A review of Müller-Brockmann's printed reference, the
only source connected to the discipline's primary literature. Treated here as
reasoning about a system, never as a route to the book's text or plates.
Uniquely supplies: the derived reasons behind margin asymmetry, binding
[00:09:43] and thumbs [00:09:43], and behind column measure, eye travel
[00:07:45]; leading growing with type size [00:09:04]; comping at the exact
finished ratio [00:18:56, 00:20:17], with 16 squares by 9 on gridded paper for
16:9 [00:19:37]; shooting and cropping to the module and the three repairs when
an image does not fit [00:21:47, 00:23:13]; overlaid type and image grids as an
advanced move, with an honest admission of difficulty, "my brain starts to
break" [00:29:04, 00:30:26]; a shared grid as what makes a series read as a
series [00:27:44]; the grid-as-investment argument [00:16:19, 00:42:08]; the
A-series halving note, and that US letter and legal are "divisible by nothing"
[00:04:30]; and the sheet-music reading of rhythm across a sequence [00:37:54].
Also uniquely useful for criticising its own authority: the reviewer flags the
1:1 comping rule as aged out, naming typewriters, Letraset and phototypesetting
as the conditions that made it matter [00:20:56], and says the later chapters
stretch the concept past usefulness, asking of one plate "what vegetable are we
looking at right here that has to do with a grid" [00:36:36]. The 8, 20 and 32
field grids are teaching examples for A4 [00:14:12], not universal counts.

**Flux Academy, "The missing guide to grids"** (`wIabVp0Hh8U`, 24 min, 369k

**Satori Graphics, "The ONLY Grid System Video"** (`QcT9a8gcG74`, 13 min). The

### Two terminology cautions from GraphiqVibe

This is the worked example behind the warning in `SKILL.md` that no term from
these transcripts is quotable unchecked.

1. Its **"baseline grid"** is a set of stacked rectangular bands subdivided into
   rows [00:07:30], which is a band or zone grid. Flux [00:01:21] and Gareth
   [00:06:39] both define a baseline grid as evenly spaced horizontal lines on
   which type sits so baselines align across columns and point sizes, and that
   matches the print literature. Two sources plus the literature against one:
   use the Flux and Gareth definition. GraphiqVibe is describing something real
   and useful under the wrong name.
2. Its grid type rendered throughout the captions as **"exhale grid"** is the
   axial grid, confirmed against Gareth David's correct usage.

## Where the sources disagree, and the decision rule

**1. Grid before content, or content before grid?**
Flux builds margins, five columns and six rows in InDesign first, then imports
copy and photographs, then sketches [00:08:25 onward]. Satori inventories the
content and answers the audience questions first, then derives the column count
[00:00:34]. The reference work constructs the type area from the measure, that
is from the typography, before any layout exists [MNMTjffhRcU 00:07:45]. Flux
optimises for getting unstuck: a grid on the canvas is something to react to.
Satori optimises for a grid that fits this brief.
**Rule: inventory and the three questions always come first, because they cost
nothing and they change the column count. Build the grid second. Flux's order
only works because he already knew the content was a magazine spread.**

**2. Does the reader's task pick the grid, or the designer's idea?**
Gareth: grids are useful "if you have a strong idea initially", and "when you
have a clear idea in mind you can choose the right grid to suit your approach"
[00:11:36]. Satori: the grid follows from what the audience needs to accomplish
[00:04:21]. Gareth optimises for expression, and his route is the only one that
produces axial, diagonal and radial grids. Satori optimises for communication.
**Rule: run the task analysis first. If it returns "the reader's task is to feel
something", that is a legitimate answer and Gareth's route takes over. What is
not legitimate is skipping the analysis because you already like a grid.**

**3. Must the four margins be unequal?**
Flux: no equal margins, bottom double the top, inner 1.5x [00:09:49]. Satori: 60
pixels both sides of a poster, no asymmetry mentioned [00:01:19]. The reference
work derives its asymmetry from binding and thumbs [MNMTjffhRcU 00:09:43].
**Rule: asymmetric margins are a bound-page convention and lose their reason on
an unbound single surface. One part survives: a block centred geometrically
reads slightly low, so a bottom margin visually equal to the top is usually a
little larger. On a feed graphic the asymmetry returns for a different reason
entirely, platform chrome and cropping. Same ratio, different derivation. Do not
inherit the ratio; re-derive it per format.**

**4. What a baseline grid is.** See the GraphiqVibe cautions above.

**5. Is a grid always necessary?**
Gareth, flatly, no [00:12:16]. The reference work treats the grid as the method,
and its own reviewer notes the later chapters stretch the concept past breaking
[MNMTjffhRcU 00:36:36].
**Rule: Gareth. Decided by format and content volume, not by principle. Below
roughly three information groups a grid adds overhead and no constraint.**

## The canon tier

Two talks outside the five, used to judge the material rather than to supply
mechanics.

**Vignelli** (`N4Dz28mgHpo`) is the right canon source, because a grid is his
argument made physical: narrow the variables deliberately, since every extra
variable is a place the system can start to lie, and one typeface cannot be used
inconsistently [00:08:58]. A grid applies that to position: it removes placement
from the set of things that can be done inconsistently. **This is a reliability
argument wearing aesthetic clothes, and it is why a grid matters more for a
system other people, or future you, or an agent, will operate than for a single
piece you finish today.**

The strongest corroboration in the set is that the book reviewer arrives at the
same conclusion independently: "as humans and especially as designers or artists
we think we want complete creative freedom... but really we thrive with
limitations" [MNMTjffhRcU 00:16:57]. Note the mechanism he names, which is not
freedom but *cost*: you solve placement once and then "don't have to solve the
same problem over and over" [00:42:08].

Three judgements imported into `SKILL.md`:

- **Syntax before beauty.** Vignelli's review order is semantics, syntax,
  understandability, and only then visually powerful or elegant; his diagnosis
  of design that does not work is that it is not internally consistent [00:02:14
  to 00:03:04]. For a grid, syntax is the whole test and it is mechanical. Hence
  Mode B runs before any aesthetic judgement.
- **The grid is not where you are inventive.** His preference for full bleed over
  clever layout rests on the claim that inventive page architecture "looks
  obsolete very fast" [00:17:14 to 00:18:01]. Usable version: invention belongs
  in what you place, not in the structure you place it on. A novel grid dates a
  piece fastest and fails the teachability test, because nobody else can operate
  it.
- **Assign the variants.** From Bierut (`7NuKb9mk0ac`), the sharpest tool here.
  His Media Lab identity generated 40,000 valid permutations and failed
  operationally, both because the marketing lead "just sent the same one over and
  over again" and because when every permutation is equally valid none of them
  can *mean* anything, so no variant could be assigned to a group [00:10:56].
  Applied to a grid: write the assignment table, and the grid becomes a
  generator rather than a canvas.

Bierut's structural warning, which applies directly: flexibility moves the work
from design to approval, because somebody has to decide which variant goes on
which thing, and that is where people stop being able to decide. For a
one-person operation, the assignment table is not documentation, it is what
stops the layout being re-litigated on every post.

### Where this canon is dogma rather than derivation

Keep the reliability argument. Discount the following, and know the difference,
because importing the second set wholesale produces a position that cannot be
defended.

- **"A few basic ones and trash the rest"** [N4Dz28mgHpo 00:44:31]. The
  reliability argument justifies *one grid per project*. It does not justify
  ruling out axial or radial grids as a class. That jump is a preference in the
  costume of a principle, and the alternative grids exist precisely for briefs
  where the reader's task is to feel something.
- **Obsolescence as a social crime.** Durability is designable; moral
  superiority about it is an assertion, and it is the sentence that turns a grid
  preference into a reason not to listen to anyone.
- **"Scale, you have it or you don't"** [00:45:23]. True as observation,
  unfalsifiable as a standard; in review it functions to end discussion. Usable
  residue: scale is a relationship, so it cannot be briefed numerically and must
  be judged by eye against the whole. Which is also why it is listed as
  something an agent cannot do.
- **Timelessness.** "Timeless" and "modernist Italian c. 1965" are not the same
  thing, and the talk conflates them. Bierut's formulation survives scrutiny and
  Vignelli's does not: it cannot start to look dated because it looks dated
  already [7NuKb9mk0ac 00:23:56]. A Swiss modular grid is a period style that
  ages slowly, not a neutral substrate. Choosing one is a style decision.
- **His own counter-evidence.** Vignelli designed a cup for demitasse drinkers,
  Americans fill a cup to the brim, coffee ran over the rim into a saucer with no
  gutter and onto the tablecloth, and he tells it as a story about American
  habits. **Deriving a grid from a print page and applying it to a phone screen
  is the identical error.**

## Not covered by these sources

- **Variable platform crops.** The central constraint on Josué's actual output.
  All five sources are silent, so the crop-safe rows and the 182 bottom margin
  in `SKILL.md` are extrapolation, not a sourced finding.
- **Responsive and fluid grids.** Flux sets a 12 column Figma frame and stops.
  Nothing on breakpoints, container queries, or behaviour between two fixed
  widths.
- **Implementation.** No CSS Grid, flexbox, auto-layout or constraint systems.
- **Accessibility.** No minimum type sizes, no touch targets, no contrast
  interaction with dense modular layouts. Absent from the canon tier too.
- **Deriving the baseline or spacing unit from a type scale.** All five assert a
  number (10px, 12px); none derives it.
- **Non-Latin scripts, right-to-left setting, vertical setting.** Absent. The
  reference work is mid-century European and presents its taxonomy as universal.
- **Grids for data display.** Charts, tables and dashboards have their own
  alignment problem; none of these touch it.
- **Motion.** Nothing on how a grid governs animation or transition.
- **Deliberate gridlessness as a craft.** Gareth concedes much design is made
  without a grid [BQEVqWiD304 00:12:16] and then says nothing about how.

## Changed by the cross-skill numeric reconciliation

The shared floor is two figures, not one: 36px at the nominal 3x downscale and 48px at the 4x stress case, both delivering about 12pt rendered. A floor quoted without its case is incomplete. The framing is established in `reference/canvas-assumptions.md`, which also records that nothing shared fixes margins, columns or gutters.

- **Test 6.** Previously gave 48px as the floor and named only the 4x case, which contradicted the span-assignment table in this same file, where kicker, body and attribution are all set at 36px type. It now gives both floors and fails a piece that states no case at all.
- **Span-assignment table and the character-count note.** The three 36px rows are now labelled as the nominal case, with the stress-case alternative computed beside them: those rows at 48px, which by the same half-the-type-size estimate gives 616 / 24 = 25 characters for the kicker and the body and 456 / 24 = 19 for the attribution. Spans and widths are unchanged, because they derive from the margin and the gutter and not from the type size.
- **The margin derivation.** Now says in the body that the margin is chosen here rather than inherited from the shared canvas file, so the rejection of 96 is a preference and not an illegality, and names the two other triples that also close: 96 with a 24 gutter giving 128px columns, and 90 with a 36 gutter giving 120px. The instruction to take this file's triple whole rather than mixing it with another is new and is the resolution for the column-count conflict across the set.
- **Values unchanged.** 72, 24 and 136 stand, and 72 + (6 x 136) + (5 x 24) + 72 = 1080 still closes, as does the portrait sum 72 + (7 x 136) + (6 x 24) + 182 = 1350.
- **Verified and left alone.** The thumbnail check at roughly 25 percent of 1080 is 270px, which is the same figure `dsn-spacing` uses for its thumbnail test, so the two agree and neither needed editing.
