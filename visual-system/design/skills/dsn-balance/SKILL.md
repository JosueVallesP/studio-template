# Balance and Tension

This skill decides how stable a frame should be, builds it to that target, and judges whether it got there. The single idea underneath it is that stability is a position you choose rather than a quality you achieve, and that the three available positions cost different amounts to look at and survive different amounts of handling. The common failure is not choosing at all, which produces a frame that is competent and invisible. The expensive failure is tuning a frame to the edge of instability, because the tuning is attached to one exact piece of content and nothing warns you when the content changes.

## What this skill is for

- composing a frame and needing to know how stable it should be
- judging a layout that feels wrong without a nameable defect, or one that is competent and invisible
- deciding whether a layout can become a template or must stay hand-tuned
- deciding what an AI content crew may be handed and what it may not
- settling an argument about whether a frame is balanced, by giving it a verdict instead of an adjective

## Confidence

The rules here are practitioner accounts of what works, reasoned into a procedure. None of them is a measured finding: nobody in this field tests balance, and no ratio, threshold or step scale for weight exists anywhere in the material behind this file. Rules marked **(thin)** rest on a single account and should be treated as one person's house style rather than as established mechanics.

The operator rule below needs its own warning. It is a reasoned default rather than a reported finding: its evidence is a single demonstration that deleting a tension device kills the effect, and every step after that, substitution, refilling, templates and crews, is inference. It is the right call on the available reasoning and it is still inference.

## Terms, defined before they are used

Terms that several skills share have one meaning each, in `reference/glossary.md` at the plugin root. Where this section differs from that file, that file wins.

**Visual weight.** How much of a viewer's attention an element takes before they read anything. Weight is optical, meaning a property of how the element looks against what surrounds it, rather than geometric, meaning computed from its area or dimensions. A small dark square can outweigh a large pale one, which is why area alone tells you nothing.

**Tonal value.** How light or dark something is on a scale from black to white, hue ignored. A saturated red and a mid grey can share a tonal value.

**Optical centre versus geometric centre.** The geometric centre is the arithmetic middle, at half the width and half the height. The optical centre is where a viewer perceives the middle to be, slightly above it, because the lower half of a frame reads as heavier at equal area. An element at the geometric centre of a tall frame looks a little low.

**Axis of symmetry.** The line a frame is mirrored about: vertical, horizontal or diagonal.

**Dominance order.** The ranked sequence in which elements are seen, first read to third, short enough to say out loud.

**Level one.** The single element competing for the first read. Dominance is zero-sum: if everything is at level one, nothing is.

**Counterweight.** A second mass placed opposite the dominant one so the frame stops tipping. It answers which side is heavier.

**Counterforce.** A force that holds a frame together after a rule has been deliberately broken, so the break reads as intended rather than as a mistake. It answers what belongs to what. Counterweight and counterforce are different tools for different symptoms.

**Anchor.** The one element chosen first, placed first, and used to derive every other position. It answers what governs.

**Tension device.** One named, deliberate violation of a compositional rule, used to make a frame refuse to settle. The available devices have their own section below.

**Proximity, and the inner-less-than-outer rule.** Elements that belong together sit closer to each other than to anything else. As a measurement: the gap inside a group must be smaller than the gap around it. Break it and the group dissolves.

**Tracking.** Uniform spacing added to or removed from the gaps between every letter in a run of type. Tighter tracking makes a line read as a denser, more unified block.

**Leading.** The vertical distance from one line of type's baseline to the next, in the same units as the type size. Type at 108 pixels on 119 pixels of leading has 11 pixels of extra air per line.

**Measure.** The length of one line of text in characters rather than pixels. Comfortable reading sits around 45 to 75 characters; much shorter and the eye jumps too often, much longer and it loses the line return.

**Ink coverage.** The fraction of a type block's bounding rectangle that is actually dark, roughly a quarter for a headline, the rest being air inside and between the letters. A text block's weight follows its ink, not its box. **(thin)**

**Orthogonal field.** A layout in which every element is square to the frame, nothing tilted.

**Void.** A deliberately empty region large enough to read as an element rather than as margin.

**Displacement.** Offsetting one element from the one it relates to, so position itself becomes the separator.

**Register.** The family an element belongs to: all-angular, all-serious, all-photographic. A register break is one element from outside it.

**Detune.** What happens to a tension device when the content it was tuned against changes: the device is still in the file and no longer produces the effect.

**Operator.** Whoever puts content into this layout next. A colleague, an agent, or you in a week with no memory of the tuning.

**Grey-box.** Replacing every photograph with a flat grey rectangle of the same crop, to see whether balance was carried by the image or by the structure.

**Delivery size.** The pixel size the piece will actually be seen at, rather than the size you build at.

## The three-point axis

Balance and tension are three positions on one axis, not two states with a correct answer, and the piece's job picks the position. The word "tension" gets used for four different things, which is why advice about it appears to contradict itself. Fix the vocabulary first: in this skill tension means a composition deliberately left unresolved, not the eye-pull of a bright accent and not variety. Name your position in words before placing anything.

**1. Symmetry.** The frame is mirrored about a vertical, horizontal or diagonal axis, with matching element count, shape, size, tonal value and spacing on both sides. It is stable and orderly, it survives any substitution of content, it costs a viewer nothing to look at, and it says nothing. Checked by the flip test.

**2. Asymmetric balance.** Unequal weights, resolved by a stated dominance order rather than by matching masses. The frame is active and still settled: the eye has somewhere to go first and does not feel the frame tipping. It survives most substitution, because the resolution lives in the structure rather than in the measurements. This is the safe default for most work. Checked by the dominance-order test.

**3. Unresolved tension.** One compositional rule deliberately left unpaid, held legible by a named counterforce. The frame refuses to settle, which is what makes it hold attention, and it is fragile: it is tuned to the content in it now and does not survive substitution. Checked by the device-and-counterforce test.

Near-symmetry is the worst position on the axis, and it is not a fourth position so much as a failure to land on one of the three. A mirror that is almost a mirror reads as a production error: too close to symmetry to register as a choice, too far from it to read as order. At small sizes the offset disappears and the frame reads as symmetrical anyway, so the cost is paid at large sizes and the benefit is never collected. Commit to the mirror or leave it. **(thin)**

## The decision ladder

Three steps in order. Either lower step can veto the first.

**1. Count the exposures.** Ask how many times one person will see this frame, and for how long each time. One exposure, under a second, competing with other frames: aim at tension. Repeated exposure, or a single long read: aim at balance. The mechanism is that instability is a cost paid on every single viewing, so it is cheap against one glance and expensive against thirty.

Where this fails: a daily ephemeral format, a story frame or a once-a-day post, can afford instability indefinitely, because nobody accumulates exposures to any individual frame even though they see many frames. Count exposures per frame, not per feed.

**2. Name the single energy source.** Decide whether the instability in this piece lives in the layout, the image or the claim, and allow exactly one of the three. If the content is already unstable, a provocative line or a violent photograph, then the layout is the stable element and gets no device. Two energy sources do not add, they compete, and a frame with two of them reads as noise rather than as twice the energy. **(thin)**

**3. Name the operator.** Below. It overrides step 1 more often than not.

## The operator rule

> **Tension is tuned per instance, and it detunes silently when anything in the frame is substituted. So if anyone other than you will refill this layout, including you next week and including an agent, the target is balance. Balance survives substitution.**

This is the most load-bearing practical rule in the file and the one that decides what a content crew may be handed. Everything else here is a judgement about one frame. This is a judgement about a workflow.

**The mechanism.** A tension device is a measured relationship: this gap is 1.4 times the gap next to it, this type block ends 40 pixels before the frame edge, this void occupies 62 percent of the live area. Those measurements were chosen against one specific piece of content, usually a specific number of words at a specific size. Change the content and every one of those relationships moves while the instruction that produced them stays where it is. The void that was 62 percent becomes 48 percent because the headline wrapped to a third line. The 40 pixel edge gap becomes 4 pixels because the word was longer. Neither of those is a visible break. Both of them are the device gone.

**The failure mode, step by step, because it is the thing nobody catches.** The template is approved on the frame it was tuned for, which genuinely works. An operator refills it with a headline two words longer. Nothing errors, nothing overflows, nothing misaligns, and no check in the file can fire, because the file still contains exactly the instruction that was approved. The frame renders, looks broadly fine, and goes out slack: the instability that was doing the work is now just an odd gap. By the time anyone could notice, the only person looking is the audience, and they will not report it, they will scroll. The defect is invisible at the point of production and inaudible at the point of delivery, which is why the rule has to be a gate rather than a caution.

**What follows for a crew.** An asymmetric structure with a fixed dominance order can be handed over and refilled a hundred times, because the structure resolves itself regardless of content length: the first read is still first because it is still the largest and darkest thing in the widest column. A tense layout cannot be handed over at all. So a carousel's first slide is hand-tuned by whoever chose the tension, and every slide after it comes from a stable template. If you find yourself hand-tuning slide seven, the template is wrong and the fix is in the template, not in slide seven.

The only exception is a format that fixes content length to the character, where substitution cannot change the measurements that the device rests on. If the content length is a range rather than a constant, you do not have that exception.

## The principles

**1. Weight is the unit balance is measured in, and it is optical rather than geometric.** Six inputs feed it, all of them relational, which is how you estimate weight before rendering or blurring anything. Checked by the blur test.

- **Size.** The largest element reads first and reads as most important. This holds only next to smaller similar objects: size is a comparison, not a dimension, so an element is not large, it is larger than its neighbours.
- **Tone, which is distance from the background value.** A dark element on a pale field is heavy. The identical element on a dark field nearly disappears, because weight here is the gap between the element's tonal value and the field's, not the element's own darkness. Move the background and you move the weight of everything on it without touching a single element. This is the input people forget, and it is the one that makes a frame tip after a background change that looked harmless.
- **Saturation.** A small patch of saturated colour in a field of muted or neutral tones buys weight far out of proportion to its area, by pulling the eye to it. Nobody can tell you how much area it is worth, so no ratio here is anything but construction. **(thin)**
- **Density, which is complexity against simplicity.** Pattern against plain, many small parts against one large part. A dense block of small type outweighs a larger plain shape of the same tonal value, which is why a stack of captions can tip a frame that a photograph was supposed to hold. Density and tone are separate inputs: a dense block can be pale and still heavy. **(thin)**
- **Texture.** The same effect as density at a finer grain, where the complexity is in the surface rather than in the parts. A flat field around one textured or photographic element is what makes that element land. **(thin)**
- **Isolation.** Space around an element makes it heavier, not lighter. Empty space is a weight input and not an absence, which is the reason a single small element in a large void can hold a frame against a mass several times its area.

No one knows how much of one input buys how much of another, so these six let you predict where the weight will land and never let you compute it. The render settles it.

**2. You make an element heavy by deleting what shares its attribute, not by enlarging it.** An element dominates when it is the only thing in the frame with its attribute: the only warm tone, the only photograph, the only thing that is not square. Sole possession is the mechanism, and deletion is the cheapest route to it. Checked by the subtraction test.

Where this fails: when the rivals you would delete are load-bearing information. Subtraction is free only against decoration. If the second and third items are facts the reader needs, keep them and buy the hero's weight another way, usually with tone or isolation. There is also a second route to sole possession that costs nothing to delete, which is to re-tune the rivals so they no longer share the attribute rather than removing them: give them a different tonal value, a different treatment, a different register.

**3. Exactly one element at level one.** Dominance is zero-sum. Two elements of similar weight do not produce two focal points, they produce none, because the eye has no reason to start at either. Checked by the level-one count.

Where this fails: a frame whose job is a grid of equals, a contact sheet or a specimen set, where the absence of a first read is the content. Even then the set as a whole has to be level one against its own caption and chrome.

**4. Every broken rule names the force that replaces it.** A rule broken with nothing paying for it is indistinguishable from not knowing the rule, and a viewer resolves that ambiguity against you. Checked by the counterforce half of the device-and-counterforce test, once per broken rule. **(thin)**

Where this fails: nowhere, and this is the one rule here with no exception. A blank in the counterforce column is an unfinished draft, not a style.

**5. One tension device per frame.** Stacked devices do not compound, because each one needs the rest of the frame to be stable in order to read as a deviation. Three deviations have nothing to deviate from. Checked by the device inventory. **(thin)**

Where this fails: a piece whose actual subject is chaos, overload or collapse, where the stacked devices are the content rather than the treatment. Even then you owe a viewer a first read, so if nothing dominates it is noise wearing the subject as an excuse.

**6. Judge every element in assembly, never alone.** Weight is a property of the frame, so a tonal value or a colour that reads wrong on its own can be the thing making the assembly work, and the reverse is just as common. Any verdict reached from a cropped view, a layers panel or a single artboard in isolation is void: re-render the whole frame and say it again. This is a condition on every test below rather than a test of its own.

**7. Drop anchor before composing.** Pick the one element that governs the frame, place it, and derive every other position from it. The alternative, placing elements in the order they arrive and balancing at the end, produces a frame with no governing relationship, which is the state that reads as competent and arbitrary. **(thin)**

## Tests

Run in this order. Each returns a verdict decidable from this file alone. Principle 6 is a condition on all of them: a verdict reached from a cropped view is void.

| Test | Catches | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Flip** | Symmetry nobody chose | Mirror the frame horizontally and compare. Nothing changed and symmetry was the target: pass. Nothing changed and symmetry was not the target: fail, this is centring rather than composition. |
| **2. Dominance-order** | Asymmetry with no winner | Say the first, second and third read out loud, in three short phrases. An order you can say: pass. Any hesitation about which is first: fail, the asymmetry is an accident. |
| **3. Device-and-counterforce** | Tension that is really an unfinished draft | Write the device in one sentence and its counterforce in one sentence. Both filled: pass. Either blank: fail. |
| **4. Device inventory** | Tension stacking | List the tension devices in use, by name from the device list below. A count of 0 or 1: pass. 2 or more: fail, keep the strongest and drop the rest. |
| **5. Blur** | Weight landing somewhere other than intended | Blur the render until type is unreadable, then name the heavy regions out loud, in the form "upper left heavy, lower right empty". Regions match the target: pass. They do not: fail, however the frame reads sharp. |
| **6. Falling** | A frame that tips | Ask which edge the frame feels like it is tipping toward, and accept "none" as an answer. "None": pass. A named edge: fail, and that edge is where the counterweight goes. **(thin)** |
| **7. Four-fields** | A frame balanced side to side and tipping top to bottom | Run four separate passes: horizontal, vertical, diagonal, and foreground against background. All four settled: pass. Per failing field, report the tipping edge. **(thin)** |
| **8. Subtraction** | Weight bought with scale that should have been bought with deletion | Before scaling the hero up, list every other element carrying the hero's attribute. A deletable rival exists: fail, delete it instead. No deletable rival: pass, scale is allowed. |
| **9. Level-one count** | Everything being the star | Count the elements competing for the first read, as an integer. Exactly 1: pass. Any other number: fail unless you can state the reason in a sentence. |
| **10. Grey-box and substitution render** | Balance carried by today's copy or by this particular image | Refill with the longest and the shortest real content the layout will carry, and replace every photograph with a flat grey rectangle of the same crop. Position holds in all renders: pass, the layout can be templated. Position changes in any of them: fail, this is hand-tuned and cannot be handed to an operator. |
| **11. Delivery-size render** | Tension that exists only at full size | Render at the pixel size the piece will actually be seen at, then at three quarters of that. Device still present: pass. Device gone: fail, it was never in the piece. Edge pressure, voids and small offsets vanish first. |
| **12. Near-symmetry** | A mirror that is almost a mirror | Measure the offset between the two halves, then divide it by the downscale factor the piece will be seen through. Offset reads as zero at delivery size while being visible at working size: fail, commit to the mirror or move it far enough to read as a choice. |

## How to work

### Mode A: composing

1. Run the decision ladder. Write the target in words as symmetry, asymmetric balance or unresolved tension. Never write "balanced" or "dynamic", because neither names a position and neither can be tested.
2. Drop anchor. Pick the one element that governs, place it, derive the rest from it.
3. Compose to the target. For symmetry: draw the axis, then mirror element count, shape, size, tonal value and spacing across it. For asymmetric balance: put the dominant mass off axis, counterweight it opposite with a *different* attribute rather than a matching mass, then state the dominance order out loud. For tension: choose one device, then name its counterforce before building either.
4. Run the blur test against the written target.
5. Run the falling test and the four-fields check.
6. Run the delivery-size render, and the near-symmetry test if the frame is anywhere near a mirror.
7. If this layout will be refilled by anyone, run the grey-box and substitution render before approving it, and treat a fail as a verdict on the template rather than on the frame.
8. For a sequence, fix one structure and let every variation come from the content. Exactly one frame may break the pattern, and that break is both the focal point and the tension for the whole set. Keep each family of elements cousins rather than siblings: one system, no duplicates.

### Mode B: judging

1. Ask the piece's job and its exposure count before looking at the frame. That is what decides whether the position it is in is the right one, and looking first contaminates the question.
2. Name the position the frame is actually in. If it is near-symmetry, stop: that is the finding, and nothing below it will matter until it is resolved.
3. Run the blur test, the falling test and the four-fields check.
4. Run the level-one count and the device inventory, demanding the counterforce for every device you find.
5. Run the grey-box and substitution render.
6. Run the delivery-size render.
7. Report what passed, what failed, and the smallest change that fixes each failure, ordered by impact. Name the element, the gap and the measurement rather than the impression.

## Tension devices, and the counterforce

In rough order of strength on a flat canvas, strongest first.

- **Broken proximity.** A gap pushed past the inner-less-than-outer rule, so that the gap becomes the feature rather than the separator. The caption sits far enough from its image that the distance is doing something.
- **Hard off-centre mass with a large void.** All content pushed to one third of the frame, with the remaining area genuinely empty rather than lightly populated.
- **Edge pressure.** Elements touching the frame border, nearly touching it, or running off it. Near-contact is stronger than contact, because the viewer keeps checking the gap.
- **Angle against an orthogonal field.** One tilted element where everything else is square to the frame.
- **Displacement.** The second line offset from the first, so that position becomes an additional separation layer on top of the gap. **(thin)**
- **Register break.** One element from outside the family: a circle in an all-angular field, a photographic element in a flat minimal one, humour on a serious message. It is the most robust device on the list, because the family it breaks is defined by the piece itself rather than by any convention, so it works in any medium and at any size.

Three counterforces, one per symptom.

- **A stronger grouping force.** Something that holds the group together on its own once proximity is no longer doing it: a shared alignment edge, a column rule, a shared tonal value. Use this when the broken relationship is a grouping. **(thin)**
- **Payment in another currency.** Re-satisfy the broken relation at a different scale. Tighten the tracking of a line so the line itself reads as a denser single block, which re-establishes the grouping the gap gave up, and which also licenses moving the elements closer. Expect the placement to move as well as the tracking: the two are paid together. **(thin)**
- **A grounding element.** A capsule, a rule or a block low in the frame that holds the bottom still while the movement sits above it. Use this when the frame tips rather than when it dissolves. **(thin)**

Diagnose before reaching for one. If a viewer cannot tell what belongs to what, you need a counterforce. If the frame tips toward an edge, you need a counterweight. If nothing dominates, you need an anchor. Those are three different repairs and reaching for the wrong one adds an element without fixing anything.

## Anti-patterns

Two broad habits, then the specific failures.

**Symmetry by default.** The template centred it and nobody decided anything, so the result is stable, competent and invisible: unchosen rather than wrong. Fix: run the exposure count and write a target, then keep the symmetry as a decision or leave it.

**Tension as ego, and tension as house style.** The urge to add instability is usually information about the maker rather than about the piece: a resolved frame's only defect is that nobody will think you were clever. The second half matters more. Tension cannot be repeated without going slack, because a device read twice is a pattern and a pattern is not a deviation, so a tuned instability is a different shape every time and accumulates nothing. A body of work accumulates recognition by repeating one structure with variations, tested by whether somebody can tell whose work it is with the logo covered. Fix: ask whose problem the instability solves, then fix one asymmetric structure for the body of work and let the content supply the variation.

Specific failures, each with the repair.

- **Centred and still tipping.** Static and wrong at once, usually because the horizontal field was checked and the vertical was not. Fix: run the vertical and the foreground-against-background fields as separate passes.
- **Asymmetry with no winner.** Two similar weights, neither dominant, no order anyone can say. Fix: subtract the loser's distinguishing attribute, then state first, second, third out loud.
- **Decorative tension.** Proximity broken with nothing carrying the association, so captions float free of their images and the frame reads as sloppy rather than taut. Fix: add a grouping force, or tighten tracking to pay the rule back. This is the unresolved-draft failure arriving one device at a time.
- **Tension stacking.** A tilt plus edge pressure plus a void plus a register break, which the maker reads as energy and a viewer reads as noise. Fix: keep the strongest device, drop the rest, re-blur.
- **Everything is the star.** Three level-one elements, each enlarged in turn to win, none winning. Fix: subtract the competitors for the hero's attribute rather than enlarging the hero further.
- **Balance carried by the photograph.** Passes on this image and fails on the next, because the image was doing the weighting. Fix: grey-box it, then rebuild the balance into the layout so the image is interchangeable.
- **Balance carried by today's copy.** The frame resolves at four words and tips at eight. This is the operator rule's failure arriving in a single frame. Fix: refill with the longest and shortest real content, then rebuild so both resolve.
- **Tension that is an unresolved draft.** The honest failure, and the most common one among makers who have just learned the device list. Fix: write the device and the counterforce in one sentence each; if you cannot write the second sentence, the frame is unfinished rather than tense.
- **Pretty and inert.** Nothing is wrong, so nothing is interesting, and the frame is fully invisible in a feed. Fix: one register break, chosen for aptness, placed where the eye lands second.

## Applied to a post visual or carousel

Canvas sizes and the downscale factors come from `reference/canvas-assumptions.md` at the plugin root: square post graphics are 1080 x 1080, portrait graphics and carousel slides are 1080 x 1350, the nominal downscale from working file to phone feed is 3x, and the stress test is 4x. The smallest size you can rely on being read is about 12 points, so the working-file floor is 12 x 4 = 48 pixels for anything that must survive the stress test, and 12 x 3 = 36 pixels for anything that only has to survive nominal delivery. Every pixel value below is derived from those figures.

Shared structure for both canvases. Margin 90 pixels on all four sides, which is 1080 / 12 and which renders as 30 points of visible edge at 3x and 22.5 at 4x. That margin is derived here from the canvas width and is not one of the shared figures: the canvas file fixes sizes and downscale factors and fixes nothing about margins, so the 900 pixel live width below and every column figure that rests on it depend on this 90 rather than on `dsn-grid`, which owns grid derivation and starts from 72. Spacing and type both step on a 1.5 ratio from a 48 pixel base, giving 48, 72, 108, 162, 243. The ratio has to be this coarse because a step must survive division by the downscale factor: a 1.125 ratio puts 48 next to 54, which is two points apart once rendered, so it is one level wearing two names.

### Square single frame, or a carousel's first slide: unresolved tension

One exposure, under a second, against a hostile feed, and no lower step vetoes it, so the ladder lands on tension. The device is a hard off-centre mass with a large void, because that is the device that survives being seen small; a tilt or a small displacement does not.

Compute the live area. 1080 - (2 x 90) = 900, so the live area is 900 x 900, running from y = 90 to y = 990.

Build the mass. A headline of two lines at 108 pixels, set on 119 pixels of leading, which is 108 x 1.1 rounded. From the top of line one to the bottom of line two that is 119 + 108 = 227 pixels. Then one spacing step of 48 pixels. Then the caption as a 48 pixel line of type inside a capsule with 24 pixels of padding above and below, so the capsule is 48 + 24 + 24 = 96 pixels tall. Total mass height is 227 + 48 + 96 = 371 pixels.

Place it. Bottom-align the mass to the live area: it runs from y = 990 - 371 = 619 to y = 990. The void above it runs from y = 90 to y = 619, which is 529 pixels, or 529 / 900 = 59 percent of the live area height. So the void is the larger region and the device is real rather than nominal.

Check it survives delivery. At 3x the void is 529 / 3 = 176 points tall against a 371 / 3 = 124 point mass; at 4x it is 132 against 93. The void stays the larger region in both, so the off-centre mass reads at delivery size and test 11 passes. The headline renders at 108 / 3 = 36 points and 108 / 4 = 27 points, both well above the floor. The caption renders at 48 / 3 = 16 points and 48 / 4 = 12 points, which is exactly at the floor, so the caption is legible at the stress test and nothing smaller may carry information a reader needs.

The counterforce is the capsule, named before building: a grounding element low in the frame, holding the bottom still while the void works above. Without it the mass is a block sitting at the bottom of an empty frame, which is the decorative-tension failure.

Second choice of device is edge pressure, tested against the platform's real crop rather than the artboard, because a feed thumbnail is cropped and an element 24 pixels from the artboard edge may simply be cut rather than read as pressing against it. Crop behaviour is unspecified anywhere reliable and changes by surface, so verify it on a real post.

Then run tests 5 and 11, and test 10 if anyone other than you will ever refill this frame. On a hand-tuned first slide, test 10 is expected to fail, and that failure is the result you want: it is what tells you not to make this frame the template.

### Portrait interior slides: asymmetric balance, identical on every slide

They are read in sequence over tens of seconds, so instability fatigues; they are a template refilled with varying copy, so tension would detune silently; and the repetition across slides is what makes the set read as one thing rather than as eight posts. The position is asymmetric balance, the structure is fixed once, and only the content varies.

Compute the live area. 1080 - 180 = 900 wide, 1350 - 180 = 1170 tall.

Build a two-column asymmetric structure. One gutter at 48 pixels, the base spacing step. 900 - 48 = 852 of column width to divide, and a 2 to 1 split gives 852 / 3 = 284, so the narrow column is 284 and the wide column is 568. Check: 568 + 48 + 284 = 900. At 3x those render as 189 and 95 points; at 4x, 142 and 71. Both remain distinguishable as separate columns at both sizes.

Check what measure the wide column can actually carry, because this decides the content rules for the whole set. Body type cannot go below 48 pixels if it must survive the stress test. At 48 pixels, average character width for a typical sans runs around half the type size, so about 24 pixels per character. 568 / 24 = 23.7, so roughly 24 characters per line. Comfortable reading starts around 45 characters, and 45 x 24 = 1080 pixels, which is wider than the entire canvas and considerably wider than the 900 pixel live area. So a 4:5 post graphic cannot carry a comfortable paragraph measure at a legible type size at all. Interior slides carry one idea in short lines, not paragraphs, and that is arithmetic rather than taste.

Set the dominance order by weight, approximately, to find ties. The approximation is area times tonal distance from the background, with text multiplied by its ink coverage, and it ignores saturation and texture entirely, so use it only to catch a tie and never to settle a close call.

First attempt. The wide column holds a headline of three lines at 108 pixels on 119 leading: (119 x 2) + 108 = 346 pixels tall, 568 wide, so 196,528 square pixels of bounding box. At roughly 25 percent ink coverage that is 49,132 square pixels of actual dark. Type at 10 percent tonal value on a field at 94 percent gives a tonal distance of 84 points, so 49,132 x 0.84 = 41,271 weight units. The narrow column holds a square image at 284 x 284 = 80,656 square pixels, mid-tone at about 45 percent, so a distance of 49 points from the field: 80,656 x 0.49 = 39,521 weight units.

41,271 against 39,521 is 1.04 to 1. That is a tie, which is the asymmetry-with-no-winner failure arriving before anything has been rendered. Test 2 would fail on it, and test 9 would return 2.

Second attempt. Take the headline up one step to 162 pixels on 178 leading: (178 x 2) + 162 = 518 pixels tall, 568 wide, 294,224 square pixels of box, 73,556 of ink at 25 percent, times 0.84 = 61,787 weight units. Crop the image to 284 x 190 = 53,960 square pixels, times 0.49 = 26,440 weight units. 61,787 / 26,440 = 2.34 to 1, which is a first read and a second read that anyone can say out loud. Confirm it on a blurred render rather than on the arithmetic, because the arithmetic ignored two of the six inputs.

Fix the structure once and let one slide only break it, the slide carrying the turn in the argument. Run test 10 before approving the template, with the longest and shortest real copy the set will carry, and treat a fail as a verdict on the structure. Keep slide chrome, numbers and rules as cousins across slides: same system, no two identical.

### Brand asset, which is a mark, avatar, header or template chrome: committed symmetry

Thousands of exposures over years, so the exposure count is decisive on its own, and the operator rule agrees: other people will place this asset into contexts you do not control, so it cannot borrow a counterforce from its surroundings. It must resolve entirely on its own, and symmetry is the only position on the axis that does.

The arithmetic makes the same call independently. A profile avatar displays at roughly 48 points. From a 1080 pixel working file that is a downscale of 1080 / 48 = 22.5x, far harsher than the 3x and 4x the feed imposes. The 12 point floor then means nothing in the mark may be smaller than 12 x 22.5 = 270 pixels in the working file, which is 25 percent of the canvas width. Any element under 270 pixels is simply gone at avatar size. A stroke needs about one point of rendered width to exist at all, so the minimum stroke is 22.5 pixels, rounded up to 24, which is 2.2 percent of the width.

The same factor settles near-symmetry. An offset of 24 pixels between two halves renders at 24 / 22.5 = 1.1 points at avatar size, which is below what anyone will perceive, so a near-mirror reads as a mirror when small and as a production error when large. The offset is paid for at every large placement and collects nothing at any small one. To read as a deliberate asymmetry at avatar size, the offset would have to be at least 270 pixels, a quarter of the frame, which is no longer near-symmetry at all. There is no useful middle, which is why the position has to be committed symmetry and why boredom is the feature here.

## Checklist

- [ ] Target written in words as symmetry, asymmetric balance or unresolved tension, with an exposure count behind it
- [ ] Exactly one energy source: layout, image or claim
- [ ] Operator named, and tension used only on frames nobody else will refill
- [ ] Anchor placed first, with every other position derived from it
- [ ] Blurred render inspected, heavy regions match intent, all four fields checked separately
- [ ] Exactly one element at level one, or a stated reason for none, and its weight bought by subtraction before scale
- [ ] Device count 0 or 1, each device with its counterforce written out, and no near-symmetry
- [ ] Passes at delivery size, with the longest and shortest real content, and grey-boxed if imagery is used
- [ ] Across a sequence: one structure, one dominance order, exactly one breaking frame, families kept as cousins

## The ultimate test

**Run tests 10 and 11 together, with the person who will actually operate the frame doing the refilling rather than you.** If the frame is still in the position you chose, with the longest and shortest real content, at the size it will be seen at, the layout is sound on this dimension. If it only works with today's copy at full size, it is a hand-tuned one-off: fine for a single post, a defect in a template. Report which of the two you have, in those words, because the distinction is what the next person needs and it is the one thing a file cannot tell them later.

## What an agent can run, and what it cannot

Can run from this file alone:

- **Proximity audit.** Every gap measured, inner against outer per group, violations listed with their measurements.
- **Tension-device inventory.** Tilted elements, elements within a stated distance of the frame edge, voids above a stated proportion of the live area. Flag any count above one.
- **Counterforce declaration check.** For every declared break, does the brief name a counterforce? A blank is a hard fail.
- **Dominance approximation.** Area times tonal distance from the background, times ink coverage for text, ranked. It ignores saturation and texture, so use it only to flag ties, which is the one thing it does reliably.
- **Measure check.** Column width divided by estimated character width against the comfortable range, which catches a column that cannot carry the copy before anyone sets it.
- **Render generation for every test here.** Blurred, mirrored, thumbnail-size, grey-boxed, refilled with longest and shortest content, so a human looks once at a sheet instead of eleven times at a file.
- **Consistency across a sequence.** Column structure, dominance order and spacing identical on every slide, with exactly one breaking slide.

Cannot run:

- **Judging perceived weight.** Weight is a relationship rather than a dimension, so it cannot be briefed numerically. The only way to reach it is to look at a render, and the approximation above earns its place only because it is honest about being one.
- **How far is too far.** Nobody can state the maximum, in this skill or outside it. Stop at the edge of your judgement and say that is where you stopped.
- **Telling a deliberate break from a mistake.** In the file they are identical. Only the declared counterforce is inspectable, which is the reason principle 4 has no exception.
- **Whether a register break is apt.** Choosing which outside-the-family element to use is the whole of the skill and the hardest thing to delegate.
- **Whether the piece needed tension at all.** That is the exposure count and the job, which come from outside the frame.

On generated work the teaching order inverts, because generators converge on the stable answers: **balance is what you get for free and tension is what you must decide and specify.** Treat that as a reasoned expectation rather than as a measured result.

## Where this skill should lose

**When the question is where the elements go rather than how much they weigh.** Use `dsn-composition`, which owns placement in the frame, focal position and the arrangement as a whole. This skill takes an arrangement and asks whether it tips; it will not tell you where to put the third element.

**When the question is reading order and how many levels there are.** Use `dsn-hierarchy`, which owns the number of levels and the ranking of everything below the first read. This skill only insists that exactly one element occupies level one, and says nothing about how levels two and three should differ.

**When the frame tips because its underlying structure is wrong.** Use `dsn-grid`, which owns columns, gutters and margins. If the balance repair you keep reaching for is a column edge, close this file and fix the structure there, because balancing on top of a bad grid is repair work you will redo on the next frame.

**When the question is how large the gaps should be.** Use `dsn-spacing`, which owns the spacing scale and the inner-less-than-outer rule. This skill breaks that rule deliberately as a tension device, which means it needs the rule to already be set correctly; it is not the place to set it.

**When contrast is a legibility question rather than a weight question.** Use `dsn-contrast`, which owns contrast ratios and whether text can actually be read. Several of the strongest devices here cut directly against legibility: low-saturation accents, near-contact, type at the frame edge. This skill treats tonal value as a weight input and will happily approve a frame nobody can read, so run that check separately and let it win.

**When tonal value and saturation are colour decisions rather than weight inputs.** Use `dsn-color`, which owns what a colour is doing in a frame and the three numbers that describe it, and `dsn-palette`, which owns which colours exist at all and the role each one fills. This skill consumes a tonal value and never chooses one, and it never adds a colour to a set.

**When the heavy element is a photograph and the question is which photograph.** Use `dsn-image`, which chooses the picture, crops it and states what it gives up. This skill needs two facts about a picture before it can weigh it, its tonal value and where its own focal point sits inside the crop, and both are decided there. If the frame tips because the picture is the wrong picture rather than because it is placed wrong, a different picture is the cheaper repair and this file cannot tell you that.

**When the frame has to stand alone and the question is what comes out of it.** Use `dsn-poster`, which decides how many elements and levels one standalone frame can carry and what gets cut to reach that number. This skill decides how stable the survivors are arranged. A frame can be balanced to the exact target chosen here and still hold two elements too many, and no test in this file will report it.

**When the question is what varies across a set and what the set is arguing.** Use `dsn-sequence`, which owns the arc across slides and which slide carries which beat. This skill fixes one structure and names the single breaking frame; it does not decide what that frame should say. Templating divides between the two files and the division is worth stating, because both sound like they own it: this file decides whether a given layout survives having its content swapped, which is the stability test run against substitution, and that file decides which frames of a set are therefore built by hand and which are refilled from a template.

**When balance would be dishonest.** In data visualisation, weighting a frame for stability means moving marks that stand for numbers, so the balance repair becomes a distortion. Accuracy governs there and this skill does not.

Four things nothing here covers, so do not read a verdict from this file as covering them. Instability created by timing rather than placement: the only motion rules in this plugin are the thin ones in `dsn-identity`, and they govern consistency of duration and easing rather than pacing. Right-to-left and non-Latin layout, where mirroring carries directional meaning: no file here covers it. Recomposing across aspect ratios beyond the thumbnail problem: the nearest available procedures are the transfer checks in `dsn-grid` and `dsn-spacing`, which decide whether a borrowed number moves to a new canvas, and nothing covers automatic reflow. And accessibility, of which exactly one part has an owner: the measured contrast floor for a text pair, in `dsn-contrast`, which is not optional. Focus order, alternative text, touch target size and user font scaling have no owner in this plugin, so do not read a contrast pass as an accessibility pass.

## Writing the output

Plain words, full sentences, and the actual element, gap or measurement rather than an abstraction: "the caption sits 40 pixels from the frame edge, which renders at 13 points" beats "the spacing feels tight". Name the position you chose, in the three available words, before reporting anything about the frame. When a reviewer needs one handle, give the verdict in a word: loose, meaning the offset is too small to register and reads as sloppiness; taut, meaning it is held; snapped, meaning the association broke. Give the principle first and the specific change as an option, so the maker can solve it another way. Write tolerance bands as prohibitions rather than aspirations: "no element under 270 pixels" rather than "keep elements reasonably large". Say which tests you ran and what each returned, including the passes, because a pass and a skipped test look identical in a summary that only lists failures. Where you reached the edge of your judgement, say that is where you stopped rather than calling it the limit.
