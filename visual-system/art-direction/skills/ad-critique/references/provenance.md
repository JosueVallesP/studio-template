### The concentration problem, and where it falls

**Five of ten files are one person**, across four distinct sessions and roughly 220 of the corpus's 390 minutes. Everything in the observed procedure rests on those four sessions and is therefore **one voice however many hours it spans**.

The asymmetry matters more than the raw count:

| Task | Independent channels |
|---|---|
| Judging a single draft | **1** |
| Portfolio review | 3 |
| Critique as a process or meeting | 3 |
| Note phrasing | 2 |

**This skill's primary task is the half with the weakest corroboration.** The skill body states this as a confidence line without the arithmetic.

One channel is itself a compilation of practitioners from six companies, so for claims about *how a team runs a crit* it carries more independent weight than one channel; for claims about *judging visual craft* it carries almost none, since it is entirely product process and contains no critique of a piece.

## Claims refused

- **The five-to-eight portfolio band.** Three channels agree and **not one gives a reason, a test or a source.** One arrives as "I want to say"; another as "I have some thoughts on that" seconds later. All three are portfolio-advice creators in one niche who plausibly read each other, so **three-channel agreement here looks like corroboration and may be shared folklore.** Carried in the skill as a convention, explicitly not as a finding.
- **One anecdote converted into a law that it does not support.** A story about a famous designer thumbing through a portfolio in seconds, concluded with *that is how many it takes for somebody to know if you are good*. One encounter, one person, decades ago, and a three-page glance says nothing about how many pieces a portfolio should contain.
- **Self-reported cognition that escalates under social pressure.** A judgment time of sixty seconds becomes five seconds, then possibly less than five, across four lines of dialogue, because a co-host raises the stakes. Nothing measured. The skill carries "well under two minutes" as a convention marked thin.
- **The worst reasoning in the corpus**, and worth naming because it licenses the above: senior people can spot talent from a mile away, and work that is not interesting *never* gets better. **Unfalsifiable by construction**, since the filter's rejects are never observed again, so it is a claim about data the speaker cannot have.
- **The combinatorial argument for crew size.** Counting lines of connection between participants does not establish an optimum. The practical observation that seven or eight is too many is from experience and is kept; the arithmetic dressing is not.
- **The student-tell inventory.** A list of projects said to mark a portfolio as a student's. Observable, and folkloric.
- **Generic closing praise** across about thirty reviews, meaning nice work, nicely done, great job, paired with specific criticism. Not a claim, but the form inverted, and named as a failure in the skill.

**And one structural finding about verdicts.** One portfolio channel closes all thirty reviews positively. **Zero rejections.** No portfolio is told it is not ready; the harshest available outcome is a list of fixes. The notes themselves are not softened: JPEG artifacts, eight-to-ten-second load times, a missing certificate, a wrong copyright year, the weakest piece named in nearly every review. **The conclusion is softened, and the mechanism is the submission funnel**, since the format runs on volunteer submissions solicited at both ends of every episode and a rejection would damage supply. Recorded as an anti-pattern rather than a refused claim, because the material is otherwise good.

**Consequence for the verdict ladder.** Because no portfolio in this corpus is ever told no, the top rung of any verdict ladder, meaning not ready or start again, cannot be sourced from this material. It is in the skill because its absence is a diagnosable failure, not because anyone demonstrated it. The skill's refusal check exists for exactly this reason.

## The critic's own failure modes

Collected because they are the most useful material here: the failures of someone genuinely good at the task, committed on camera.

Editing the submitter's file within minutes rather than critiquing it, to the point where a co-host asks him to duplicate it first so a before-state exists, which is **the exact behaviour his own art-direction session is premised on avoiding.** Rendering verdicts for about seventy minutes and only then noting he has no brief, no research and no client contact. Judging a portfolio before opening it, saying so aloud, conceding afterwards that the work was better once he did, and then repeating the pattern. Assuming intent immediately after saying he dislikes assuming. Letting an unbriefed chat audience change a colour and abandon a piece mid-repair, leaving it worse than he found it. Critiquing inside a culture he does not know and conceding insensitivity only after viewers object. Abandoning pieces on cost rather than reaching a verdict. Straw-manning a co-critic and being corrected live. Roughly twenty-two of seventy-nine minutes of one session going to sponsor reads and announcements, with submissions left unreviewed at the end.

Two of these are directly inheritable by an agent and cheap to avoid: judging before looking properly, and delivering a verdict while missing the brief.

## Gaps and known weaknesses

- **Single-draft critique is one voice.** The skill's core procedure has no independent corroboration. Stated in the skill's confidence line.
- **Judge-first behaviour is an artefact of the format.** Across four sessions and about sixty pieces, the live critic asks a maker a genuine question essentially once. In three of those four sessions the maker is absent or is a stranger in a chat window, so questions have nowhere to go; in the one session with a maker present, the question appears. So judge-first behaviour here is a property of livestream critique rather than a method, and anything built from these transcripts inherits it by accident. The skill refuses it deliberately, siding with the three channels that prescribe the opposite against the one that demonstrates otherwise.
- **No source critiques generated output or a crew's work.** That worked example in the skill is constructed from transferable mechanisms and labelled as such.
- **The measurement procedure requires the source file**, which the same critic lacks in three of his four sessions, and he asserts without measuring in exactly those three. He names the dependency himself, asking for a layered file rather than outlined artwork.
- **No source follows a critique to the next version.** Only one session ends in a commissioned deliverable, and the corpus never shows what came back. Whether any of this produces better work is untested.
- **No example of a well-written note exists anywhere in the corpus.** Across ten videos and roughly 390 minutes, the sources demonstrate critique as speech: fluent, live, improvised, with the maker usually absent. Not once do they show the written artefact a maker would actually receive and work from. So the note *form* in the skill is assembled from rules about speaking, and the output order is constructed rather than modelled. This matters most for the case the skill is likeliest to serve, where the deliverable is written and asynchronous. The skill says so in its output section.
- **Nothing on asynchronous critique of a finished artefact**, which is the dominant mode for anyone reviewing agent output.
- **The quality bar is conceded to be subjective** by the one team that discusses it, set by whoever was present earliest.
- **No canvas or platform-rendering figures come from these sources.** The delivered-size arithmetic in the skill is cited to the design plugin's `reference/canvas-assumptions.md`, which carries its own derivation and its own caveats. Nothing in this corpus addresses feed rendering.
- Not run on live client work.
