# Critiquing someone else's work

This skill takes a piece someone else made and produces notes they can act on without you in the room, plus one verdict. The single idea underneath it: **a critique is a set of causes, not a set of reactions.** A reaction tells the maker how you felt; a cause tells them which decision produced the effect, which is the only form they can do anything with. Everything else in this file exists to keep reactions out of the verdict and to make sure the thing you noticed first is the thing worth saying first.

## What this skill is for

Use it to:

- give notes on a draft the maker can act on alone
- decide what to say first, and what to refuse to discuss yet
- keep your own taste from deciding the verdict
- call the end of exploration and commit a project to a direction
- review a body of work and reach a retainable judgement about a person
- check your own critique before you deliver it
- review an agent's or a crew's output against what was asked

## Confidence

Everything here is practitioner account rather than measured finding: experienced art directors describing and demonstrating how they give notes, with nothing studied or tested behind any of it. The weakest part is the part you will use most, because judging a single draft rests on much less independent agreement than reviewing a body of work does. No source follows a critique through to the next version, so whether any of this produces better work is untested.

Rules marked **(thin)** rest on a single source and should be read as one person's house style rather than as a requirement. Where a figure appears without a derivation, it was inherited and is marked the same way.

## Terms, defined before they are used

Terms that several skills share have one meaning each, in `reference/glossary.md` at the plugin root. Where this section differs from that file, that file wins.

**Art direction, against design.** Art direction decides what a piece has to be and judges whether it got there. Design makes it: the grid, the type sizes, the colour values, the drawing.

**The feedback request.** What the maker wants from you on this occasion, stated by them: the kind of feedback, at what altitude. Distinct from the brief. A maker can have a clear brief and still only want help with the crop.

**The brief.** What the piece was commissioned to do: the requirement list the work is checked against. Supplied by whoever commissioned it, never invented by you during the critique.

**The direction, against a style.** A direction is a set of rules a maker can be held to, each with a threshold or a prohibition. A style is a recognisable look. A direction can be tested; a style can only be recognised. You judge against a direction.

**Phase: exploration against refinement.** In **exploration** the direction is unsettled and structural options are open, so notes may change the structure. In **refinement** the direction is settled and only execution is in play: crop, scale, colour values, point size, one element removed. A structural note delivered during refinement is wrong-altitude feedback however correct it is, because acting on it throws away a decision the project already made.

**Altitude.** How structural a note is. High altitude touches concept and arrangement. Low altitude touches crop, scale, point size and single-element removal.

**A note, against a preference.** A **note** is an adverse observation with a named cause and an action available to the maker. A **preference** is a reaction you cannot attach a cause to. Both are reportable; only a note may move the verdict.

**The note ladder.** Five forms a single adverse observation can take, from a question that locates a problem up to an instruction with a deliverable. The rung is set by phase. Defined in full below.

**Concept, against execution.** A **concept** is not merely having an idea, which is only intent. It is A plus B making C: a riddle the viewer unpacks with a payoff at the end, so it needs surprise. **(thin)** **Execution** is the craft that renders it. Say which verdict you are giving, because a piece can be conceptually flat with good graphic execution, or conceptually sharp and badly made.

**Reference board, against a moodboard.** A **reference board** is a reduced set of images, each annotated with the one attribute it is there for, so a maker can be held to it. A **moodboard** is images someone liked with no attribute stated, which cannot be judged against. Asked to judge fidelity to a moodboard, say that first.

**Treatment.** The consistent processing applied across an image set: grade, grain, retouching level, crop convention.

**Grade.** The colour and tonal adjustment applied to a photograph after capture. The same shot at two grades reads as two pieces.

**Crop.** Which part of the frame is kept, and at what aspect ratio.

**Specification.** The written, checkable statement of what a piece must contain before it is accepted. A brief becomes a specification when every line of it has a test.

**Body of work.** Pieces presented together as evidence about a person, commonly a portfolio. The set scores at its floor rather than its average.

**The lint pass.** A fixed checklist run on your own critique before sending it, the way a linter is run on code. Listed below.

**The read-back.** Describing the work cold, in plain descriptive words, before consulting the brief. The gap between what it reads as and what was asked for is a finding on its own.

**The verdict.** The one outcome statement closing a critique. Three exist: next version with named fixes, approved as is, or not critiquable yet and why.

**Trapped space.** An enclosed light shape created by accident between elements, which the eye reads as a shape rather than as background.

**Orphan.** A mark that neither aligns with anything nor repeats anything, so it reads as arbitrary.

**Unit of measure.** One element picked as the reference length, named, and used to check every comparable element. Picking a single letter and basing everything off it is the usual move. **(thin)**

**Delivered size.** Canvas pixels divided by the downscale factor, which is what a reader actually sees. Canvases are 1080 x 1080 and 1080 x 1350, the factor is 3x nominal with 4x as a stress case, and the type floor has two cases: 36px at 3x nominal, 48px at the 4x stress case. Those figures come from the design plugin's `reference/canvas-assumptions.md`, and a floor quoted without its case is incomplete.

## Establishing the feedback request before you judge

Before any opinion, establish three things: **what was asked for** (the brief), **what phase the work is in** (exploration or refinement), and **what feedback the maker wants** (the feedback request). If you cannot establish them, that absence is your first finding, delivered before any judgement rather than after it.

Three mechanisms do this, in increasing order of reliability.

1. **Ask questions first.** Open with genuine questions about the work rather than an assessment of it. That starts a dialogue, makes it easier for the maker to explain what they did, and easier for you to understand what you are looking at.
2. **Charge an entry fee.** Nobody in the room may offer an opinion until two contextual questions have been asked. **(thin)** A presentation is not enough time to absorb what you need to know, so opinions formed inside it rest on missing information.
3. **Make the maker declare the feedback request in writing.** **(thin)** Before the critique starts, the maker states what feedback they want and where the work sits on an axis from structural exploration to implementation. This is the strongest of the three because it makes phase a precondition of the feedback rather than something you discover by getting the altitude wrong.

**Judging before asking is the drift you fall into whenever the maker is absent**, which is the normal case for written submissions and generated output: nobody is there to ask, so the questions go unasked and assessment starts immediately. Where there is no maker to question, reconstruct the feedback request from the commissioning document and say in your first line which parts of it you could not establish.

## The note ladder, chosen by phase

Five rungs. The rung is chosen by project phase, not by politeness, and the first thing to settle is which rung the maker wants.

1. **A question that locates the problem without naming a fix.** "What do you think would create more breathing room here."
2. **A named problem with no fix offered.** "There is a flat spot there." "There is a light triangle trapped in there, do you see it."
3. **An owned preference carrying a move.** The form is "what I do not love is X" followed by "I would Y". It is neither a question nor an order: it names a move, attributes it to you, and leaves the maker free to decline.
4. **A drawn or built alternative, placed beside the work.** Beside it, never into it. The maker needs a before state to compare against and to reject.
5. **An instruction with a deliverable and a deadline.** "Here is what I want to see next, by Friday."

**Early in a project, work at rungs one and two. Late, at four and five.** Rung three is available at any phase and is the only rung carrying a preference without converting it into a requirement. Better still, have the maker name the rung they want before you start.

Rung three has a trap: it is easy to deliver eighteen of them in a sitting, none with a cause attached, and call that a critique. An owned preference does not become a note by being phrased politely.

## The principles

1. **Name what works, specifically, and give it a reason.** Name the feature and the effect it produces, in the same mechanical register you use for criticism: what resonates and why, what could be better and why. Generic warmth is filler, and specific criticism closed with generic praise is the form inverted. *Checked by positive specificity.*

2. **A note that cannot be acted on is not a note.** "I just do not like it" gives the maker nothing to learn from, nothing to act on and no room to argue back, so bare opinion is not grounds to kill an idea. The symmetric failure is softness: a note so indirect the maker cannot tell what to change is confusing even when it is kind. *Checked by actionability.*

3. **Diagnose the cause, not the symptom.** This separates useful notes from merely accurate ones. Type reading wrong because it was scaled horizontally, which turns circular curves elliptical. Two colours fighting because they sit at similar saturation. Lettering with uneven weights because the brush angle was inconsistent. A composition reading as three separate things because each element fills its own space. In each case the symptom was visible to anyone and the cause is what the maker can change. *Checked by the mechanism check.*

4. **Mark taste as yours, and do not let it move the verdict.** Two obligations, not one. Dislike a lettering style, say so, note that the brief asked for something else, and praise the piece anyway. The conversion move matters most: start at pure reaction, hunt for a mechanism, and if you find one the reaction becomes a note. If not, it stays yours and stays out of the verdict. *Checked by taste labelling.*

5. **Rank, and deliver one thing first.** One ranked takeaway, and resist stacking faults. **(thin)** An unranked list of a dozen notes makes the maker re-select the important ones, which was your job, and a closing summary that re-selects them proves the list was never ranked. *Checked by the rank check.*

6. **Do not take the mouse.** When you feel like stepping in and moving the mouse yourself, step back. The purpose of art direction is feedback that makes people better, not a piece you fixed. The receiving end is a room where critics point at a screen and direct the maker's hands, so the maker must absorb feedback and operate the tool at once. With generated output, taking the mouse means editing the artefact instead of fixing the brief. *Checked by the mouse check.*

7. **Measure anything measurable rather than asserting it.** With the source file: establish and *name* a unit of measure, build a rectangle from it, walk it across every comparable element, set corner radii numerically rather than by eye, and toggle optical overshoot to ask whether a curve falls short or reads too tall. **(thin)** With only the render, the measurables are counts and sizes: typefaces, weights, delivered type size, element count. *Checked by the unit sweep and the delivered-size read.*

8. **Attach the principle to the move.** State the rule the fix rests on, so the fix outlives the piece. A note that transfers is worth several that do not.

9. **Keep the rejection available.** If every verdict you have issued was "next version with fixes", the top rung has quietly been removed and your passes mean nothing. *Checked by the refusal check.*

## How to work

### Mode A: critiquing a single draft

1. **Establish the feedback request, the brief and the phase.** If any is unavailable, state that before judging anything.
2. **Look in silence first.** The first thing you say should not be the first thing you felt.
3. **Read it back descriptively** in plain words, before anything evaluative. If you cannot name what you are looking at, that is the first finding.
4. **Name what works, with reasons.**
5. **Decide the order of findings by what is checkable.** Do not default to concept before execution.
6. **State the one primary fault**, in first person, and stop rather than stacking.
7. **Separate the concept verdict from the execution verdict and say which is which.**
8. **Diagnose the cause of each adverse note.**
9. **Measure what is measurable.** Unit sweep and overshoot with the source file; counts and delivered sizes with only the render.
10. **Change viewing distance.** Zoom out, squint, and print it if the piece will ever be printed.
11. **Fetch the reference in the open** rather than from memory, and justify the pick when challenged.
12. **If words run out, draw the alternative beside the work, not into it.**
13. **Close with one ranked takeaway, a changed deliverable, a deadline, and permission to refuse.** **(thin)** Without the permission a maker feels obliged to agree, says yes, and comes back panicked. Then ask whether that makes sense.
14. **Run the lint pass on your own critique** before delivering it.

### Mode B: reviewing a body of work

1. **Run the first-screen pass.** Thirty seconds on the landing screen only. Can you tell who this person is and what they do.
2. **Score the set at its floor.** Every weak project dilutes a strong one, so name the weakest piece explicitly in every review.
3. **Check role clarity on every project.** Borrowed credit raises a question about judgement rather than about ability, which is worse.
4. **Check that each project carries context or an argument.** A grid of finished images with no context is a gallery.
5. **Check the order.** Strongest first, nothing visibly dated at the top.
6. **Run the mechanics.** **(thin)** Load time, live text rather than flattened images, placeholder text, reachability of the next project without returning to the top, contactability.
7. **Reach a retainable judgement.** The output is a bet about a person that gets kept and returned to, so it has to be a judgement rather than a list of fixes.

**Piece count is a convention, not a finding.** Five to eight is the band people quote, with no mechanism, test or derivation behind it. **(thin)** Reviewer attention is well under two minutes, also quoted rather than measured. **(thin)** Never fail anyone against either figure.

## Choosing the first finding

**The first note is the highest-order thing that is actually checkable from what you were given.** Not the highest-order thing in principle. If the brief is missing, concept fidelity is not checkable, so it cannot be first, and saying the brief is missing is what goes first instead.

| Situation | First note | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Single piece, brief known | Brief compliance, then concept, execution withheld | A missing requirement outranks every craft remark, and craft notes on a piece that does not meet the brief will be thrown away with the piece |
| Single piece, brief unavailable | The absence of the brief, then measurable craft | Nothing above craft is checkable, so claiming otherwise is asserting rather than judging |
| Body of work, under time pressure | The first screen: can I tell who you are | The reviewer's real attention is spent there, so a finding about anything else describes a review nobody conducts |
| Committed project, maker present | The governing constraint, then the one takeaway | The work has a next version, so the note has to be something the next version can obey |

**Four legitimate refusals to proceed.** Each is a complete critique on its own.

- **Brief violation**, stated before any craft remark.
- **Cannot read it, so that is the whole note.** "I am trying my hardest, and if I have to try this hard it is not clear." Stop there; nothing underneath an illegible piece is worth discussing.
- **Wrong category, so no critique is available.** "There is little to critique here because this is closer to an illustration than a logo." Judging against the wrong category produces notes that are all technically correct and all useless.
- **No frame, so ask rather than judge.** "Give me context so I know how to evaluate this, otherwise I will evaluate it the wrong way."

**The opposite case, work that is nearly there.** Attack it anyway, at low altitude: you are going to try to break it and see if you can. The notes that follow are crop, scale, chroma, point size and one element removed. Never structural, because the structure was settled two phases ago.

## Calling the end of exploration

Closing exploration is not picking a winner. **You close it by naming the constraint that now outranks exploring and by changing what you want to be shown.** The commitment is to a constraint and a deliverable, not to a design, which is why no option has to beat the others for the search to end.

Eight moves, in order. **(thin)**

1. Credit the exploration as you end it, and read its breadth as a good sign about the maker rather than about the work.
2. Name the one constraint that now governs, with its reason. A hard production limit is the clearest kind.
3. Bound where expression survives: one or two places rather than the bulk.
4. **Change the required artefact.** Ask for spreads rather than single pages, a full set rather than a sample. This is the move that actually ends the search, because the old artefact can no longer be produced by exploring.
5. Point at an already-agreed reference set rather than describing a feeling.
6. Make the call out loud.
7. State the new unit of work.
8. Set a deadline and grant explicit permission to refuse it.

**The inverse mechanism is better where you can get it.** Have the *maker* declare the phase in writing before the critique starts: no structural changes because that was already discussed, the design is being nailed down, what is wanted now is tightening. That makes phase a precondition of the feedback rather than a verdict at the end of it. Keep any go or no-go commitment gate separate from the critique itself, because deciding whether to proceed while giving notes contaminates both.

**There is a real disagreement here and you should state which regime you are in.** One position holds that critique is always a brainstorm and a space should exist where anyone can offer new ideas at any point. That is exactly what a declared phase and a refusal to hear structural notes forbid. Both regimes work. Mixing them silently does not, because the maker cannot tell whether a structural note is an invitation or a failure of discipline.

## The lint pass on your own critique

Run it on the written critique before delivering it, including on your own.

- Did the maker state what they want and what phase they are in, or did you establish it?
- Did you ask contextual questions before offering an opinion?
- Could the maker do something tomorrow with every sentence?
- Is at least one thing named as working, with a reason?
- Is every preference marked as yours, and did any of them move the verdict?
- Is there one named first thing?
- Did you name a cause for each adverse note, or only a symptom?
- Did you edit the work, or critique it?
- Is the concept verdict separate from the execution verdict?
- Could this review have concluded "not ready", and would you have said so?
- Are there more than five people in the room? Four to five is the working ceiling. **(thin)**

## Tests

Each returns a verdict decidable from this file alone.

### On your own critique

| Test | Catches | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Request check** | A verdict on work whose brief you never read | Point to the sentence naming the brief, the phase and the feedback request, or naming their absence. Fail if none exists, or if it follows the first judgement. |
| **Actionability** | Notes the maker cannot act on | For each adverse sentence, write the action it implies as one imperative verb plus an object. Fail any sentence for which you cannot. |
| **Positive specificity** | Generic praise paired with specific criticism | Each positive statement must name a feature and the effect it produces. Fail any naming only a judgement: "nice work", "this is strong", "I like this". |
| **Mechanism check** | Symptom reported as diagnosis | For each adverse note, name the decision that produced the effect. Fail where the answer restates the effect. "It feels dated" fails; "the tracking is loose, which is why it reads as a nineties lockup" passes. |
| **Taste labelling** | Preference deciding the outcome | Every adverse item with no named cause must be labelled as yours. Then delete all of them and reread the verdict. Fail if the verdict changes. |
| **Rank check** | A stack posing as a critique | Number the findings. Fail if two share a number, if the closing summary re-selects which mattered, or if there are more than five. |
| **Mouse check** | Editing instead of critiquing | Fail if you modified the maker's file at all, or modified a copy with no preserved before state to compare against. |
| **Refusal check** | A review scale with no bottom | Name which of the three verdicts you issued and what would have produced the next one down. Fail if you cannot state the conditions for "not ready". |
| **Rung match** | Wrong-altitude feedback | Name each note's rung and the declared phase. Fail any rung four or five note during exploration, and any structural note during refinement. |

### On the rendered image

| Test | Catches | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Read-back** | A piece that does not read as what was asked for | Describe the work in plain words before opening the brief, then compare. Fail if your description and the brief's first requirement are not the same thing. |
| **Squint** | No first thing, or two first things | Blur until detail is gone and name what reads first. Fail if you name two elements, or none. |
| **Scale triad** | Dead size contrast | Point at a large, a medium and a small element. Fail if any two are close enough that you hesitate over which is which. **(thin)** |
| **Trapped space** | Accidental shapes between elements | Find every enclosed light area bounded by elements rather than by the frame edge. Fail any that reads as a shape in its own right when squinted at. |
| **Orphan** | Arbitrary marks | For each mark, name what it aligns with or what it repeats. Fail any mark for which you can name neither. **(thin)** |
| **Middle test** | A piece stranded between two positions | Name which extreme the piece sits at on its governing axis. Fail if the honest answer is that it is between two. **(thin)** |
| **Count check** | Unbudgeted typefaces and weights | Count typefaces and weights against the number the direction permits. Fail on any excess, and report the count. |
| **Delivered-size read** | Type that works on the artboard and not in the feed | Divide every type size by the downscale factor. Fail any text below its case floor, 36px at 3x nominal or 48px at the 4x stress case, and report which case you applied. |
| **Saturation fight** | Two colours competing | For any two colours that seem to vibrate, compare their saturation. Fail if they sit at similar saturation, and report that as the cause. |
| **Tangency** | Two things reading as one plane | Find every place a stroke matches an adjacent rule in weight and position. Fail on any, because the eye merges them. |
| **Body-copy test** | Type skill faked by display letters | Read the rags and spacing of two paragraphs of running text. Fail if there is no running text to examine, since anyone can crop a beautiful display letter. |
| **Photo-set consistency** | A set that is not a set | Compare white balance, background, time of day and grain across the images. Fail on any axis where one image does not match the rest. |
| **Mockup realism** | A presentation hiding the piece | Compare shadow opacity and distance across the mockup. Fail if uniform, which means the shadows were applied rather than cast. |

### On the concept

| Test | Catches | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **A plus B equals C** | Intent mistaken for a concept | Name element A, name element B, and name the C a viewer arrives at. Fail if you can only name a subject. **(thin)** |
| **Found against forced** | A hidden figure pasted on | Check whether the second reading uses shadow or negative space already present in the scene. Fail if it was added on top. |
| **Over-tell** | Narration where a hint was wanted | Count how many elements state the same idea. Fail at more than one, because the payoff depends on the viewer completing it. |
| **Element meaning** | Decoration posing as content | For each element, say in one sentence what it is doing. Fail any element with no answer, then delete it and look again. |
| **Brief compliance** | Requirements quietly dropped | Write the brief as a numbered list of requirements and mark each met or unmet. Fail on any unmet item, and report the list rather than a summary. |

### On a body of work

| Test | Catches | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Thirty-second pass** | A landing screen that says nothing | Spend thirty seconds on the first screen only, then say who this person is and what they do. Fail if you cannot say both. |
| **Weakest piece** | An average hiding a floor | Name the weakest piece out loud. Fail the set if that piece would not survive being the only one shown. |
| **Role clarity** | Borrowed credit | For each project, state the person's own contribution. Fail any project where the work shown exceeds the role claimed, or where no role is stated. |
| **Odd one out** | A set that is two sets | Name the piece that does not belong. Fail if removing it makes the set read more clearly, because then it is diluting. |
| **Bait and switch** | Thumbnails that oversell | Compare each thumbnail against the project behind it. Fail on any where the thumbnail is the best thing in the project. |
| **Mechanics** | Avoidable disqualifiers | Check load time, whether text is live rather than an image, placeholder text, broken links and a reachable contact route. Fail on any, and report them as a list rather than as a judgement. **(thin)** |

## Failure modes

**Of the critique as a meeting.** Over-structuring it until nobody attends. No shared editing surface, so critics direct the maker's hands. Sharing late, after every pixel is polished and the maker is no longer open to anything. Too many people. The word *critique* itself, whose synonyms run toward censure, which is reason enough to rename the meeting. **(thin)** An undefined quality bar, set by whoever was present earliest and defended on feel. Loud voices crowding out quiet ones, which needs a non-verbal channel rather than an instruction to speak up. Prep cost high enough that people stop bringing work.

**Of the notes.** Wrong-altitude feedback, meaning detailed visual notes on early-stage work. Scope creep between kinds of review. Critique mistaken for approval, where a maker assumes that being seen in a review means cleared to ship, which is why the commitment gate stays separate. Opinion dumped without context. Unranked serial notes. Generic praise with specific criticism. Symptom without mechanism. Taking the mouse. Taste moving the verdict, especially when backed by exposure as a credential rather than by a mechanism. Stacking faults. A verdict on work whose brief you do not have, delivered without saying so. Grading when you were asked for notes: grades belong to a body-of-work review, and a draft critique produces a next version.

**Of the critic.** Guard hardest against these, because they are committed by people who are good at this.

- **Editing the file within minutes instead of critiquing it**, with no before state preserved, which is the exact behaviour art direction is premised on avoiding.
- **Delivering verdicts while lacking the brief**, and only saying so an hour in. Saying it first rather than last is the whole difference.
- **Judging before opening.** Announcing a verdict before looking, conceding afterwards that the work was better once looked at, then repeating the pattern.
- **Assuming intent** immediately after disclaiming assumption.
- **Letting an unbriefed audience set the verdict.** A colour changed because a room reacted; a piece abandoned mid-repair on sentiment, left worse than found.
- **Critiquing inside a culture you do not know**, conceding insensitivity only after someone objects.
- **Abandoning a piece on cost rather than reaching a verdict.** "This would take too long" is not an outcome.
- **Zero rejections.** A format that closes every case positively, where the harshest outcome available is a list of fixes. The notes may be perfectly sharp; it is the *conclusion* that is softened, and the mechanism is usually supply. If you cannot fail anything, you are not reviewing.

Two transfer straight to reviewing generated output and both are cheap to avoid: judging before looking properly, and rendering a verdict while missing the brief.

## Applied

### Worked example: one carousel slide, brief available

**Before.** A 1080 x 1350 slide. Headline at 56px. Subhead at 48px. Deck label at 44px. Body at 40px. Footnote at 30px. Six sizes across the deck. The brief's first requirement is that the product name appears on every slide; it is absent from this one. The grade on the supporting photograph is warmer than on the two slides either side of it.

**Diagnosis, computed.**

- *Brief compliance.* Requirement one, product name on every slide: **unmet.** That outranks everything below it, so it is finding one and craft notes are withheld until it is fixed.
- *Delivered-size read.* Footnote at 30px is **6px under the 36px nominal floor** and 18px under the 48px stress-case floor, so it fails both. Delivered, 30 / 3 = 10pt at nominal and 30 / 4 = 7.5pt under stress, against a readable floor of about 12pt.
- *Count and scale.* 48px and 44px deliver at 16.0pt and 14.7pt at nominal, **1.3pt apart**, so that is one level wearing two names and the ladder has five real steps rather than six. The scale triad still passes on 56, 40 and 30, but the middle of the ladder is mush.
- *Photo-set consistency.* Warmer than its neighbours. Cause: the grade, not the photograph, so the maker can match it without reshooting.
- *Preference, labelled.* I would set the headline tighter. I can find no mechanism beyond liking it tighter, so it is mine and stays out of the verdict.

**After.** Finding one: the product name is missing, and nothing else is worth discussing until it is back. Then, ranked: the footnote to 36px minimum, 48px if anyone must read it under the stress case; the 48 and 44 steps collapsed to one value; the photograph regraded to match its neighbours. Deliverable: the full deck as spreads rather than single slides, because consistency is what is under review and single slides cannot show it. Deadline Thursday, and say no if Thursday is wrong. Preference excluded: tighter headline tracking, mine.

### Worked example: a body of work

**Before.** Eleven projects on one scrolling page. The first screen is a full-bleed image with no name and no statement. Three projects are coursework. One thumbnail is its project's single best frame. Team projects state no role. Text is flattened into images.

**Diagnosis.** Thirty-second pass **fails**: after thirty seconds I can say what this person makes but not who they are, because the first screen carries neither name nor statement. Weakest piece: the thinnest coursework project, which would not survive being shown alone, so the set scores there. Odd one out: the same project, and removing it makes the set read more clearly, which is dilution by definition. Role clarity **fails** on three team projects, where the work shown exceeds any stated role. Bait and switch **fails** on one thumbnail. Mechanics **fails** the live-text check. Piece count is eleven against a quoted convention of five to eight, which has no derivation behind it, so it is not reported as a finding.

**After.** Verdict: not ready. One ranked takeaway: the first screen does not say who you are, and that screen is where the whole review is spent. Then cut to the strongest work, which removes the weakest piece and the odd one out in one move. State your own role on every team project. Replace the oversold thumbnail. Set live text. Deliverable: the first screen alone, resubmitted before the rest.

### Worked example: generated output, no maker present

**Before.** A prompt asked for an editorial portrait, cool grade, single light source. Four images came back, all reading as warm studio product photography. There is nobody to question.

**Diagnosis.** Request reconstruction, stated in the output: the brief exists, there is no maker to state a feedback request, so I supply the phase myself and say so. Read-back, written before rereading the prompt: four warm studio product shots. The prompt's first requirement was a cool grade, so the description and the requirement are **not the same thing**, the read-back fails, and that gap is the entire finding. Cause: not the render but the reference set behind it, which averaged toward studio lighting. "This feels generic" would report the symptom and send the prompt nowhere.

**After.** One finding with its cause: the grade requirement did not survive, and the cause is an averaged reference set rather than a bad render. One ranked action: name the grade as a value, pin one reference image per attribute, regenerate. Not twelve notes stacked onto one regeneration, which produces a different wrong thing. Verdict: not yet critiquable on craft, because nothing above grade fidelity is checkable until the grade is right. The artefact was not retouched by hand: the brief is the asset and the output is disposable.

## The ultimate test

**Can the maker produce the next version from your notes without you in the room.** Hand the written critique to someone who has not seen the work and ask what they would change. If they cannot tell, the maker cannot either.

## What an agent can run, and what it cannot

**Can run fully.** The lint pass, in full, on any written critique including its own. This is the most valuable automatable thing here, because it catches the failures experienced critics commit. The read-back, by describing output cold before being shown the brief. Brief compliance as an enumerated list. Most rendered-image tests: trapped space, orphans, the scale triad, typeface and weight counts, saturation conflict, tangency, photo-set consistency, the delivered-size division. Body-of-work mechanics: load time, live text, placeholder text, contactability, order.

**Cannot run.** Anything needing a source file it does not have. The print and viewing-distance tests. Whether a cultural reference is being handled competently. Whether the brief was the right brief.

**Delegate deliberately.** The read-back, which is void if the reader already knows the intent, and the lint pass, because the author of a critique is the worst judge of whether their own preference moved the verdict.

## Writing the output

Plain words and full sentences, in this order.

1. What the work reads as, cold, before the brief.
2. What was asked for, or that you were not told, and what phase you are treating it as.
3. What works, named with reasons.
4. The one primary finding, with its cause.
5. Supporting findings, ranked and numbered, each naming the test that caught it.
6. Preferences, labelled as yours and explicitly excluded from the verdict.
7. One verdict: next version with named fixes, approved as is, or not critiquable yet and why.
8. The deliverable and the deadline, with permission to refuse.

Quote the case alongside any floor figure, because a floor without its case does not say what it survives. Where a finding rests on a rule marked **(thin)**, say so in the same sentence.

**The order above is constructed rather than validated. (thin)** Treat it as a first shape to improve by use, particularly for the case this skill is likeliest to serve: notes sent to a crew or an agent, written and asynchronous, with no maker in the room.

## Where this skill should lose

**When the direction does not yet exist as rules.** Use `ad-rules`, which turns a visual direction into clauses with thresholds and prohibitions a maker can be held to. That is the artefact this file judges against, so if you find yourself inventing the standard mid-critique, stop: you are writing the direction rather than judging compliance with it, and the work is then graded against a rule it was never given.

**When the reference set is the problem.** Use `ad-board`, which builds a reduced set of images each annotated with the attribute it is there for. If your finding keeps coming out as "this averaged toward something generic", the cause is upstream in the set and no amount of notes on the render will reach it.

**When the piece does not exist yet.** Use `ad-brief`, which specifies an image before it is made and sets the acceptance criteria it will be checked against. Asked to judge a piece nobody has made, you are being asked to write its specification instead.

**When legibility is the question and a number would settle it.** Use `dsn-contrast`, the design skill that measures contrast against the accessibility specification: it computes a ratio from two colour values, reads the threshold applying at the delivered size, and returns a pass or fail with a margin. That is the one craft failure here that is objectively decidable, so route it there rather than reporting it as a judgement. The squint test and delivered-size read tell you something may be unreadable; they do not return a verdict anyone can audit.

**When the finding is that nothing reads first.** Use `dsn-hierarchy`, which ranks what should read first, second and third and computes the separation each level needs. The squint test diagnoses the symptom; building the ladder of sizes, weights and positions is not this file's job.

**When type is badly set rather than badly chosen.** Use `dsn-typeset` for size, leading, tracking and measure, and `dsn-typeface` when the question is which family to use at all. "The type reads wrong" is a symptom; the cause is usually a measure too wide, leading too tight, or a scale whose steps collapse after downscaling.

**When the subject is a logo and the question is construction.** Use `dsn-mark`, which owns how a mark is drawn and whether it is ownable. This file can tell you a mark fails at small size. It does not redraw it, and a critique that drifts into redrawing has taken the mouse.

**When the piece is a sequence rather than a frame.** Use `dsn-sequence`, which owns the shape of a carousel or spread set, its turns and the budget per frame. Judging slide one well says nothing about whether slides two to eight merely continue.

**When the question is which photograph, or how to treat one.** Use `dsn-image`. This file can tell you a set does not match on grade and name the grade as the cause. It does not choose the picture or specify the treatment.

**The dependency runs one way.** This file points at design skills; design never points back here. Design makes the page and owns the craft underneath; art direction specifies the piece and judges whether it arrived. Where a measured design verdict and a critique note disagree, the measured verdict wins on its own ground, because a frame nobody can physically read is not a matter of taste.
