# The POVs interview

One session, about 20 to 30 minutes. It ends with one to three POVs handed back for a yes. Read `../SKILL.md` ("The method" and "The shape of every session") first; this file holds only what's particular to POVs.

## What a POV is

- **Is:** a claim about how something in their field works or should work, in three parts:
  - **the claim:** one sentence, specific enough that a reasonable peer could reject it;
  - **the ground:** what they saw that made them believe it;
  - **the condition:** when it's true, and so when it isn't.
- **Isn't:** a value (a value is about what matters; a POV is about how things work), a hot take (a claim with no ground), or a fact (a claim no reasonable peer could reject).
- **The evidence it leaves:** the ground is what makes a POV theirs. POVs show up in moments they watched something work or fail for a reason others didn't expect.

So the questions point at those moments. Never "what do you believe that others don't?" or "what does your field get wrong?": both ask for the abstraction, and people answer them with the takes they've already read. The same goes for a story question with that ending bolted on ("...and what did you see that they didn't?"): it still asks them to rank themselves above other people, and the answer comes back as a complaint or a hot take. Ask what happened and what they think caused it; whether others saw it too is for the follow-up on what the POV rejects.

## Before you start

Read `canon/person/povs.md`, the drafts the map left and its findings (`bank/map/findings.md`), the stories on file, and any `/think about` notes. A POV candidate from `/think` already has its three parts and only needs the yes.

If the person chose to start fresh (they said their older material no longer speaks for them, recorded in `progress.md`), the drafts stay out of the questions unless they point at one. Ask from the table below, about their work as a whole. Don't narrow the question to a topic the studio picked for them, such as what their recent pieces or the studio itself are about: "their current focus" is theirs to name. If they said their focus changed and haven't said what it is now, the first question asks that, in one line, before any story question.

## The questions

| Ask | Why it works |
| --- | --- |
| "Tell me about a project that went well for a reason you didn't expect." | The unexpected reason is the ground of a POV others don't hold |
| "What's the last thing you had to un-teach a client? What did they believe, and what happened?" | Un-teaching names the common view (what it rejects) and the evidence in one story |
| "Tell me about a piece of advice you stopped following. What happened when you stopped?" | A changed mind comes with its ground attached |
| "Tell me about a time a client wanted to do it the usual way and you pushed back. How did it turn out?" | The usual way is what the POV rejects; the outcome is the ground |
| "Tell me about something you worked on that didn't work. What do you think happened?" | Their own account of a cause is the ground; who else missed it comes out in the follow-up on what it rejects, not in the question |

Then follow up for the three parts:

- **The ground:** "What did you see that made you believe that?" A case, a pattern across clients, a number, a moment.
- **The condition:** "When isn't that true?" or "Who would that advice hurt?" Every POV stops somewhere. If they can't name where, the POV stays a draft with confidence `forming`.
- **What it rejects:** "Who says the opposite, and what do they say exactly?"
- **Confidence:** "Would you say that in public today, or are you still working it out?"

## Pushback

| The answer | The pushback |
| --- | --- |
| A hot take ("cold outreach is dead") | "What did you see that made you believe that?" |
| Too broad ("content is about trust") | "When isn't it true?" |
| A fact everyone agrees with | "Who in your field would argue with that?" If nobody would, it's not a POV yet. |
| A value dressed as a POV ("I believe in honesty") | "That sounds like something you care about. What does it mean for how the work should be done?" |
| Polished | "What's the version you'd say to a friend?" |

## Finding the POV

The person usually tells a story and says the POV halfway through, half-formed. Find the sentence where they say how things work, then build the three parts from the story: the claim from what they said, the ground from what they saw, the condition from where it stopped (or ask). Keep the raw words in `quote`; offer the sharper claim beside them (`CLAUDE.md` rule 3). The test for your version is whether it's more exact, not more impressive.

## The handback

> 3. **Clients don't need more content**
> Clients don't need more content; they need fewer pieces they can stand behind.
> What you saw: the two clients who cut to one post a week got more inbound than when they posted daily.
> When it's true: for people selling expensive work through trust. Probably not for someone selling a cheap product to a big audience.
> From the call with Dana: "Honestly the ones who post less but actually believe what they post do way better."

Then: tell me if I misunderstood something, or if the "when it's true" part needs changing.

## The gate

A POV is confirmed when all three parts are present, a reasonable peer could reject the claim (the disagreement test in `../references/tests.md`), every field under POVs in `../references/fields.md` is filled, and the person says it's theirs. A POV with no condition stays a draft with confidence `forming`.

## What it makes possible

Opinion pieces with a real stance. Three confirmed POVs make the philosophy session possible, and the conditions are where the manifesto's honest concessions come from later.
