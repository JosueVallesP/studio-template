# The values interview

One session, about 20 to 30 minutes. It ends with one to three values handed back for a yes. Read `../SKILL.md` ("The method" and "The shape of every session") first; this file holds only what's particular to values.

## What a value is

- **Is:** what wins when two good things conflict. A ranking, not a list of virtues. A person has very few, one to three that a reader can see, and they rarely change.
- **Isn't:** a virtue everyone claims (honesty, integrity, quality), a preference ("I like working with founders"), or a goal ("I want more freedom").
- **The evidence it leaves:** a choice that cost them something real and that they'd make again. Anger is evidence too, because people get angry when something they value is broken in front of them.

So the questions point at costly choices and at anger, never at the value itself.

## Before you start

Read `canon/person/values.md`, `canon/bank/stories.md`, any drafts for values the map left, the root questions on the map (`bank/map/findings.md`, "Questions for the interviews"), and anything the person has written about turning points in their work. A story already on file is a better opening than a cold question: "You told me about turning down the agency retainer. Can we start there?"

## The questions

Pick the one that fits. Each asks about something that happened.

| Ask | Why it works |
| --- | --- |
| "Tell me about a time you turned down work or money. What was on the table, and why did you say no?" | Saying no to money is the clearest costly choice there is |
| "Tell me about a decision you paid for. What did it cost you, and would you make it again?" | The cost and the "again" are the two halves of the gate |
| "When was the last time someone's work in your field made you angry? What did they do?" | Anger points at a value being broken; the "what did they do" keeps it an event |
| "Tell me about the most pressure you've been under at work. What did you refuse to give up?" | Pressure forces the ranking into the open |
| "Tell me about a decision the people around you thought was a mistake." | A choice others wouldn't make is a ranking others don't share |

Then, on the story, follow up for what the gate needs:

- **The other good thing:** "What were you giving up by choosing that?" The thing a value beats has to be good too; "quality over laziness" tells a reader nothing.
- **The cost:** "What did it actually cost you?" A real loss: a client, money, a relationship, time with someone.
- **Again:** "Knowing how it went, would you do it the same way?" A choice they regret is a lesson, not a value.
- **The limit:** "How much bigger would the cost have had to be before you chose the other way?" Most values stop somewhere, and the honest answer goes in `limit`. It keeps a piece from making them sound more absolute than they are.

## Pushback

| The answer | The pushback |
| --- | --- |
| A virtue ("I value honesty") | "When did that cost you something?" |
| A preference or a goal | "Tell me about a time you had to choose between that and something else you wanted." |
| A story where nothing was lost | "What would have happened if you'd chosen the other way?" Then look for a different story. |
| Polished ("I've always believed in putting people first") | "What's the version you'd say to a friend?" |

## Finding the value

In each story, name the two good things that conflicted and which one won. The winner is the value; the loser is what it beats; the loss is the cost. When two or three stories have the same winner, that's one value with several stories behind it, and it's much stronger than a value with one.

Write the claim in the plainest sentence that holds the ranking, using their words where they had them: "I'd rather lose the client than tell them a plan will work when I think it won't." A claim with no "rather", "over" or "even when" in it usually hasn't caught the ranking yet.

## The handback

Numbered, one line of title each, then the value in one plain sentence, the two things it ranks, and their lines under it with where they came from. Then the invitation: tell me if I misread something or if you'd put it differently.

> 1. **Telling clients the truth over keeping them**
> You'd rather lose a client than tell them a plan will work when you think it won't.
> From the agency story: "I told them the launch would flop and they fired us two weeks later. I'd do it again."

## The gate

A value is confirmed when:

1. at least one costly choice stands behind it, told as a story (the story gets its own S entry, with permission asked),
2. every field in `../references/fields.md` under Values is filled, and
3. the person recognizes it as theirs.

The layer is done when the decision test in `../references/tests.md` passes.

## What it makes possible, and what else to file

Values and behaviors make the voice and the stop list possible: the studio learns what the person would never say and why. Every story told in this session goes to `canon/bank/stories.md` as it comes up, whatever layer it ends up supporting. A refusal that comes up ("I never take equity") is a behavior candidate; note it for the behaviors session rather than chasing it now.
