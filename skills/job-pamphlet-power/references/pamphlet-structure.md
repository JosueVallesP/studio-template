## 1. Length and what a pamphlet is

Four sections, 40 to 60 pages in all (some run to 75). Don't overstay: each section does its job and stops.

A pamphlet isn't a course, a how-to guide or a set of tips. It teaches one mechanism at the level of the idea, completely enough that the reader can use it, and is honest about what putting it fully into practice takes. The page counts per section below are the original's starting points; the material decides the real length.

## 2. Section 1: Credibility

About 8 to 12 pages. The job: show the reader why this person is worth listening to on this, through what they've lived and done, not through claims.

In this order:

**The discovery story.** How the person found the method they're about to teach, so the reader hears themselves in it. The shape it usually takes: stuck, couldn't work it out, there had to be a better way, found one. The specific moment, client or situation where the mechanism first worked, with the real details (names with permission, dates, amounts, places). What wasn't working with the usual approaches. What in their experience let them see what others missed. The part that was accidental, when there was one; admitting it makes the story believable.

The story comes from `canon/bank/stories.md` or from the person telling it now. The studio never fills in a detail the person didn't give. A missing detail is a question: "Where were you when you first saw it work? Who was the client?"

**Client results, three to five.** It didn't only work for the person; they taught it and it worked for others. Brief stories: the starting situation, what was taught, the result with its numbers and timeline, what the client said. Each comes from `canon/bank/proof.md` or the person's words today, with the client's permission to be named noted. Fewer than three real ones means fewer than three in the pamphlet; never a composite.

**Why the usual approaches fail.** The approaches the reader has tried (`tried` in `audience.md`), each with the specific reason it falls short, so the old ways look incomplete once the new one is in view. A reason is an argument and gets tested with `skills/craft-copylosophy`.

**The person's experience.** The real numbers: clients served, years, results, said plainly and once. Numbers that sound too big make readers distrust them; real ones said simply don't. "I've been where you are" only when it's true and in their stories.

## 3. Section 2: The problem and the method

About 12 to 18 pages. The job: show the reader the problem under the one they came in with, then teach the mechanism that answers it.

**Why the problem goes deeper than it looks.** Three to five reasons the obvious fixes don't work, or make it worse. The shape: the problem the reader names, then "one layer down", then the cause under it. The case: "Businesses run out of cash" was the symptom; "they can't keep their customers" was the cause. Evidence comes from the person's proof, the audience research, or real sourced figures (`borrowed: verify`), never from a statistic nobody can find. What do people in the segment consistently underestimate about it, by the research?

**The mechanism, step by step.** Defined clearly in one or two sentences. Its two to four parts, which together show why it beats the usual method. An analogy from another field that maps how the parts relate (the original's cases: the insurance industry's "float" applied to coaching revenue; a business as a car). A real history lesson where there is one. A diagram where the structure helps (`/visual`). The contrarian sentence from the mechanism brief, filled in.

**Depth that shows what the person knows.** The right terms, real data, the nuance a beginner wouldn't see, references to other fields or studies (each sourced). The reader should come away thinking this person knows something they don't. Depth is not jargon: every term gets explained the first time (house rules H6).

**The approaches it replaces, compared.** "If you've tried A, B or C, here's why they fall short" pays off the setup in section 1. A two-column comparison often works: the old way and its problems, the person's way.

## 4. Section 3: The shift in belief

About 8 to 12 pages. The job: name the beliefs that stop the reader from acting on what they've just learned, and answer them honestly.

**The beliefs that have to change.** What the segment believes that holds them back: the reasoning column in `audience.md`, and what the person kept correcting in the client audit (Q2). The person often knows these because they used to believe them; ask about when they did. Answer each one directly, with its reason.

**Investment and cost.** Why spending money and effort on solving this is an investment, argued from the reader's real alternative (what they spend now, what the problem costs them, from the research and `/pricing-tests`). The original's line on this: a sophisticated reader knows time is worth more than money; a more sophisticated one knows energy is worth more than time. A story of an investment the person made in their own learning and how it paid off, when they have one (`canon/bank/stories.md`); never invented.

**The cost of waiting.** What it costs the reader to leave things as they are, only as the research and the person's clients show it. The usual reasons people put it off (the original found about four), answered in the reader's words. This is the place the care-not-leverage gate (`skills/craft-sales-copy`) matters most: the cost of waiting is said once, plainly, and never inflated.

**Proof, a second time.** Results from people in the person's higher-priced work, with permission, so the reader sees what the guided version led to. How the person thinks about betting on yourself, in their words if they have them. A story of someone who waited and regretted it only if it's real and the person may tell it.

## 5. Section 4: What doing it takes, and the next step

About 8 to 12 pages. The job: show honestly what putting the mechanism fully into practice takes, and offer the guided version plainly.

**What it takes.** Everything doing it properly requires: the tools, skills, knowledge, time and resources. The original names this the person's version of a "codex": the system and its three to five parts, named. It's what doing the work really involves, from the person's experience with clients, shown as it is.

**The common mistakes, five to seven.** The real, non-obvious pitfalls that trip people up, each from a client the person saw hit it. Understanding a concept and doing it well are different things; the mistakes show where the difference lies. A pitfall the person hasn't seen happen doesn't go in.

**Why help is worth it.** Connect what it takes to what the person offers: getting time back, saving energy, getting there sooner. If the person feels an obligation to the reader's success (their values, their philosophy), say it in their words; never borrow a moral pitch.

**The next step, in the open.** Invite the reader to the next step without pressure. Say what the higher-priced work is, what's included, and its price when the person wants it public, from the vehicle. Name the next step exactly: a call, an application, an assessment, with the call to action word for word from a `C` entry. Say plainly that there's more: the original's move is to admit the motive openly ("I have an ulterior motive here: once you've seen what this does, you'll want to know what else I can teach you, and I'll be glad to tell you"). In the studio, the person's own version of that sentence, in their words, is the one that goes in.

## 6. The questions, section by section

One at a time, about what happened (`skills/craft-interview`). Each answer is kept in the person's words with its source, and a half-thought gets a sharper version offered beside it (`CLAUDE.md` rule 3).

**Section 1**
1. "Tell me about the moment you first saw this work. Where were you, who was the client, what had you tried before?"
2. "Pick three to five clients this worked for. For each: where they started, what you taught them, what changed and by when, and what they said. Are they happy to be named?"
3. "What had these clients tried before they came to you? Why didn't it work for them?"
4. "How many clients have you done this with, over how long? What results can you point to?"

**Section 2**
5. "When clients first describe the problem to you, what do they say it is? What turns out to be going on underneath? What evidence do you have?"
6. "Walk me through the method as you'd teach it on a call. What do you compare it to when it's not landing?"
7. "What's the part of this a beginner wouldn't know? Explain it to me as you would to a peer."
8. "What do people usually try instead? Where does each fall short, from what you've seen?"

**Section 3**
9. "What did your clients believe at the start that you had to talk them out of? When did you last believe it yourself?"
10. "What does this problem cost your clients before they fix it, in money, time or energy? Is there an investment you made in your own learning that paid off?"
11. "What happens to a client who waits a year? Have you seen it?"
12. "Who in your higher-priced work has a result you could share, with their permission?"

**Section 4**
13. "List everything a client needs to do this properly: tools, skills, knowledge, time."
14. "What are the mistakes you've seen people make trying this alone? Tell me about one."
15. "How would you say to a reader that you have more to offer, in your own words, with no pressure?"

## 7. How each section is drafted and judged

One section at a time, in order, through `skills/craft-sales-copy` with the copy kind `pamphlet section`:

1. The person's answers to that section's questions are the material, and their sentences come first.
2. Three drafts of the section, different in what leads and how it moves (the story first, the reader's problem first, the comparison first), with a source map.
3. The scripts, then the judge in a fresh context, which picks one or sends all three back. The section's own checks (this file's section for it, and the checklist below) are check 12 in `skills/craft-sales-copy/stages/judge.md`.
4. The person reads the passed section before the next one starts. Their edits are kept as pairs for `bank/rewrites/`.

When all four pass, `pamphlet.md` holds them in order, and `/refine` can take the whole further.

## 8. The checklist

Before the pamphlet goes out, it has:

- [ ] A mechanism name the person would say out loud, for a pattern in their material
- [ ] A discovery story with real details, from their stories or their words
- [ ] A contrarian insight with a premise that holds
- [ ] Depth that shows what they know, every term explained
- [ ] An analogy that maps how the parts relate
- [ ] The investment argued from the reader's real alternative
- [ ] What doing it takes, shown as it really is
- [ ] An open, pressure-free next step, with the call to action word for word
- [ ] Every result, number and testimonial sourced, with permission noted
- [ ] The person's own words for the method throughout, not generic terms
- [ ] A clear design and layout, with the diagrams from `/visual`
- [ ] 40 to 60 pages, or the length the material carries
