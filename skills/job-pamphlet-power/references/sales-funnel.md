Every product, price, result and testimonial on these pages comes from the person and passes the truth tests in `skills/craft-sales-copy`. The cases are from the Revolving Price funnel of Taylor Welch's The Wealthy Consultant, cited so the person can see the move; its numbers are theirs, not a target.

---

## 1. The funnel's shape

A series of pages, each offering the next product up:

```
Where buyers come from (email, posts, communities, ads, partners)
        ↓
    SALES PAGE (sells the pamphlet)
        ↓
    CHECKOUT (with the add-on checkbox)
        ↓
    OFFER 1 (shown once, after purchase)
        ↓ yes            ↓ no
    OFFER 2          (optional fallback 1)
        ↓ yes            ↓ no
    CONFIRMATION     (optional fallback 2)
        ↓
    CONFIRMATION PAGE
```

The whole funnel goes in `pieces/<...>/funnel.md`, with each new product as a `draft` `O` entry in `canon/vehicle/vehicle.md` until the person says yes (a change to a `confirmed` offer goes to `proposals.md` only, as `kind: change to O<n>`). A product that doesn't exist yet is marked so; the page doesn't sell it until it does.

## 2. Prices

The Revolving Price funnel, as the case:

- **The pamphlet:** $3, so the price is no barrier.
- **The checkout add-on:** $5, making $8 at checkout.
- **Offer 1:** $47 to $97, solving a problem the pamphlet creates downstream.
- **Offer 2:** $297 to $497, the core curriculum of the flagship offer, to get results sooner.
- **Fallbacks:** a stripped-down version at a lower price, or a payment plan.

In that model the pamphlet isn't where the money is made: the add-on, the offers and, later, the higher-priced programs are. The pamphlet exists to find buyers at the lowest cost. The person's prices come from them, with `/pricing-tests` for the higher-priced offers; the studio suggests ranges with their reasoning and never states a price as theirs.

## 3. The sales page, in eight parts

The sales page is one piece through `skills/craft-sales-copy` (copy kind `sales page`, `kind: cornerstone`). For a long page, draft it part by part: three versions of each part, the judge, then the next part. These are the parts and the moves inside them; the moves are possibilities, each passing the house rules.

### 1. The header

**Who it's for.** Two ways. By name: "For coaches, consultants and trainers who..." Or by the problem they want out of, which the original prefers, because readers recognise their problem faster than their title: "Read this if you want to stop <the problem, in the reader's words from the language bank>."

**The headline.** Four things a headline can carry: the problem, the benefit, the contradiction (what the person does that the reader wouldn't expect), and the authority. The original's template began "Brand new pamphlet reveals how you can..."; in the studio, say the same four things in plain words, with no "reveals". One finding from the case: using only the product name as the headline ("The Revolving Price Method") lifted later conversion by 20% over the long headline. The person can test both.

**The subheadline.** Carries the benefit when the headline is the name: "Build a consulting business that pays for itself with this unusual pricing model," in the case.

**An early buy button.** At a few dollars, some readers are already sold when they arrive. A button at the top lets them buy.

### 2. The opening: why this, why now

Three beats.

**Get attention with something true and specific.** A real number, a claim the person can back, an unexpected fact about the reader's situation. The case opened on the industry's projected value ("My $329 billion bet") and the rate at which businesses fail, with sourced figures. Introduce the person briefly. Then the reader's problem in their words. The studio uses real figures with sources, said once; it never builds a drum-roll of numbers.

**Say what the page will show them.** "On this page I'll show you why <the deeper problem>, and what to do about it." A plain signpost, followed straight away by the content (house rules H18).

**The person's standing, and the problem underneath.** Their real numbers, said plainly and once, without a list. Then the symptom, and the sign that there's a real problem beneath it.

### 3. The teaching

**The deeper issue.** The reader's problem, then one layer down, then the cause. The case: "Businesses run out of cash" (symptom), "they can't keep their customers" (cause). A question set up early can be answered later in the page, as long as the answer really comes.

**An object lesson.** A real story about a well-known company, person or moment in history that makes the mechanism feel like the logical answer (`borrowed: verify`, sourced). The case used Apple's move from one-time computer sales to subscriptions.

**A detour that isn't one.** What looks like a tangent and shows how much the usual approaches waste, and where people trying half-measures fall down. It earns its place only if the reader learns something from it.

**The reader's own question.** One real question the reader asks themselves, quoted or said close to how the research has it ("Why do some businesses burn through every dollar while others keep growing?"), followed by the answer. Never a string of rhetorical questions, and never as the opening line (house rules H23).

### 4. The reveal: the product and its price

A short recap of the person's standing, then the turn: the answer isn't more of the usual thing, it's the person's model. Then what's included (the pamphlet, any bonus training), and the price.

**Price anchoring, only with a real anchor.** The case compared the $3 price to what the person's guided program costs ("I'm not going to ask you for $10,000"). In the studio, the anchor is a real price of something the person really sells; with no such price, there's no anchor. The buy button follows the price straight away.

### 5. Objections

**"Why is it so cheap?"** The reader expects cheap to mean thin. The answer, in the person's words: why it's priced this way, said honestly. The case's version was that the author doesn't make money from $3 book sales but from readers who want to work with him later. It's a fact about the person, so only their version goes in.

**"Can this help me?"** Two or three kinds of reader the pamphlet is for, so each can see themselves: in the case, people who've had some success and are burning out, and people starting out who want to build it right. In the studio, the kinds come from the segments and the research.

**Doubt, answered with proof.** Real testimonials and results from people like the reader, with permission.

### 6. Bullets, ten to twenty

Each bullet says something concrete the reader will get, and one or more of these:

- an assumption it challenges, said with its reason;
- a specific tactic or idea, with the page it's on ("the pricing table on page 23");
- something only the person would know.

The page numbers are a signature move: they make the book feel real and give readers a reason to buy just to turn to that page. The original also used curiosity gaps (half-revealing so the reader has to buy to finish the thought); the house rules ban those (H23), so the studio's bullets say what's there, plainly, and let the content do the pulling.

### 7. Before the close

**Who it's for, again.** The kinds of reader, said so they nod: in the case, "you want to grow without giving up your sanity", "you care about your clients' results". From the research, in the reader's words.

**Low risk, said plainly.** The price, with no hidden fees or subscriptions when that's true. Then the next offers, in the open: "after seeing what's in a $3 book, you'll probably want to know what else we have." A relationship, not a single sale.

**The guarantee.** Only the one the person offers: the case's was "read it, hate it, and we'll refund you; keep the book."

### 8. The close

**Trust.** The person's own reason for the low price and for being worth working with later, in their words.

**"Still not sure?"** The case offered a real phone number with real people answering it. Only if the person has one, and someone answers.

**The final call to action,** word for word from a `C` entry.

### Where they are, where they want to be, the gap

This runs through the objections: where the reader is now (the research's words), the gap (in the case, "the quitting fantasy", when you daydream about shutting the business), and where they want to be (more time with family, more income, the wish to help people). The pamphlet is what gets them across. In the studio, each of the three comes from `audience.md`, and a named gap is the person's term only if they use it.

## 4. The checkout add-on

A checkbox at checkout:

- it fits with the pamphlet;
- it costs little (the case: $5);
- a few lines on why someone who bought the pamphlet benefits from it, with one sentence on what it gives them.

The case: the pamphlet taught the pricing model; the add-on was a book on client experience, because keeping people paying monthly needs exceptional delivery.

## 5. Offers after purchase

An offer shown once after purchase can do only one of two things:

1. **Extend or speed up what the pamphlet gives.** The case: the pamphlet teaches the pricing model; the offer teaches building offers that win more clients at better prices.
2. **Solve a problem the pamphlet creates.** The case: the pamphlet teaches stacking recurring revenue; the offer teaches managing the extra cash flow.

The page:

1. **A congratulations line** for what they bought.
2. **An easy way out at the top:** "Not interested? Skip this." The buyer comes first.
3. **One more chance** to get more from what they bought.
4. **What it is,** why it matters, and how it connects to the pamphlet.
5. **Two buttons only.** Yes: "Add <product> to my order for $<price>", with a plain note that clicking adds the charge. No: "No thanks."

Each offer is a piece through `skills/craft-sales-copy`. When an offer is a real product of its own, `/offer-designer` shapes it first.

## 6. Fallback offers

When someone says no to an offer:

- **Under about $100:** a stripped-down version at a lower price. "Don't want the whole course? Get the trackers and templates for $27."
- **Over about $100:** a payment plan. "Was $400 too much? Two payments of $200," with only the first charged today.

## 7. The confirmation page

At least: what they bought, and what happens next and when. Beyond that, the next step: a community to join, a call to book, where to start. Only steps that exist.

## 8. The buyer's experience

These hold on every page:

- An easy "no thanks" at the top of every offer page.
- No strong-arming.
- By the second offer, the buyer has given real time. Honour it: "You've already given me a lot of time today, and I don't want to waste any more of it."
- The buyer's experience comes before getting the most money out of them.
- A share of something beats all of nothing.
- Long relationships beat short-term revenue, every time.
