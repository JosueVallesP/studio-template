# Body moves

The cards were first built from 51 full posts read across all 18 hook moves (the two or three highest-engagement posts per move, plus one from the swipe file), then checked against the full sort of all 357 posts below. Same card format as the hooks, with one extra field, "pairs with", because a body has to keep the promise a hook made.

A body has three layers, and they need separate cards because they are chosen separately:

1. **The structure.** The skeleton of the whole post. One per post.
2. **The moves inside.** Smaller things a paragraph does. A post uses several.
3. **The close.** The last beat. One per post.

The hook makes a promise. The structure is how you keep it. The moves inside are how you make each paragraph earn its place. The close is what the reader walks away with.

---

# Layer 1: Structures

## S1. The bare list

**Job.** "Give me all of it, fast."

**Mechanism.** Every item stands alone, so the reader can scan, stop anywhere, and come back. The list gets saved. The hook does all the work of credibility; the list just has to be dense and real.

**What it needs.** Enough items that each one is a real thing, not padding. A source of authority in the hook (your result, years, a sample, a book).

**Shape.** Hook with the promise and the proof. Then numbered items, one line each. Sometimes a single closing line.

**How it fails.**
- Items that are platitudes ("be consistent").
- Items that need explaining but don't get it.
- The number was chosen first and the list stretched to fit.

**Pairs with hooks.** Proof then payoff (1), the numbered promise (12), then and now (5).

**Examples.** L061 (41 longevity rules, 5,013 eng.), L070 (9 things she taught me, 6,537), S036 (12 beliefs about leadership teams), S013 (two-item list, "do interesting things, share them publicly").

## S2. The explained list

**Job.** "Give me the items, and tell me why each one matters."

**Mechanism.** Same scan value as the bare list, but every item gets a sentence or two of reasoning or an example. This is where the writer's thinking shows. The explanation is what makes it the writer's list and not anyone's.

**What it needs.** Items plus, for each one, a reason, a concrete example, or a line from the source. Items should be in an order that means something (the order you learned them, most important first).

**Shape.** Hook. Item title. One to three sentences under it. Repeat. Short close that ties the items together.

**How it fails.**
- Explanations that restate the item ("Find your obsession. It's important to be obsessed.").
- Uneven items: three with substance, nine with a line.
- Too long for the feed; people stop at item 5.

**Pairs with hooks.** Proof then payoff (1), here's the actual document (2), someone notable did something odd (3), the numbered promise (12).

**Examples.** L191 (14 startup lessons, each with the reasoning, 2,135), L214 (the 1982 ad, each principle with the writer's gloss, 4,771), S101 (the JWT test, each prompt plus what it tests), L182 (18 ideas before the win, each with the money and the lesson).

## S3. The story arc

**Job.** "What happened next?"

**Mechanism.** Beat by beat, with the complication in the middle and the turn near the end. The reader has to finish it. The lesson is short and comes after, because the story already made it.

**What it needs.** A real sequence of events with at least one complication (the HR team panicked; the buyer was losing interest; nobody called back). Specific details at each beat. A turn: the moment things went the other way.

**Shape.** Setup in one or two lines. Complication. What the person did. The turn. The result. One or two lines of what it means.

**How it fails.**
- No complication; it's a list of events.
- The lesson is longer than the story.
- The lesson is stated before the story is done, so the ending has nothing left to do.

**Pairs with hooks.** Someone notable did something odd (3), put the reader in the room (4), then and now (5), the line someone said (9).

**Examples.** L189 (Mary Barra, 6,487), L226 (Sara Blakely, 3,097), L008 (the popsicle experiment, 12,258), S012 (the panic attack on the flight), L114 (Morris Chang, 7,317).

## S4. The argument

**Job.** "Convince me."

**Mechanism.** A claim, then the reasons, then the claim again with the reader now agreeing. The body is the proof that the hook's claim isn't just an opinion. Often it names the objection before answering it.

**What it needs.** A claim you hold. At least two independent reasons or pieces of evidence (an example from your work, a number, a named case). An honest version of the other side.

**Shape.** Claim. "Hear me out" or "I get the appeal." Reason one with its evidence. Reason two. The reframe or distinction that resolves it. Claim restated in stronger words.

**How it fails.**
- Reasons that are the claim reworded.
- Only one piece of evidence, and it's an anecdote.
- Skipping the other side, so the reader argues with you instead of reading.

**Pairs with hooks.** The common advice is wrong (6), the call-out (7), the reframe (13), a bet on the future (17), the pattern I keep seeing (8).

**Examples.** L063 (tech stack doesn't matter, 4,222), L163 (marketing isn't measurable, two real examples, 2,052), S079 (what "authentic" means, resolved with a 2x2), L121 (Conway's Law, 1,924), S089 (attention is not expertise).

## S5. The breakdown

**Job.** "Show me how it was done, piece by piece."

**Mechanism.** Someone's work (a deal, a campaign, a method, an email) taken apart, each part named and explained. The reader gets a method they can copy, and the writer shows they can see what others don't. It borrows the credibility of the thing being broken down.

**What it needs.** A real, specific thing that worked (with the result stated). Access to how it was done. The ability to name each part and say why it mattered.

**Shape.** Hook with the result. Short context block. Numbered parts, each with a name and what it did. Takeaway in one or two lines.

**How it fails.**
- The parts are generic ("she built trust") instead of specific actions.
- The context block runs too long before the breakdown starts.
- The takeaway contradicts or oversells the breakdown.

**Pairs with hooks.** Proof then payoff (1), here's the actual document (2), the line someone said (9), someone notable did something odd (3).

**Examples.** S007 (how Morgan closed $1M), L193 (the framework used to compete with PowerPoint, 3,469), L218 (James Webb Young's five steps, 1,237), L129 (what Wispr did in India, 2,395).

## S6. The experiment report

**Job.** "You tried it. What happened?"

**Mechanism.** The writer did a thing with a real risk of a boring result, and reports what changed. The honesty of the format carries it: the findings are observations, not advice. Readers trust a report more than a recommendation.

**What it needs.** Something you did for a defined period (a month without a smartphone, a price change, 80 invitations). Observations that surprised you. A verdict.

**Shape.** What you did and for how long. "I was surprised by" or "here's what happened." Findings as a short list of observations. Verdict. Often an open offer to answer questions.

**How it fails.**
- Findings that were the point all along (the experiment was staged to prove something).
- No surprise; everything went as expected.
- Turning observations into commandments.

**Pairs with hooks.** Then and now (5), the admission (15), put the reader in the room (4), the finding (11).

**Examples.** L035 (one month on a flip phone, 13,604), L164 (1,735 days of WHOOP, 1,476), L008 (the popsicle question), L015 (200 miles and no marketing lesson, 2,903).

## S7. The body is the hook

**Job.** None beyond the hook. The claim stands alone.

**Mechanism.** The line is complete; anything added would weaken it. Works when the claim is sharp enough that readers finish it in their heads, and the comments do the rest.

**What it needs.** A single line that is specific, a little provocative, and obviously true to the people it's for. The confidence to stop.

**Shape.** One to three lines. No explanation.

**How it fails.**
- The line needs explaining and doesn't get it.
- It's a slogan, not a claim.
- Used so often the account looks lazy.

**Pairs with hooks.** The call-out (7), the pattern I keep seeing (8), a bet on the future (17), the common advice is wrong (6).

**Examples.** L067 ($200k employees vibe coding a Calendly replacement, one line, 2,827), L007 (6 figures a year vs 6 figures a day, 1,284), L113 (bullish on IRL, five lines, 3,352), L131 (the kindergarten best friends, 2,908).

## S8. The script

**Job.** "Say what everyone's thinking."

**Mechanism.** The post is written as if spoken by someone else (the honest founder, the honest brand, the bad CEO and the good CEO). The format lets the writer say true things that would sound mean in their own voice.

**What it needs.** A genre of speech the audience knows by heart. Real inside knowledge. A straight face.

**Shape.** The frame ("if people told the truth"). Then the speech or the list of lines, in character, all the way to the end. Often a question to the reader to add their own.

**How it fails.**
- Breaking character to explain the joke.
- Cruelty aimed at a person rather than a genre.
- The translation isn't accurate, so insiders don't laugh.

**Pairs with hooks.** The honest version (14), the line someone said (9).

**Examples.** L022 (the honest Series A announcement, 1,521), L168 (honest brand ads, 1,364), L119 (bad CEO / good CEO).

## S9. The announcement

**Job.** "What is it, why now, and what do you want from me?"

**Mechanism.** Not an argument and not a story. The reader already knows you and wants the facts: what you made, why you made it, who it's for, and what to do next. The ones that work spend most of the body on the why (the gap you saw, the thing you wished existed) and keep the ask to one line.

**What it needs.** A real thing to announce. An origin: the observation or frustration that led to it. The specific person it's for. One ask.

**Shape.** The news in one line, sometimes a short TLDR. The origin. What it is and who it's for. Credits or thanks if they're real. One ask.

**How it fails.**
- All ask, no origin.
- The thanks list is longer than the announcement.
- Announcing to people who don't know you, with no story to carry it.

**Pairs with hooks.** The announcement (18). Also proof then payoff (1) when the launch sits on a result.

**Examples.** L088 (Science x Story launch, 2,100), L071 (JustaDesignList), S093 (the comms community), L118 ("I'm building something new"), L040 (the Second Best beer company).

# Layer 2: Moves inside the body

These are paragraph-level. A post uses two to five of them.

## M1. The context block

**Job.** "Who, what, when, so I can follow."

**Mechanism.** Two to four lines of facts the reader needs before the main part lands. Done fast, it buys trust (you know the details). Done slow, it loses the reader before the post starts.

**What it needs.** Only the facts the post actually uses. Often labeled ("BACKGROUND:", "CONTEXT", "The book is...").

**How it fails.** Too long. Facts the post never uses. Opinion smuggled in as context.

**Examples.** S007 ("BACKGROUND: Morgan is a superstar AE... Deel is a $12B HR-Tech giant"), L218 ("The book is 'A Technique for Producing Ideas'... Young wasn't an academic"), L221 ("Back in 2014, they weren't printing new copies").

## M2. The turn

**Job.** "Here's where it goes the other way."

**Mechanism.** A one-line pivot after the setup: "But Barra held firm." "Instead, she went to work." "But the math isn't the problem. The mindset is." The turn is where the reader's expectation breaks. Every story and every argument has one; the best posts make it a single short line.

**What it needs.** A real reversal. A setup that made the reader expect the opposite.

**How it fails.** The turn is announced ("Here's the twist:") instead of made. More than one turn, so none lands. A turn with no setup.

**Examples.** L189 ("The HR team panicked... But Barra held firm."), L129 ("Then they wonder why nobody buys. But the math isn't the problem."), L042 ("The traditional startup playbook... But there's another way.").

## M3. The evidence pair

**Job.** "Prove it."

**Mechanism.** Two or three concrete cases, labeled, each short. Two is the minimum for a pattern; one is an anecdote. Labeling them ("Example #1", bullets, dashes) tells the reader how much is coming.

**What it needs.** Real cases with a number or a name in each. Cases that are different from each other, so they don't read as one case twice.

**How it fails.** One case stretched into two. Cases without specifics. Five cases where two would do.

**Examples.** L163 ("Example #1: ... 4 new leads and 2 asset downloads. Example #2: 90% of my inbound leads have never commented"), L042 (three "when our X needed Y, they built Z" examples), S017 (six CEOs quoted on brand).

## M4. The data line

**Job.** "Is that true, or just a story?"

**Mechanism.** A short set of numbers dropped into a story or argument to show the case is not an exception. The numbers do in three lines what three paragraphs of argument couldn't.

**What it needs.** Numbers from a source you can name. Two or three at most, each one doing a different job.

**How it fails.** Numbers without a source. So many numbers it becomes a report. A number that doesn't support the point it sits next to.

**Examples.** L114 ("The average successful founder is 42. A 50-year-old is nearly twice as likely..."), L022 ("There is a 30% chance I get fired as CEO by my own board"), L015 ("Last year roughly 0.0007% of the world's population completed a race of 100 miles or more").

## M5. The side-by-side

**Job.** "Show me the difference."

**Mechanism.** Two versions placed next to each other: the jargon and the plain version, the wrong line and the right line, the old way and the new way. The reader sees the gap without being told about it.

**What it needs.** Two things that are the same in every way except the one that matters.

**How it fails.** The two sides differ in several ways, so the point is muddy. The "wrong" side is a straw man. Stacked fragments ("Not X. Not Y. It's Z.") doing the job a real comparison should.

**Examples.** L214 ("'The biota exhibited a one hundred percent mortality response.' Translation: 'All the fish died.'"), L129 ("Price-sensitive = wants something cheap. Spending-conscious = every rupee must be worth it."), L232 (the "trust me" line vs the "I'm recommending X because" line), S042 (conscious mind vs subconscious mind, two columns).

## M6. The analogy

**Job.** "Make the abstract thing physical."

**Mechanism.** The idea is mapped onto something the reader already understands with their hands: a check-engine light, a pilot's checklist, a horse and buggy next to a plane, fast food. One analogy, used once, then dropped.

**What it needs.** An idea that is hard to picture. A physical thing that maps onto it in the one way that matters, and doesn't mislead in other ways.

**How it fails.** Mixed analogies. An analogy that is more complicated than the idea. Riding it for the whole post.

**Examples.** S041 ("Like the check engine warning light going off in your car"), L014 ("Every pilot takes off using the same checklist"), S042 (horse and buggy vs plane), L240 ("the new fast food of market research").

## M7. The other side, taken seriously

**Job.** "You've heard my objection. Answer it."

**Mechanism.** Before the turn, the writer states the opposing view fairly, sometimes generously ("I get the appeal", "I get why. Deadlines are tight."). It removes the reader's main reason to stop reading and makes the turn feel earned.

**What it needs.** The real objection, in the words the reader would use. An answer to it that comes after.

**How it fails.** A weak version of the objection. Agreeing with it so much the turn never comes. Making it a paragraph when it needs a line.

**Examples.** S079 ("I get the appeal. After all, it's hard to believe someone is performing when they're showing you their worst moments."), L089 ("I get why. Deadlines are tight. The blank canvas is always one click away."), L096 ("Some days AI saves me three hours. Some days I spend three hours arguing with Claude").

## M8. The subject's own words

**Job.** "Let them say it."

**Mechanism.** A quote from the person in the story, placed at the moment of the lesson. The lesson lands in their voice, which is more credible than the writer's, and the writer doesn't have to editorialize.

**What it needs.** A real quote, verifiable or firsthand. Placed where it does the most work (usually right at or after the turn).

**How it fails.** Invented quotes. A quote that says what the writer already said. Quotes everywhere, so the post is a clip reel.

**Examples.** L189 (Barra: "If they can't handle 'dress appropriately,' what other judgment decisions are they not making?"), L114 (Chang: "I could not have done it sooner... Because I was the first one."), S007 (Morgan: "No way I could have used email. It's a deal killer"), L226 (Blakely: "I saw how good my butt looked").

## M9. The meaning line

**Job.** "So what was that about?"

**Mechanism.** One or two sentences after a story or a list that say what it meant, in the plainest possible words. Often a contrast ("Most companies write longer policies to avoid problems. Mary wrote shorter ones to create leaders."). It should feel like the reader got there a second before you said it.

**What it needs.** A story or list that already made the point. One sentence that names it without stretching it.

**How it fails.** The meaning is bigger than the story. More than two sentences. A moral the story doesn't support.

**Examples.** L189 (the two-line contrast above), L008 ("The experiment wasn't really about popsicles. It showed me that people want to know their neighbors."), L121 ("The shorthand for this is simple: you ship your org chart."), L226 ("She didn't get there by 'thinking like a CEO.' She got there by being scrappy.").

# Layer 3: Closes

## C1. The restated principle

**Job.** "Say it once more so I remember it."

**Mechanism.** The post's idea, in one line, stronger than the hook because the reader has now seen the evidence. Often a contrast or a short imperative.

**Needs.** The body to have earned it. New words, not the hook copied.

**Fails.** A summary paragraph. A new idea introduced at the end.

**Examples.** L063 ("TASTE > TECHNOLOGY."), L042 ("The future belongs to tiny teams of extraordinary people."), L015 ("Being smart will only get you so far. Being dumb will get you at least 200 miles further."), L214 ("Most people don't write clearly because they're trying to impress others instead of just being clear.").

## C2. The question back

**Job.** "Now you."

**Mechanism.** Hands the reader the idea to apply to themselves, or invites them to add to the list. Gets comments, and the comments are the content.

**Needs.** A question with a specific answer the reader can give in one line.

**Fails.** "Thoughts?" A question the post already answered. A question nobody wants to answer in public.

**Examples.** S027 ("Who's the last person you introduced to someone else, just because they needed to meet?"), L168 ("What else would you add?"), S101 ("Which of these prompts would be the hardest for you to answer?"), L114 ("What could you build with all your hard-earned wisdom?").

## C3. The small instruction

**Job.** "What do I do with this?"

**Mechanism.** One concrete thing the reader can try, sized small ("try going old school for five minutes", "pull up your org chart", "notice which tense you're in"). It turns an idea into a behavior without turning the post into a how-to.

**Needs.** One action, under a minute, that uses the idea.

**Fails.** A list of actions. A big one ("rebuild your strategy"). Advice disguised as instruction.

**Examples.** L089 ("if you're stuck, try going old school for five minutes. Pencil. Paper. No undo button."), L121 ("Pull up your org chart. Look at where the boundaries sit between teams."), S082 ("Next time you write something, just notice which tense you're in").

## C4. The open door

**Job.** "I can ask, or I can get the thing."

**Mechanism.** An offer: answer questions, send a template, a link. Works when it follows a post that gave real value and when the offer is directly related. Converts attention into a relationship or a lead.

**Needs.** Something real to offer. A post that earned the ask.

**Fails.** The offer is bigger than the post. "Comment X and I'll DM you" on a post with no substance. Three asks (follow, repost, link) stacked.

**Examples.** L035 ("Happy to answer any questions for those wanting to make the switch"), S007 (the Deal Room template link), L088 ("If this is you then we should talk. If this isn't you, we should talk sooner."), L096 ("Comment 'PROMPTDADDY' and I'll DM you a blank Figma file").

## C5. The aside

**Job.** "End on a human."

**Mechanism.** A last line that drops the register: a joke, a credit, a small confession. It tells the reader the writer is a person, and it keeps the post from ending on a lesson.

**Needs.** A real detail. The nerve to not tie a bow.

**Fails.** A joke that undercuts the post. A P.S. that's a sales pitch.

**Examples.** L131 ("except samantha, who said she wasn't his best friend. samantha needs to chill."), S029 ("I guess I'd better be on my best behavior with contract renewal coming up!"), L015 ("you'll probably trip balls for the last 9 hours and have every rock in the trail turn into a face").

## C6. No close

**Job.** None. The last beat of the story or the last item is the end.

**Mechanism.** Stopping on the strongest line, usually the end of the story or the last item of a list. Anything after would be weaker.

**Needs.** A last beat strong enough to end on.

**Fails.** Stopping because you ran out, not because it was done.

**Examples.** S012 ("She sat next to Jenny Acuff."), L070 (ends on item 9), L182 (ends on "and now... Hampton").

## Notes from the full sort (all 357 posts)

**Counts.** The argument is the most common structure (119 posts), then the story arc (69) and the explained list (61). The experiment report (8) and the script (4) are rare but distinct. Only 5 posts had no structure once the announcement got its own card.

**Structure follows the hook, and the library confirms it.** From the sorted posts:
- Proof then payoff goes to the explained list (17), the breakdown (10) or the bare list (6).
- Here's the actual document goes to the explained list (12).
- Someone notable did something odd goes to the story arc (16).
- Put the reader in the room goes to the story arc (23 of 31).
- Then and now goes to the story arc (10).
- The common advice is wrong goes to the argument (27 of 36).
- The call-out goes to the argument (16) or the body is the hook (6).
- News plus a take, the reframe, a bet on the future, the finding, the pattern: all go to the argument.
- The numbered promise splits between the explained list (10) and the bare list (9).
- The honest version goes to the script (4 of 6).
- The admission goes to the story arc (5) or the experiment report.
- The line someone said goes to the argument (9) or the story arc (6): the argument when the line is a claim to answer, the story arc when it's a moment to replay.
- Do this right now has too few posts to call (5, spread across four structures); use the explained list when there are steps, the argument when there's one instruction to defend.
- The announcement goes to its own structure, the announcement (8 of 11).

These pairs hold because both the hook and the structure follow from the same thing: the material and what the reader needs to get the idea. A scene opens a story because the idea came from something that happened. So the studio picks the shape from the reader's questions first and uses these pairs as a check on the choice (the moves stage, step 4b); a pair outside them is allowed when the post can say why.

**Closes.** The restated principle is used in 143 posts, far more than any other. But in the Storied file the posts with no close (median 907 engagements) and the small instruction (792) and the question back (748) outperform the restated principle (547). One library, not a law, but the default close is the weakest one.

**The turn and the meaning line are the most used inside moves** (91 posts each). The data line (75) and the context block (68) come next. The evidence pair (34) and the analogy (33) are used less than they're worth.

**Short beats long in this sample.** The one-line and five-line posts (L067, L131, L113, L007) sit with the biggest engagement, and the longest posts (L247, L061) are the ones that get saved. Two different jobs. The library should treat "the body is the hook" as a real structure and not a cheat.

**The turn is the most reused move.** Nearly every story arc, argument and experiment report has one, and the best ones make it a single line. Worth building a check for: does the draft have a turn, and is it one line?

**Things that aren't moves.** Hashtags, "repost to help your network", emoji bullets, credit lists. Connectors and furniture. Same as with hooks, worth a short list so the AI doesn't learn them as moves.

The full chain for every post (hook move, structure, inside moves, close) lives in `content-moves-sorted.csv` in this folder.
