# Hook moves

A move is a thing a hook does to the reader, not a sentence shape. Two hooks with different wording can be the same move. One shape can serve two different moves. We file by the job, because the job is what tells you when to use it and when a draft is failing.

18 moves drawn from the 357 posts in the two libraries (S = swipe file, L = Storied library, with engagement numbers), each post sorted by its primary and secondary move in `content-moves-sorted.csv`. Every example below is a real opening from those posts.

## The card

Each move has these fields:

- **Job.** The question in the reader's head that the hook answers.
- **Mechanism.** Why it works, in one or two sentences. This is what lets an AI judge a draft instead of filling a blank.
- **What it needs.** The raw material you must have before you can use it. If you don't have it, you can't use the move. This is the field that turns the library into a tool: start from what the person has, then see which moves that material can carry.
- **Shape.** The skeleton in plain words. Not a fill-in template.
- **How it fails.** The common ways a draft gets the shape right and the move wrong.
- **Good for.** What the move tends to buy: trust, reach, leads, recruiting, a relationship. This is the hook for the strategy layer later.
- **Examples.** Openings from the library, with IDs so we can pull the full post.

## 1. Proof, then payoff

**Job.** "Why should I listen to you?" and then "What do I get if I keep reading?"

**Mechanism.** The proof makes the promise believable. A promise without proof is a claim; a result without a promise is a brag. Together, the result earns the right to teach. The stronger the link between what you did and what you promise, the stronger the hook.

**What it needs.** A real result, a long practice, or a large sample (years, number of clients, pieces written, dollars, deal reviews). And a payoff that comes from that exact experience.

**Shape.** The specific thing you did or got. Then what you'll hand over because of it.

**How it fails.**
- The proof doesn't earn the payoff ("I've posted for 3 years, here are my 5 favorite coffee shops").
- The number is vague or rounded into nothing ("years of experience").
- The payoff is generic ("here's what I learned") instead of a named thing (a 7-step process, 9 books, the exact test).

**Good for.** Trust and leads. It's the move that turns a body of work into authority.

**Examples.**
- S001: "In the past 3 months, I've generated 6,700 saves and 1,690 new followers with what I call 'Proof → Payoff' hooks. Here's the exact formula I use (and why it's so effective):"
- S050: "I've onboarded 18 ghostwriting clients in the past 1.5 years. Before I write a single piece of content, I run a 7-step research process..."
- S010: "I've managed 100+ AEs and run probably 100,000 deal reviews... Here are the 8 biggest mistakes AEs make when selling with champions:"
- L148: "I've sold $443k in digital products using this landing page formula in 8 months. 8 sections. Each one has a job."
- L078: "My newsletter has 133k subs, makes a half million $$ a year, and takes me 5 hours a week. The #1 question I get, by far, is 'how do I grow my newsletter?'"
- L196: "I asked 200+ HR leaders about the ONE book that transformed how they hire. Here's 9 books that came up again and again"
- L092: "I have 2.4 stars on Glassdoor. But over the last year, we've had near-zero voluntary employee attrition. Here's how I retain top talent:" (the proof is a contradiction, which makes it stronger)

## 2. Here's the actual document

**Job.** "Is this real?" and "Can I see it?"

**Mechanism.** A real object (a memo, a letter, a test, an email, a book) carries its own authority and its own curiosity. The reader doesn't have to trust you; they get to see the thing. Age and rarity add to it: a 79-year-old letter that still holds up is proof that the idea is durable.

**What it needs.** A real, verifiable artifact you can quote verbatim. Plus one fact that makes it worth seeing (who wrote it, when, what it cost, how many people it reached).

**Shape.** Who made it, when, and one striking fact about it. Then "here it is."

**How it fails.**
- Paraphrasing instead of quoting. The move depends on the verbatim object.
- Picking an artifact nobody can check, or getting a date or figure wrong.
- Adding an advice section that the artifact didn't need.

**Good for.** Reach and trust at the same time. These are the "classics" posts.

**Examples.**
- S098: "In 1947, the creative director of Grey Advertising sent a letter to his bosses warning them what the agency was becoming. It's crazy to think this is 79 years old and not a single worry in it has expired:"
- S077 / L220: "David Ogilvy built a $363.2 million advertising empire. In 1982, he sent this internal memo to 3,000+ employees:"
- S101 / L213: "In 1984, J. Walter Thompson ran this copy test once. Thousands took it. Only 10 people landed jobs... Here is the exact test they had to pass:"
- L214 (4,771 engagements): "In 1982, a paper company paid to teach America how to write. The editor of Reader's Digest wrote the ad. 44 years later, it's still the most useful thing I've read for the age of AI slop:"
- S100 / L221: "In 2014, I found a book so rare I had to pay $900 for it. It's 60 years old now, but it's still the best marketing advice I've ever read:"
- L106: "This is the only cold email I answered in the last 12 months. Here's why it worked on me:"
- L216: "Every year for 60 years, Warren Buffett sat down and wrote a letter... He started nearly every draft with these exact words: 'Dear Doris and Bertie.'"

## 3. Someone notable did something odd

**Job.** "Why would they do that?"

**Mechanism.** A known name plus an unexpected, specific action creates a gap the reader wants closed. The name lends credibility to the lesson before the lesson is stated. The odder and more concrete the action, the stronger the pull.

**What it needs.** A true story about a real, recognizable person or company, with one specific action you can verify. A point of your own that the story carries (otherwise it's trivia).

**Shape.** Name, moment, the odd thing they did. Sometimes a quoted line. Then the story.

**How it fails.**
- The action isn't odd or specific ("Steve Jobs cared about design").
- The story is unverified or embellished.
- The lesson is bolted on and the story doesn't actually show it.

**Good for.** Reach. In this library, this move and "news plus a take" produce most of the biggest posts.

**Examples.**
- L189 (6,487): "When Mary Barra took over GM's HR department, she found a 10-page dress code policy. She replaced all 10 pages with just two words: 'Dress appropriately.'"
- S049: "When Indra Nooyi became CEO of PepsiCo in 2006, she started writing handwritten letters to her senior leaders' parents."
- L053: "When Herb Kelleher ran Southwest Airlines, he made new employees clean planes...including the toilets. Even senior hires."
- S027: "In 1957, an investor gave a young Warren Buffett $100,000 to manage. When Buffett asked why, the man said: 'Because you remind me of Charlie Munger.'"
- S087: "Tolkien spent about 12 years writing The Lord of the Rings. For most of that time, he had an audience of one: His friend C.S. Lewis."
- L050 (2,929): "In 1996, a 33-year-old CEO flew to Tokyo to tell his biggest customer three things. Each one was more humiliating than the last."
- L206: "Japan has created a World Cup tradition like no other. After their brilliant 2-2 draw against the Netherlands, the Japanese players left their dressing room spotless."
- L215: "Massimo Vignelli used only 4 typefaces across a 50-year career as a designer"

## 4. Put the reader in the room

**Job.** "What's happening?" The reader is dropped into a moment and wants to see how it plays out.

**Mechanism.** Concrete sensory detail (a time, a place, a line of dialogue) makes the reader present before they've decided whether to care. Stories pull harder than claims because the reader has to finish them.

**What it needs.** A real moment you lived, with one or two specific details you remember exactly. It must lead somewhere: a turn, a realization, a thing the moment proved.

**Shape.** Time or place. One small action. A detail that doesn't fit. Then the story.

**How it fails.**
- Starting with the setup instead of the moment ("Last week I was thinking about...").
- The moment doesn't go anywhere; it's a diary entry.
- Details that are generic ("a busy day at the office") instead of exact ("It's 9:28 AM").

**Good for.** Being seen as a person. Relationships more than reach, though the best ones get both.

**Examples.**
- L008 (12,258): "I had a simple question: if I invited my neighbors over for a popsicle, would anyone actually show up?"
- S032 / L254: "I'm on a flight. It's 9:28 AM."
- S012: "There's a panic attack you can't see in this photo. Yesterday, Jenny and I flew from Santa Barbara to Denver."
- L105: "Last week I was at an 8-person founder breakfast in Aspen. Guy goes around the table and asks everyone how they're using AI day-to-day... Me: 'I don't.' Everybody looked at me like I had five heads."
- L143: "I asked my co-founder to argue with me in front of our whole team. That one moment changed our entire company culture."
- L110: "Last week I hosted a dinner with 12 strangers. We played games with magnet tiles and gold coins. And we accidentally proved why your manager's feedback isn't working."
- L131: "i asked my son who his best friends are in kindergarten... he named ten kids. TEN!"

## 5. Then and now

**Job.** "How did you get from there to here?"

**Mechanism.** Two points in time, far apart, with the gap unexplained. The distance between them is the promise. It also carries proof: the "now" is the credential.

**What it needs.** A real before and a real after, both specific. The bigger the honest gap, the better. A reason for the change you can actually explain.

**Shape.** Then: a number, a state, a date. Now: the opposite. Then what happened in between, or the one thing that changed.

**How it fails.**
- The "then" is invented or dramatized to make the gap bigger.
- The "now" is the whole point and the post is a brag.
- The cause of the change is "I worked hard."

**Good for.** Trust and reach. Also the natural home for origin stories.

**Examples.**
- L054: "$17,000,000. That's what I sold my company for in 2001. Ten years later, I walked into Google."
- L070 (6,537): "I never made more than $3M per year before I met wife. 2 years after I took home $17M in personal income. She taught me:"
- L016: "In 1990, I landed in Australia with £70 in my pocket. 4 months later, I flew home with $15,000 from selling my 1st ever business."
- S045: "3 years ago, I was called crazy for posting here. Now it's where 99% of my creative opportunities are generated from."
- S003: "Investor: 'Posting on LinkedIn is not a CEO's job'. I almost believed them. But one year later, those very posts helped lock in $$$ Millions during our Series A."
- L076: "2013: Tried to build a music app (failed). / 2014: Taught myself how to code..." (the timeline variant)
- L035 (13,604): "It's officially been 1 month since I switched to a flip phone. And I gotta be honest, I have been surprised by several things:" (the "I did the experiment, here's what changed" variant)

## 6. The common advice is wrong

**Job.** "Wait, I believed that. Why is it wrong?"

**Mechanism.** The reader holds a belief; the hook names it and denies it. They read on to defend the belief or to replace it. It only works when the belief is common in the audience and the argument is real.

**What it needs.** A piece of advice your audience actually follows, named plainly. An argument that holds up, built on your own reasoning or experience. (Run it through the copylosophy check; a contrarian hook with a weak argument burns trust.)

**Shape.** The advice, in the words people use. "I think that's wrong" or "it works, kind of." Then why.

**How it fails.**
- Attacking a belief nobody holds (a straw man).
- Being contrarian for its own sake with no replacement idea.
- "Unpopular opinion:" followed by a popular opinion.

**Good for.** Point of view. This is how a person becomes known for a position.

**Examples.**
- S018: "I don't understand this obsessive advice to 'raise your prices'. Price is a very simple concept:"
- S081: "A common piece of advice in personal branding is to pick a topic and own it. Become 'the hooks guy' or 'the pricing lady.' And it works, kind of..."
- L043: "Most startups are worrying about 'moats' way too early."
- L048: "I ghostwrite for a whole bunch of LinkedIn accounts. And one of the most frustrating things I've had to learn: Nobody wants your wise thoughts."
- L163: "Arguably the hardest marketing pill to swallow (and people have been fighting it for years): Sometimes marketing isn't measurable"
- L063 (4,222): "I'm the Head of Growth at Clay and I'm here to tell you your tech stack doesn't matter."
- S086: "If your business model only needs 19 clients to hit your annual income goal, what the hell are you doing chasing a million strangers?"
- L248: "Most people think strategy is about setting goals. Wrong."

## 7. The call-out

**Job.** "Someone finally said it."

**Mechanism.** The writer names a thing the reader is annoyed by but hasn't said out loud, often about their own industry. The emotional charge is the hook; the reader feels permission. Works best from inside ("my own industry").

**What it needs.** A real grievance your audience shares. Standing to say it (you're in the group you're calling out). Enough restraint that the post has a point beyond the anger.

**Shape.** "I know this will make me unpopular with X, but..." or a flat statement of the thing that's wrong. Then the specifics.

**How it fails.**
- Punching down or at strangers.
- No standing: calling out an industry you're not part of.
- Anger with no argument. The reader nods and forgets.

**Good for.** Reach and loyalty from the people who agree. Costs you some of the people who don't. Use with the strategy in mind.

**Examples.**
- S065 / L116: "I know this will make me unpopular with my own industry but I think the coaching world has a massive accountability problem and we need to talk about it:"
- S089: "It pisses me off when so-called 'experts' whose only skill is building an audience hand out advice on business, money, health, and relationships as if they really knew what they're talking about."
- L057: "It pisses me off when founders act like the fact that I am running my business to make money is some kind of low brow pursuit."
- L096 (3,143): "It's getting weird out here. Everyone and their mother has a framework now."
- L140: "You CANNOT TELL ME that role, the one with more than 1000 applicants, which has been posted for months and was reposted again, is Still. Open."
- L059: "The reason so many people passionately hate AI is because it is society's way of saying 'you don't matter.'"
- L180: "Dear CEOs, Investors, Boards, Take some time to understand the discipline of marketing."

## 8. The pattern I keep seeing

**Job.** "What do the people who have what I want have in common?"

**Mechanism.** An observed regularity across a sample ("the most successful people I know all...") carries implied proof without a number. The reader wants the trait so they can check themselves against it.

**What it needs.** A real sample you've actually watched (clients, colleagues, people you've studied). One trait, named concretely. Not a platitude.

**Shape.** "The most X people I know all Y." Or: "A mistake I see over and over." Then what the trait looks like in practice.

**How it fails.**
- The trait is a cliché ("they work hard", "they're consistent").
- No sample behind it; it's just an opinion in a lab coat.
- Listing ten traits instead of one.

**Good for.** Trust. It positions the writer as someone who has seen a lot.

**Examples.**
- S013: "The most effective networking strategy I've found has nothing to do with conferences, cocktail hours, cold emails, or any of the common ideas you hear."
- L161: "The most successful people I know all have an almost irrational belief that everything will work out. And I just recently learned the word for it: Pronoia."
- L186: "I've noticed that the most accomplished people I know share one trait: They have an enormous capacity for independent thought."
- S090: "A few hundred people who trust you, who think your work is genius, and who would take your call will do more for you than having 100,000 followers online (and this comes from someone with over 100,000 followers online)."
- L068: "A mistake I've painfully made over and over again: Its almost always better to hire people who've already done what you want done"
- L233: "IMO When folks have trouble being concise, the problem isn't usually the delivery. The problem is you actually don't know your idea as well as you think you do."

## 9. The line someone said

**Job.** "I've heard that exact sentence." Or: "I've said that exact sentence."

**Mechanism.** A quoted line in someone's real voice is instantly recognizable and sets up a response. When it's the reader's own line, they feel seen. When it's a client's, an investor's, a boss's, it creates a small conflict the reader wants resolved.

**What it needs.** A real line, as close to verbatim as you can get. A response that the reader didn't expect.

**Shape.** The quote, in quotation marks, first. Then your answer or what happened next.

**How it fails.**
- The line is invented and sounds like it ("Many people ask me...").
- The response is the obvious one.
- Several quotes stacked up with no response (that's a different move, the honest version, below).

**Good for.** Relationships and leads. People DM when they recognize their own words.

**Examples.**
- L252: "'I share everything I know. I get plenty of likes. But my content doesn't attract clients.' I hear this all the time, especially from creators with 10k-50k followers."
- L114 (7,317): "'I'm too old to start a company.' If Morris Chang had believed that at 55, we wouldn't have TSMC"
- L125: "'Why's my engagement so low??' Well... maybe because you never actually engage back."
- S025: "I hear a lot of young people say, 'I can't date right now, I gotta focus on my career.' I think that's B.S."
- S067 / L204: "The best advice I've ever got about delivering bad news was from the head of people at Morrisons. He said: 'When you've got to deliver bad news, deliver it kindly, deliver it straight.'"
- L181: "I hear this so often from founders and CEOs... They look at their People team and say: 'Fix the culture.'"
- L184: "I've been a marketer for 9 years. This is the most surprising question I've been asked: 'Testing sounds interesting, but it's not for us. Can we skip to the results phase?'"
- L095: "I once hired an agency to make a funny video ad for Ahrefs. They sent me a script. I read it and thought: 'this doesn't seem funny.' Their response?"

## 10. News, plus a take

**Job.** "What does this mean for me?"

**Mechanism.** The event brings the attention; the take is the reason to read this post about it instead of the hundred others. Timing does half the work. The post lives or dies on whether the take is yours and not the consensus.

**What it needs.** Something that actually happened, recent, with a source. A take that comes from your own position or expertise and that the reader wouldn't get from the headline.

**Shape.** The fact, stated flat, with the specific number or name. Then the turn: "and here's what it actually means" or "and this is why I'm not surprised."

**How it fails.**
- Reporting the news with no take.
- A take that is just the obvious reaction.
- Posting three days late.

**Good for.** Reach, fast. Builds a reputation for having a view on things.

**Examples.**
- L091 (12,571): "The Amazon River just wrote its first ever brand logo. Literally."
- L042 (6,880): "The New York Times profiled a start-up with 28 employees serving nearly 50 million users. That company is us."
- L052 (4,598): "BREAKING: Meta laid off 8,000 people today via 4am emails."
- L173: "Fortune 500 CEOs are now paying McKinsey to explain why a 24-year-old with a ring light is eating their lunch."
- L066: "Kylie Jenner lost 15 million Instagram followers yesterday. Not in a year. Not in a month. In a few hours."
- S052: "Apple's new CEO posted on social for the first time today. It contained one word. 'hello'"
- L169 (4,458): "End of an era. The MTV music channels are shutting down end of this year."
- L211: "While everyone's building AI companies, Mark Cuban just invested 7-figures in... an events company."
- S017: "Unilever's Fernando Fernandez says the days of underinvesting in brands to squeeze out more profit are over." (three CEOs stacked as evidence)

## 11. The finding

**Job.** "Is that true? Show me the data."

**Mechanism.** A specific, surprising number from a real sample. Like news, but you made the news yourself, which is its own credential. The surprise has to be real; a number that confirms what everyone thinks is a footnote.

**What it needs.** A survey, a study, an analysis you ran or can cite exactly. A result that contradicts expectation.

**Shape.** The surprising result first, with the number. Then the sample ("we surveyed 101 CMOs"). Then what it changes.

**How it fails.**
- Burying the finding under the method.
- The number is not surprising.
- The sample is too small or too vague to carry the claim.

**Good for.** Trust and reach, and it gets reposted more than most moves.

**Examples.**
- L239: "You're pitching to the wrong person. We asked 101 B2B SaaS CMOs how they actually select vendors... 82% of CMOs enter after the team has narrowed the list down to the final few."
- L241: "Agencies are getting fired. We surveyed B2B SaaS mid-market marketing leaders about where they're cutting budgets. The #1 answer wasn't paid ads. Wasn't tools. Wasn't events."
- L069: "Slop doesn't pay. A 16-month study of 2,000 AI-generated articles on new websites found that, despite an initial surge, search performance collapsed"
- L166: "LinkedIn profiles with fewer than 500 followers produced the biggest reach outliers in November."
- L083: "The Great Flattening is upon us. Barron's just surfaced a stat that should stop every marketing leader in their tracks: AlphaSense logged 73 corporate documents using the exact phrase 'not just X, it's Y' in Q4 2025 alone"
- L129: "We dropped Wispr Flow's price from $12 to ₹300 ($3.30) in India. Then our Indian users started converting at rates we'd..."

## 12. The numbered promise

**Job.** "How much am I getting, and can I scan it?"

**Mechanism.** A number sets the size of the promise and signals the post is scannable. Readers save lists. Usually sits on top of another move (proof, a book, a story) which supplies the credibility. On its own it's weak.

**What it needs.** An actual list where every item earns its place. Usually a source of authority for the list (your experience, a book, a sample).

**Shape.** "[Number] [things] about [topic]:" plus the credential, then the list.

**How it fails.**
- The number is the only hook (no proof, no angle).
- Items are padding to reach the number.
- The list could have been written by anyone.

**Good for.** Saves and followers. Less for relationships.

**Examples.**
- S036: "12 things I believe about building high-performing leadership teams:"
- L191: "Building a startup is harder than I ever imagined. 14 lessons in the order I learned them:"
- L231: "To improve your writing, delete these 9 words:"
- L176: "25 things I wish I knew about LinkedIn when I started: (5 years and 1,000+ posts later)"
- L014: "Whenever I want to learn how to do anything. I do these 5 things to be the best at it."
- S039: "The top 10 habits that block your potential:"
- L171: "10 ideas for greater clarity in 2026"

## 13. The reframe

**Job.** "Oh. I never thought of it that way."

**Mechanism.** A familiar thing is described through a different lens, and the lens explains things the old description couldn't. The reader gets a new tool for seeing. Works when the reframe is both surprising and obviously true once said.

**What it needs.** An idea of your own, or a borrowed one you can credit (Conway's law). A familiar situation it reorganizes. It has to survive the test: does the new frame explain more than the old one?

**Shape.** The familiar thing. "Is actually" or "is not X, it's Y" (used once, with the explanation right behind it). Then the lens applied.

**How it fails.**
- The reframe is a slogan with nothing behind it.
- Metaphor for its own sake.
- Stacked "Not X. Not Y. It's Z." fragments standing in for the explanation.

**Good for.** Being remembered for an idea. This is the move most tied to intellectual property.

**Examples.**
- L121: "Every product is a mirror of the organization that built it. In 1968, computer scientist Melvin Conway made an observation"
- L082: "When you present brand concepts to a client, you're not asking 'which idea do you like?' You're actually asking 'how much risk are you willing to take?'"
- S084: "The most interesting people, brands, and ideas are walking contradictions. They mix opposites and blend ideas that usually don't go together."
- S078: "People rarely buy stuff in isolation. Every buyer sits inside a few circles of people who shape how they think, what they trust and whether they move. I think of it as four circles:"
- L240: "Synthetic respondents are the new fast food of market research. Quick. Cheap. And you have no idea what's actually in the meat."
- L201: "Companies don't stall because of product. They stall because the story's weak."
- S014: "You can't build a business around expertise anymore. But you can build it around authority. Authority ="

## 14. The honest version

**Job.** "Ha. That's exactly what it really means."

**Mechanism.** Humor through translation: the official language is restated as what everyone knows it means. The reader laughs because it's true and because nobody says it. Shared recognition.

**What it needs.** A ritual or genre of language your audience knows by heart (funding announcements, ads, CEO lines, LinkedIn profiles). Real insider knowledge of what it hides. Restraint: it should still be true, not just mean.

**Shape.** "What X would sound like if people told the truth:" then the translation, often as a list or a script.

**How it fails.**
- The translation is cynical without being accurate.
- Aimed at a specific person instead of a genre.
- Too long; the joke needs to land fast.

**Good for.** Reach and likability. Shows you know the game from inside.

**Examples.**
- L022: "What a Series A fundraising announcement would sound like if people told the truth: 'I am proud to announce that we have sold 20% of our company to XYZ Venture Labs for $30M at a $150M valuation.'"
- L168: "If the world's biggest brands were 100% honest, what would their ads say? 1. Apple: 'You will pay a 40% premium for this phone because of the color of text bubbles.'"
- L030: "The 5-step guide to blending in: 1. Put a fake plant in the background 2. Setup a bookshelf next to it with books you've never read"
- L119: "Bad CEO: 'Why isn't the team delivering?' Good CEO: 'Have I been clear about what I expect?'"
- L207: "This post on X is truthfully funny. Lots of takes just come from podcasts. Value-add is elusive in VC."

## 15. The admission

**Job.** "They're admitting that?"

**Mechanism.** Lowering your own status on purpose buys credibility, because people assume you'd hide it. It also creates a gap: if you're admitting it, something must have come of it. Works best when the admission is specific and a little uncomfortable.

**What it needs.** Something true about you that most people in your position wouldn't say. A reason it matters beyond confession (what it taught, what it proves).

**Shape.** The admission, flat, first. Then why you're telling it.

**How it fails.**
- A humblebrag dressed as an admission ("I'm embarrassed to say I only made $2M").
- Oversharing with no point.
- Admitting a thing nobody would hold against you.

**Good for.** Trust and being seen as a person. Strong for relationships.

**Examples.**
- L203: "I haven't read my emails in 3 years. That's when I hired my first Executive Assistant"
- L031: "I'm actually embarrassed to admit this... I think the advice I'm giving gets mixed up sometimes."
- S041: "In 2021 I'd built a $10MM rapidly growing business and was getting anxiety multiple times a week that would throw off my entire day."
- L164: "Today is the first morning in 1,735 days that I woke up without a WHOOP recovery score telling me whether I was 'green, yellow, or red.'"
- S072: "I learned a difficult lesson after eight years at a national newspaper. I'd said yes to almost everything my bosses asked of me."
- L094: "I'm a CMO of a $100M+ ARR (boostrapped) company. And I have 7 parallel sessions of Claude Code running on my laptop right now. I've never written a line of code in my life."
- L015 (2,903): "Over the weekend, I ran 200 miles around Lake Tahoe, and I didn't learn a damn thing about marketing."

## 16. Do this right now

**Job.** "Okay, I'm doing it. What happens?"

**Mechanism.** An instruction the reader can follow in the next ten seconds turns reading into doing. The reader finds the problem on their own, which is more convincing than being told about it.

**What it needs.** A check or exercise that takes under a minute and reveals something. A reason the result matters.

**Shape.** Imperative. "Go to X. Open Y. Read Z." Then what it means if the result is bad.

**How it fails.**
- The exercise takes too long or needs a tool.
- The result is predictable, so nobody does it.
- The instruction is really just advice ("Start journaling").

**Good for.** Leads. People who run the check and fail it often reach out.

**Examples.**
- L085: "Go to your LinkedIn company page right now. Then open your G2 listing. Then your YouTube profile. Then your homepage. Read the tagline on each one. If all four say different things, LLMs have no idea what you actually do."
- L247: "Fix your LinkedIn in under 20 minutes. All you need is Claude. 1/ Download the profile PDF"
- L175: "The best writing tip I've received since I became a content writer 3.5 months ago was to play a game. Here are the 3 rules. 1/ Before you click the post button, highlight all the verbs in your draft."
- L019: "This sounds lame but hear me out: Whenever I want to make a transformation, I unfollow everyone and only follow the people I wanna be like."

## 17. A bet on the future

**Job.** "What's coming, and are they right?"

**Mechanism.** A specific prediction is a claim the reader can argue with, and the writer is putting reputation on the line. Specificity does the work: a date, a category, a number. "Things will change" is not a bet.

**What it needs.** A thesis you actually hold, with reasoning. Specific enough to be wrong.

**Shape.** The prediction, dated and concrete. Then the reasoning, often from a trend you've watched up close.

**How it fails.**
- Vague enough that it can't be wrong.
- Borrowed from a headline instead of reasoned.
- Hedged into nothing.

**Good for.** Point of view and being referenced later ("I told you so" posts, like L123, are this move cashing in).

**Examples.**
- L113 (3,352): "I'm incredibly bullish on IRL. Intimate dinner parties. Music festivals. Group adventures... The pendulum will swing back to craftsmanship, curation, texture, rituals"
- S059: "I'm incredibly bullish on the unscalable, high-touch, deeply human things AI can never truly replicate."
- L137: "You probably can make $10m+ in 2026 by installing, configuring and optimizing clawdbots for businesses right now."
- L104: "PREDICTION: Gartner says that PR and earned media budgets will DOUBLE by 2027... and the reason why should matter to every marketer"
- L174: "My theory about LinkedIn in 2026 is if you treat it like Twitter in 2021 you will win."
- L021: "Claude Mythos is going to launch to the public this afternoon. To save you the time, let me tell you what is going to happen:"
- L159: "I'm so confident that writing will remain a career-defining skill in the AI era that I'm literally betting my future on it. Here's why."

## 18. The announcement

**Job.** "What are you up to?"

**Mechanism.** News about the writer. It only works on an existing relationship; strangers don't care. The ones that work turn the announcement into a story (why this, why now) or into an invitation.

**What it needs.** Something real to announce. A relationship with the audience already in place. A reason the reader should care beyond politeness.

**Shape.** The news, plainly. Then the why, or the ask.

**How it fails.**
- Announcing to people who don't know you.
- Leading with the ask.
- Vague ("something new is coming").

**Good for.** Converting an existing audience. Not for growing one.

**Examples.**
- L088: "We built the company I wish existed when I was a CMO. TLDR: After two years in stealth, we're officially launching Science x Story"
- L027: "Fuck it, let's announce something big - I've built a marketing course."
- L071: "I just launched JustaDesignList.com. It started in 2023 as a private folder of bookmarks"
- S093: "I want to start a community for people who communicate ideas for a living"
- S060: "I want to assemble a group of surfer entrepreneurs."
- L118: "Well, this is exciting: I'm building something new."
- L122: "I've coached over 3,000+ entrepreneurs and creators. Today, I want to review your posts for free." (announcement sitting on proof)

## Notes from the first read

**Moves stack.** Most strong hooks run two moves at once: proof under a list (S050), a story under a reframe (L121), news under a contrarian take (L211), an admission under then-and-now (L035). In the full sort, each post has a primary move and, where it's clear, a second one.

**What performs in this library.** The biggest posts in the Storied file cluster in four moves: someone notable did something odd (L189, L050, L114), news plus a take (L091, L042, L052, L169), then and now (L070, L035), and put the reader in the room (L008). Proof-then-payoff and the numbered promise get saves more than reactions. The call-out gets comments. This is one library, not a law, but it's a usable prior for the strategy layer.

**Things that look like hooks but aren't moves.** "Unpopular opinion:", "Here's the story:", "Here's why:", "(screenshot this)" are connectors. They attach to a move; they don't do a job on their own. We should keep a short list of these so the AI doesn't mistake them for the move.

**Two open questions.** Whether "the pattern I keep seeing" (8) and "the reframe" (13) should merge: both are a claim from the writer's own thinking, kept apart because the raw material differs (one needs a sample you've observed, the other needs an idea you hold). And whether "the finding" (11) belongs inside "news plus a take" (10): same shape, but one needs data you produced and the other needs an event you didn't.
