## Post

People rarely buy stuff in isolation.

Every buyer sits inside a few circles of people who shape how they think, what they trust and whether they move. I think of it as four circles:

1. People who can say no. Bosses, partners, family, anyone who shares the consequences of the decision. 
2. Peers. Colleagues, friends, Slack groups. 
3. Community voices. People they don’t know but trust: reviews, testimonials, case studies. 
4. Culture. The trends, values and stories that shape how they see the problem in the first place. 

It’s natural to focus on the buyer, and you should.

But talking to the buyer alone isn’t always enough.

How much each circle weighs depends on the decision: who lives with the consequences, how much is at stake, how visible the purchase is and how hard it is to judge the quality before buying.

That’s why a sneaker brand and an enterprise software company deal with very different circles. Someone buying sneakers doesn’t need anyone’s permission, so the first circle barely matters, but peers and culture matter a lot. 

Nobody needed Michael Jordan’s permission to buy a pair of Air Jordans, but a lot of people wanted to be a little more like him.

Someone buying software for a large company is in the opposite situation. They might need sign-off from their boss, finance and IT, and they’ll want to know which similar companies already use it.

Each circle also responds to something different:

* The people who can say no weigh risk, and they usually only hear about you through the buyer. Proof that you’ve done this before, a smaller first step or a clear way out lowers the risk. So does giving the buyer an explanation they can repeat without you there.

* Peers can only pass on what they or someone they know has experienced, so word spreads faster when your clients know each other.

* Community voices carry weight because they have nothing to gain. That means they only work if you have real results and clients willing to describe them in their own words.

* Culture shapes which problems feel important. You can say clearly where you agree with the usual view of the problem and where you don’t, and keep making that case for a long time.

The farther out the circle, the less control you have and the longer it takes to make a difference.

So before you plan how to reach anyone, answer four questions about the decision your buyers make:

1. Who can say no, or shares the consequences?
2. Whose opinion do they care about?
3. Whose experience do they trust?
4. What do they already believe about the problem?

The answers will tell you which circles matter most for your buyers, and where to spend your time.

Maybe not the perfect model, but it’s a good place to start the next time someone tells you they need to think about it.
