## Post

I quit my job as a staff writer for The Observer with no warning after being there for 8 years.
On paper, this was my dream job. It made no sense to leave. Steady salary, the kind of job every young journalist is told to want and hang onto.

But I'd started to notice the gap between the version of my career that looked impressive from the outside and the one that existed in reality.

So in 2016, I did the thing everyone warns you against. I walked away from the security. I went freelance with no guarantee of what came next.
Here's what nobody tells you about quitting something that looks like a great job: the fear isn't really about money, or CV gaps, or what people will think. It's about giving up an identity you've spent years building. I wasn't just leaving a job. I was leaving the story I'd told myself about who I was.

Freelancing gave me the opportunity to rebuild my identity, and work out who I was beneath the newspaper deadlines. I started a podcast about failure in my spare room with no idea if anyone would listen. It's what let me write the books I wanted to write, the freedom to express my own opinions and not cringe as I tick off a checklist of topics I had to ask an unwilling celebrity about at the behest of a stressed editor.

It's how I now have a career that feels like mine. I’m so grateful to the Observer for teaching me many things - but mostly this:

I don't think quitting is always the answer. But I do think we're taught to treat "sticking it out" as automatically virtuous -  and walking away as though it’s automatically a failure. Sometimes it's the opposite. 
For me, the best career decision I ever made wasn't a promotion. It was a resignation letter.
