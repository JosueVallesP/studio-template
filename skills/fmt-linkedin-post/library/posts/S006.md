## Post

When I was a Sales Director at a $100M ARR company, my top AEs had annual quotas of $1.5M. Every seller who hit (or exceeded) it, mastered 4 key elements of the sales process:


1. Their demos offered value like a $1,000/hr consultant

Poor AE demos leave the buyer with the mental load, forcing them to figure out why do anything at all, why now, and why this vendor. These demos typically focus on what their company does, what X feature does, etc.

Top AE demos make buyers feel like THEY are the only thing that matters. No "This feature does X" or "Our ROI is Y". They are 100% focused on the buyer's problems and how industry peers have used their product to solve them.


2. Their follow-ups feel like they’re offering 'project management as a service'

Poor AE follow-ups push the next step. All they see is their dead pipeline, so they’ll do anything to just get an answer.

Top AEs split their pipeline into two buckets:

A) The deals they’re working WITH buyers, towards a mutually agreed plan
B) The deals they’re not sure about

Group A is in project management mode. There’s a work-back plan built from the value delivery date which was built with their champion. They revisit it at every meeting.

Group B is in value management mode. They re-listen to their calls, read their notes, and tailor their actions. Their goal is to hit a nerve by making moves (not follow-ups) that uncover value or find a potential champion.


3. Their multithreading builds consensus and champion support

Poor AE multithreading is all about the seller getting as high up as possible to speak with execs. They do it behind the champion’s back and hurt the deal.

Top AEs understand that BTL of power stakeholders are as important as ATL. They understand that decisions are made in committees and that strong mid-level champions and even end-users can influence decisions.


4. Their resource-sharing is an orchestrated decision-support system

Poor AE resource sharing is all about them. Getting THEIR best white papers, or case studies in front of prospects. A spray-and-pray of assets, with little or no thought about how they tie into the buyer’s journey.

Top AEs put a lot of thought into what, when, how, and who they share with:

- ‘What we’ve heard' recap in email 1 that will evolve into a business case
- A document outlining the POC scope shared with the POC team
- Requirement list builder template shared early in the vendor comparison
- Tailored summary deck shared with their champion ahead of the exec call


TAKEAWAY:

The top 1% of AEs are not selling.

They are expert guides or advisors.

They offer 'Buying Process As A Service’.

They become an extension of the buying team.

They facilitate the decision-making process.
