## Post

Nike's Norway World Cup font got banned by FIFA. Then it got better.
  
In 2024, Nike unveiled a typeface for the Norwegian national team called Taakeferd, meaning "journey through the fog." It was built on Elder Futhark, the oldest Norse runic alphabet, all sharp angles and two-tone lettering. It looked incredible. It also got rejected.
  
FIFA flagged two problems. The two-tone numbers broke kit regulations outright. And the runic forms sat close to the line on legibility, since only Latin-derived alphabets are technically allowed on World Cup jerseys.
  
Most teams would have scrapped the concept and gone generic. Nike's Vancouver studio, led by creative director Luis Callegari, didn't. They kept the geometry and the Viking soul of the design, stripped away everything that made it hard to read, and rebuilt it as a cleaner, more minimal version of itself.
  
That's the version on the pitch now. Angular. Distinctly Norwegian. Legible from the stands and on broadcast. The name Braut Haaland in that font is instantly recognisable, and it still carries the weight of a thousand years of history in every letterform.
  
The lesson for anyone who designs identities for a living: a rejected concept usually isn't a bad concept. It's an unfinished one. The idea was right from day one. What was missing was the discipline to make it work within real constraints, not around them.
  
Constraints aren't the enemy of a strong identity. They're what force it to become one.
