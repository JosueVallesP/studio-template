## Post

Your personal brand is not your job title. It's not a list of skills, either. And it's clearly not a "positioning statement." 

A lot of people approach personal branding like product branding. It's static, formulaic, and lacks the human element that makes people interesting. 

But you're a person, not a product.

And that means your personal brand should be dynamic, authentic, and ever-evolving…just like you are.

So instead of crafting a sterile "positioning statement", try this:

1. Acknowledge your past (the "Then")
2. Describe your present (the "Now")
3. Paint a picture of your desired future (the "Future")

It's easy to get stuck in one of them.

Some people write almost only in the past tense. Case studies, credentials, the big clients they worked with ten years ago. It builds credibility, but after a while it reads like a resume.

Some write almost only in the present. Tips, methods, what they're doing this week. It's useful, but there's no story, so there's nothing to follow.

And a few write almost only about the future. Big visions, where the industry is going, what they're about to build. It can be exciting, but without the past and present behind it, it's hard to believe.

So I'd write about all three tenses, and spend most of your time where they overlap.

The overlap between your past and present is where authority comes from. It's how something that happened years ago shaped the way you work today. The past explains the present, and the reader understands why you're good at what you do.

The overlap between your present and future is where connection comes from. It's what you're working on now and where it's heading. People like to join something that's moving, and they like to feel they were there early.

The same topics can show up in every tense, which makes it easier than it sounds. 

Take people, for example. 

In the past tense, you can write about the mentors who shaped you. In the present, about the people you work with now. In the future, about the people you'd love to work with someday. You can do the same with tools, with how you work, with your projects and with what you believe.

You don't need to plan this perfectly. 

Next time you write something, just notice which tense you're in, and ask yourself whether the post would be stronger if it reached a little into the past or a little into the future.
