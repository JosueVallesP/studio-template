## Post

Every year for 60 years, Warren Buffett sat down and wrote a letter explaining Berkshire Hathaway to its shareholders. He started nearly every draft with these exact words:

"Dear Doris and Bertie."

Doris and Bertie were his two sisters. 

Both were intelligent but had no background in finance. 

Before publishing, he'd remove the greeting...but the constraint stayed. Every sentence had to be clear enough for them to understand.

During that time, Berkshire went from a failing textile mill to a trillion-dollar conglomerate. And Buffett explained every step of it in plain English. Here are the rules he followed:

1. Write with a specific person in mind. Not an audience or a demographic. ONE person. Buffett said that the moment you write for "shareholders" in the abstract, you start writing to impress instead of inform.

2. Assume your reader has been away for a year. Don't assume they remember what you said last time. Don't assume they know the context. Give them everything they need to understand the situation from scratch.

3. Eliminate jargon. Buffett wrote "far worse" instead of "significantly worse." "About 750 locations" instead of "approximately 750 locations." Every formal word replaced with a conversational one.

4. Use analogies instead of explanations. When Buffett needed to explain market volatility, he didn't use financial terminology. He told readers to imagine a farmer whose neighbor shows up every day offering to buy or sell his farm at a different price...sometimes euphoric, sometimes depressed.

5. Have a sincere desire to inform. His exact words: "To succeed, I don't need to be Shakespeare; I must, though, have a sincere desire to inform." He believed that if you start fooling your readers, you'll soon believe your own nonsense and start fooling yourself.

6. Frame everything from the reader's perspective. Buffett wrote about "what you own," not "what I manage." Every sentence was structured around what the reader needed to know, not what the writer wanted to say.

7. If you're confused by your own writing, so is your reader. Buffett treated unclear writing as a sign of unclear thinking. If he couldn't explain something simply, he assumed he didn't understand it well enough yet.

One of the biggest mistakes people make when writing is trying to sound smart, or credible, or authoritative. Warren Buffett is undeniably one of the sharpest financial minds alive...and he still wrote every letter like he was talking to his sisters. 

Maybe there's a lesson there 🤷‍♂️
