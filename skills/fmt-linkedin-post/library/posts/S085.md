## Post

Great content doesn't start by opening Google Docs, Figma or Claude.

Very often, I talk with potential clients who want to start with our content services, but they haven't done any strategy work.

When we get on a call, it's clear they aren't short on ideas. They have 15 or 20 years of work behind them and sometimes a book that's half written. Many of them sell 3 or 4 different things.

That's the problem.

When you know a lot, every idea feels important, so you don't know where to start.

Most LinkedIn experts argue that you should focus on "positioning" or "content pillars" or "a niche" or "hooks." And there's definitely a place for that. But I think you should start way way earlier, by looking at everything you already know and how it fits together.

To fix it, you must see your work as something fractal.

Imagine your work, your career, is a tree.

Over the years, you develop new branches.

Branches are dense ideas, methods and ways of thinking. You can't explain them in a single post or video because they need time. So a branch has to become a book, a series of essays, a series of videos or a series of interviews.

Leaves, on the other hand, belong to a specific branch, but you can explain each one on its own, in a single piece.

Your work becomes fractal: one tree, many branches and many leaves.

This means you don't have to say everything at once.

You choose one branch and work on it for a few months. You write one long piece that explains the full idea, then you write short posts that each explain one part of it. When you finish, you move to the next branch.

Every branch grows from the same trunk.

The trunk is your core concept: the one idea that connects everything you do. If you skip this step, each branch points in a different direction, and readers can't tell what your work is about.

Now, publishing a lot doesn't make money by itself.

You can publish for years, have people tell you they love your posts, and still get almost all of your clients through referrals.

A body of work produces commercial leverage when every branch leads to a problem you solve and a way to work with you.

So before you start a new branch, make sure it connects to three things:

1. A pain point. The problem this idea helps someone solve.

2. Your point of view. What you believe about that problem that other people in your field don't.

3. An offer. What someone can pay you to do about that problem.

This also tells you where to start.

If you have ten ideas and only three of them lead to something you sell, start with those three.

NOTE:

This process won't work for most people.

If you run a B2B SaaS or an e-commerce brand or you're early in your career, this is probably not the right framework for you, but if you have years of experience, your own way of doing things, and more ideas than you know where to put, this will help you turn all of it into a body of work people can follow and hire you for.
