## Post

A few days into the first lockdown, a friendship ended.

We'd met years before in our early 20s and she was infectiously fun. I loved being around her. Though, looking back, I realise I had spent a lot of those nights performing an enjoyment I didn't quite feel, convinced that if I wasn't having the kind of fun she was having, the fault must be mine.

For years, that imbalance didn't matter. Then, in my 30s, I went through fertility treatment. Then a miscarriage. Then a divorce. And the version of fun we'd always shared - sushi, vodka, late nights out - stopped being a comfort and started being something I used to avoid feeling the things I actually felt.

She felt the shift and didn’t like it. I could understand why: the truth was, I wasn’t as much ‘fun’ as I’d used to be because I was going through something really difficult. But I was too scared to talk about it with her. And she didn’t bring it up with me either. 

We both changed. And although there wasn't much room left in our friendship for each other, neither of us acknowledged this outwardly.

We ended up letting each other go. There was no explicit conversation. No fanfare. Just a slow dwindling of comms and a silent acknowledgment that our lives were in different phases. Maybe, somewhere down the line, we’d find each other again.

Here's what I've come to believe: we have an entire vocabulary for romantic breakups. Grief, closure, moving on, that time-honoured phrase ‘It’s not you, it’s me’ - all socially sanctioned. We have almost none of that for friendships ending. A friendship quietly falling away still comes wrapped in shame even if it’s the right thing for both parties. Which is why we need to talk more about friendship and give this precious platonic love its own language.
