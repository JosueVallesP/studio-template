## Post

In 2021, Joanna Griffiths raised $53 million while pregnant with twins. Then she started rejecting investors who questioned her pregnancy. Here's the story:

Griffiths is the founder of Knix, a Toronto underwear brand she'd been building since 2013. She went out to raise while heavily pregnant. And in some of those rooms, the investors couldn't help themselves.

One group asked, "who wants to be the last money in before she goes off and has her twins?"

She didn't argue or try to win them over. 

She told her bank to disqualify them from the round.

She closed the round on a Friday afternoon, her last day before leave, three days before the girls were born. A year later, Knix sold to Essity for about $320 million, one of the largest exits ever by a Canadian female founder. Griffiths kept a fifth of the company and stayed on to run it.

The investors who flinched at a pregnancy passed on that.

Here's what I take from it:

When you raise money, you're not collecting cash. 

You're choosing partners for the next seven to ten years and handing them a seat at your table. How an investor behaves when something about you is inconvenient for them is the most honest data you'll ever get about who they are.

Griffiths read that data and acted on it while eight months pregnant and mid-raise. 

Most founders don't read it at all until they're already stuck with the wrong people on their board.

The term sheet runs both ways. 

You're allowed to use it.

What would you have done in that room?
