# Newsjacking: exemplars

> Reference for the `newsjacking` type (`../newsjacking.md`). Method by Josué Valles. The posts quoted here are other people's, kept as they were published: learn the move, never copy the wording or the tics (dashes, stacked fragments, labelled takeaways). Every number, name and example in a post the studio writes comes from the person's sources or is a real case marked `borrowed: verify`; the figures in this file show the level of detail, never material to reuse.


Twelve complete posts from the 358-post library. Every one is a keeper.

Each entry opens with the **news/read split** and the **crossing sentence**, the exact point where reporting stops and the writer's contribution begins. That number is the most useful single fact about any post in this archetype, and the split ranges from 6% to 74% across the corpus.

Read the entries for your construction before drafting.

Three are worth reading as a set: **post 173** revises its own prior in public, **post 123** vindicates a prior it never stated, and **post 66** places its credential after the evidence rather than before it. Together they define the whole range of the prior-position move.

Two more pair on exposure: **posts 52 and 374** are the only two in the corpus whose conclusion depends on the facts being exactly right, and they are consequently the two that cannot be walked back.

# Character Verdict

## Post 52: Maximum exposure, minimum hedging, and the pronoun architecture that makes it shareable

**216 words**

**News share: ~50%.** Crossing sentence: *"They told everyone to work from home today - not out of consideration, but so there'd be no scene at the office when the emails landed."*

**Why it's here.** The clearest demonstration of *report the gap, not the event*. It's 216 words about a layoff most readers had already seen the headline for, and it spends almost none of them on the headline. It spends them on texture nobody else reported, *4am emails*, *"From Singapore to the UK to the US, employees became ex-employees before the sun came up,"* the work-from-home instruction, and the internal programme name. **It never explains who Meta is.**

**The verdict is three words and it is fully earned.** *"It's a choice."* Two imported facts about the same actor pay for it: the surveillance programme, and *"Meta just posted record profits this quarter."* The post states its own precondition out loud: *"This isn't some distressed company making hard choices."* Without an alternative-proving fact you're asserting bad faith rather than demonstrating a choice.

**Standing with no "I" anywhere.** There is no first person in the post. The credential is knowing things the reader doesn't, and **naming an internal programme substitutes for a biography.**

**Watch the pronouns.** *They* for Meta throughout; *you* reserved entirely for the laid-off. The close is the only direct address to a non-participant reader in the corpus: *"If you were impacted today, please know this is not on you. You were following the rules, they just forgot to mention they were changing them."* **That's what converts a takedown into a consolation, and what makes an angry post shareable by the people it's about.**

**Where it's exposed, and it's the most exposed post here.** Five instances of *today* weld it to one calendar day, unresharable, dead by Wednesday. The motive attribution (*"not out of consideration, but so there'd be no scene"*) is unfalsifiable, unsourced, and load-bearing. And no link anywhere, in the post that needs one most. This is a deliberate trade, but it should be a decision rather than an accident.

```
BREAKING: Meta laid off 8,000 people today via 4am emails.

From Singapore to the UK to the US, employees became ex-employees before the sun came up.

And Meta made sure of it. They told everyone to work from home today - not out of consideration, but so there’d be no scene at the office when the emails landed.

The same company that has been tracking employees’ mouse clicks, keystrokes, and screen activity - under a program called the “Model Capability Initiative” - to train AI to replicate how humans work. Today, 8,000 of those humans got a 4am email.

If you were impacted today, please know this is not on you. You were following the rules, they just forgot to mention they were changing them.

## Post 374: Praise, and why the disclaimer comes first

**350 words**

**News share: ~70%**: the highest in the corpus, and every word of it is load-bearing. Crossing sentence: *"I've spent twenty years watching what happens when people build something they're proud of and then get told the price of keeping it is to give up the thing that made it worth building."*

**Why the reporting runs so long.** The claim is about a person's character, and **character claims are only supportable as sequences.** Five beats, all required: the contract, the refusal (two named lines), the threats (*"They threatened to designate Anthropic a supply chain risk. They threatened to invoke the Defence Production Act."*), the cost (*"He lost the contract."*), and the aftermath. Remove the threats and the refusal is cheap. Remove the cost and it isn't a sacrifice. Remove the aftermath and there's no reversal.

**The disclaimer of relationship is the first move**: *"I don't know him personally."* It does two jobs, kills the "you're networking in public" reading, and functions as an epistemic hedge announcing the whole account is second-hand. **Attack requires proximity disclosure; praise requires distance disclosure.** Each pre-empts the exact charge its stance invites.

**The verdict is withheld for 300 words** and arrives as the conclusion of an argument rather than an opinion about a person: *"Most people take the deal. Most people convince themselves it's the pragmatic thing to do."*

**Note the one hedge, and where it sits.** *"Uninstalls of competing products **reportedly** jumped nearly 300%."* That is the softest claim in the post and it is the only one carrying a hedge, correctly triaged. Contrast *"Employees at Amazon, Meta, and OpenAI started writing open letters"*, which is unhedged, unsourced, and about identifiable employers.

**It also explains who its subject is**: *"Amodei is the CEO and co-founder of Anthropic, the company behind Claude"*, where post 52 doesn't explain Meta. That's a decision about the audience's fluency, and it's the right one for a story this specific.

**And it converts lateness into deliberation:** *"I've been thinking about him all week."* The delay becomes the reason the post exists.

```
Dario Amodei built one of the world's most capable AI systems. I don't know him personally. But I've been thinking about him all week, because he just did something I haven't seen a founder do in a LONG time:

He said NO to the most powerful customer in the world.

CONTEXT:

Amodei is the CEO and co-founder of Anthropic, the company behind Claude.

The US government had a massive contract with Anthropic to use Claude across all federal agencies. 

But when Amodei looked at what that actually meant in practice, he realised it would give every department (including the CIA, the FBI, and the Department of Defence) completely unrestricted access to the technology.

So he drew two lines.

1. You cannot use this for mass surveillance of your own citizens. 
2. You cannot use this in fully autonomous weapons. 

The government's response was about as aggressive as it gets. 

They threatened to designate Anthropic a supply chain risk. They threatened to invoke the Defence Production Act. They threatened to strip Claude from every government system. 

Amodei called the threats "inherently contradictory"...one labels his company a national security risk, while the other implies his technology is so essential to national security that the government needs to force him to hand it over.

He said: "We cannot in good conscience accede to their request."

So they blacklisted him. 

He lost the contract. 

And then something quite remarkable happened.

Claude became the number one app in the US App Store, overtaking ChatGPT. Downloads broke every record Anthropic had ever set. Uninstalls of competing products reportedly jumped nearly 300%.

I've spent twenty years watching what happens when people build something they're proud of and then get told the price of keeping it is to give up the thing that made it worth building. 

Most people take the deal. 

Most people convince themselves it's the pragmatic thing to do.

Dario Amodei didn't. 

And I find that genuinely admirable.

Well done, Dario. 

Genuinely, well done.
```

# Counter-Evidence

## Post 34: The sharpest attack in the corpus, aimed at someone who is never named

**246 words**

**News share: ~26%.** Crossing sentence: *"While I appreciate a good work ethic, I don't mean this hyperbolically when I say I think this kind of rhetoric will kill people."*

**Why it's here.** It identifies without naming: *"There's an interview going viral with the CEO of a young startup that just raised $106M at a $2.6B valuation."* Two numbers and a source link make him trivially identifiable; withholding the name means **he is not tagged, not notified, and not made the subject.** This is the move for when the person is an instance of a type and your argument is about the type.

**The harm claim is about the speech act, not the man.** *Rhetoric*, three times: *"this kind of rhetoric will kill people"*, *"this sort of rhetoric is irresponsible and dangerous."*

**Two concessions, and they're structural.** *"While I appreciate a good work ethic"* and *"I get that startups call for a certain level of intensity that might not exist in your average day job."* Concession is what makes numbers land as adjudication rather than cherry-picking.

**Two hyperlinked sources**: one for the interview, one for the productivity research. This is the best-sourced post in the corpus and not coincidentally the most aggressive.

**The generalisation carries a noun across the bridge:** *"$2.6B valuation"* appears verbatim in both the report and the claim, *"When you have a platform and a $2.6B valuation behind you, people listen."*

**Where the armour slips.** *"This startup is less than 2 years old and this founder has no clue what produces long term success."* Direct, personal, unhedged. That's the sentence that generates the reply-guys, and it's the one line in the post that doesn't follow its own rules.

**The close is a bet, not a verdict:** *"Let's all sleep in a bed and check back in 8 years."* And note the decay hack in the opening, *"going viral"*, present progressive, asserts currency while pinning no date.

<summary><b>Full post</b></summary>

```
There's an interview going viral with the CEO of a young startup that just raised $106M at a $2.6B valuation. The CEO brags about working 7 days a week, sleeping in the office, and sleeping for only a few hours each night. (source: https://lnkd.in/gJhKFxZY)

His take: "Whatever you can get done in 5 days, I promise you can get more done in 6 or 7." While I appreciate a good work ethic, I don't mean this hyperbolically when I say I think this kind of rhetoric will kill people. 

Chronic sleep deprivation is linked to heart disease, cancer, dementia, and a host of other issues. Not to mention that if you are living in the office 24/7, you're probably not working out or eating a healthy diet, increasing your risk for all sorts of diseases that will reduce your lifespan.

And to what end? Studies show that productivity drops sharply after 50 hours a week (source: https://lnkd.in/gSdNTad3). The people grinding for 70 hours are doing just as much as the people working 55.

I get that startups call for a certain level of intensity that might not exist in your average day job. But this sort of rhetoric is irresponsible and dangerous. When you have a platform and a $2.6B valuation behind you, people listen. This startup is less than 2 years old and this founder has no clue what produces long term success. Let's all sleep in a bed and check back in 8 years.

## Post 236: The steelman, and what a number is actually for

**259 words**

**News share: ~36%.** Crossing sentence: *"Even the companies building this tech are hitting the same wall."*

**Why the reporting is dense.** The claim is an accusation, a named publisher passed off computer output as public opinion, so it needs the source (*"The NYT just called"*), the mechanism defined (*"The term is 'silicon sampling', using LLMs to simulate survey responses instead of asking real people"*), the stakes, and a steelman.

**The error metric is the steelman, and this is the entry's real lesson.** *"Emporia Research spent 8 months building and validating B2B digital twins. Their results were genuinely strong (0.44 mean absolute error on a 5-point scale, beating every baseline they tested)."*

Almost no reader can evaluate 0.44 MAE on a 5-point scale. **Its job is not to inform. Its job is to prove the writer isn't dismissing the technology out of ignorance**, and to set up the far more damaging admission from a *friendly* witness: *"You cannot prove synthetic responses are right."* The concession is load-bearing; the metric is what makes the concession credible.

**The generalisation is a deduction, not a slogan.** *"the only way to check is to ask the real people, which defeats the purpose of the simulation"* is what earns *"If you're simulating the answer, you're not doing research. You're writing fiction."* The specific logical trap is on the page, so the closing line is derived rather than asserted.

**Standing by possession of evidence the reader can't get**, and note it's third person throughout. The writer never says "we", so the case reads as independent corroboration rather than self-promotion. A quieter second move grades the source as a peer: *"Here's what the NYT nailed."*

**Heavily protected by attribution.** Nearly every claim is routed through a source. The remaining exposure is one unsourced round number: *"Hundreds of millions in VC funding behind this."*

<details open>
<summary><b>Full post</b></summary>

```
The NYT just called synthetic research what it is: made up.
Yesterday's piece exposed Axios for citing "findings" from an AI startup called Aaru. No humans were surveyed. The opinions were computer-generated. And they were presented as public opinion.
The term is "silicon sampling", using LLMs to simulate survey responses instead of asking real people.
Hundreds of millions in VC funding behind this.
Here's what the NYT nailed: the biases in traditional polling get amplified by silicon sampling. The further you get from real people, the more results reflect the model's assumptions, not reality.
Even the companies building this tech are hitting the same wall. Emporia Research spent 8 months building and validating B2B digital twins. Their results were genuinely strong (0.44 mean absolute error on a 5-point scale, beating every baseline they tested).
But their honest conclusion? 
"You cannot prove synthetic responses are right." 
When you show a client AI-generated survey data, the inevitable question is: is this real? How do we know this reflects what real people would say? And the only way to check is to ask the real people — which defeats the purpose of the simulation.
The market agreed. Buyers were enthusiastic about the concept but hesitated at the point of commitment. Emporia found traction only in augmentation (pre-screening before human testing). Not replacement.
The value of research is listening to real people and getting confidence in decision making. Getting high-fidelity information from real people about what they actually think, right now.
If you're simulating the answer, you're not doing research. You're writing fiction.
```

</details>

## Post 123: ANTI-PATTERN, where the evidence and the claim have different subjects

**209 words**

**News share: ~55%**: a 114-word uninterrupted block quote. Crossing sentence: *"I know, I know some will still answer with 'But, but, but...' until the end of time…"*

**Why it's here.** It's the corpus's clearest instance of two failure tells at once, in a post that still performed at 1,163 reactions. Both are worth being able to see in your own draft.

**Failure tell 1: the evidence and the claim have different subjects.** The study reports workload intensification. The generalisation is about the psychology of AI advocates: *"Because for some, it is easier to find a new excuse than to acknowledge the huge pile of limitations, pushback against, and impossibilities of 'AI.'"*

The diagnostic is exact: **the 73 words after the block quote could be pasted under any negative AI study without alteration.** That is the swap test failing.

**Failure tell 2: a vindication with no prior attached to it.** *"I told you 2026 will be the year of 'I told you so.'"* No prior claim is named. Nothing falsifiable was ever put on record, so the vindication has nothing to attach to, the prior position *is* the gloat.

Compare post 173 below, which names a year, an employer and an estimate it got wrong. **The rule: the prior must be more specific than the vindication. Reverse that and it reads as gloating.**

**And the aim.** The vindication is directed at people: *"Very probably because of their not understanding it or how it actually works in the first place. Or because of their inability to give up on BS."* **Direct a vindication at a structure and the reader can join you. Direct it at a class of people and the reader has to check which side they're on.**

**What it does well, and it's worth keeping.** The block quote is uninterrupted and untrimmed, the source argues on its own. That's the Counter-Evidence requirement, met properly. The post is factually safe; the exposure is entirely in the framing.

<summary><b>Full post</b></summary>

```
I told you 2026 will be the year of "I told you so." Here is more about the so-called "benefits" of "AI":

"One of the promises of AI is that it can reduce workloads so employees can focus more on higher-value and more engaging tasks. But according to new research, AI tools don’t reduce work, they consistently intensify it: In the study, employees worked at a faster pace, took on a broader scope of tasks, and extended work into more hours of the day, often without being asked to do so. Because for some, it is easier to find a new excuse than to acknowledge the huge pile of limitations, pushback against, and impossibilities of "AI." Very probably because of their not understanding it or how it actually works in the first place. Or because of their inability to give up on BS. Or both. 🤷‍♀️

# Trajectory Placement

## Post 173: Nine words of news funding 275 words of model

**292 words**

**News share: ~6%**: the lowest reporting share of any substantive post here. Crossing sentence: *"That distinction matters more than most people realize."*

**Why it's here.** It is the model for handling news you're late to, and for a hook that outlives its event.

**The actual news is one sentence and it's demoted to paragraph two:** *"McKinsey just hired Monica Khan, a creator economy advisor."* The hook is a translation of that hire into a scene, *"Fortune 500 CEOs are now paying McKinsey to explain why a 24-year-old with a ring light is eating their lunch."* **The hire being three weeks old costs nothing, because the reader never met it as breaking news.**

**Trajectory Placement needs three positions, not two.** *"Every disruption follows the same arc: Denial / Consultants / Panic acquisitions / Irrelevance"*, then the item is located on it: *"McKinsey hiring creator expertise tells me we're firmly in stage two."* Note *tells me*, the inference is explicitly marked as inference.

**The post does not judge McKinsey.** The hire is a thermometer. That's what separates this construction from a Character Verdict.

**The strongest standing move in the corpus, and it does double duty:** *"I've seen this pattern before. At Discovery, we brought in digital consultants around 2010. By 2015, every major firm had a 'digital transformation' practice."* Two dates, one employer, one verifiable industry fact, it licenses the writer *and* supplies the model's evidence base.

**And it revises its own prior in public:** *"But digital disruption unfolded over a decade. Creator disruption however is happening in quarters."* The writer's earlier model was wrong about the speed. **That single *But* is what converts prophecy into thinking**, and it's the difference between this post and post 123.

**Where it overclaims:** *"Every disruption follows the same arc"* is funded by exactly one prior cycle. One precedent offered as a universal.

**The generalisation carries nouns across:** *"seven-figure consulting fees"* → *"a 200-slide deck"* → *"won't wait for a recommendation deck."*

```
Fortune 500 CEOs are now paying McKinsey to explain why a 24-year-old with a ring light is eating their lunch.

McKinsey just hired Monica Khan, a creator economy advisor. Not a social media consultant. Not an influencer specialist. That distinction matters more than most people realize.

These CEOs aren't paying seven-figure consulting fees to learn how to "do TikTok." They're asking a far more existential question. What do we do when creator-powered companies start eating our category from the inside?

This isn't a marketing problem. It's structural. Creators are becoming brand, distribution, product insight, and customer trust, all at once. And they're doing it faster than legacy organizations can react.

This isn't validation that creators have "arrived." It's confirmation that many traditional models are already late. The real question for leadership teams isn't whether to work with creators. It's whether you're willing to let them help redesign how your business actually works.

Is your company still treating creators as a marketing line item? Or are they in the room when strategy gets made?

# Class Escalation

## Post 66: The strongest generalisation in the corpus, and why

**227 words**

**News share: ~31%.** Crossing sentence: *"If you've ever envied a creator's follower count… now you know."*

**Why it's here.** The pivot does explicit set-theory work and it requires no analogy:

> *"This isn't a Kylie Jenner problem. This is an 'everyone who built their business on a platform they don't own' problem."*

**The mechanism is literally identical for the reader**: same company, same policy, same button. That's the precondition: not "similar risk," but the same one. If the class you name at the pivot is no larger than the victim's class ("this isn't a Kylie problem, it's an influencer problem"), the reader isn't in it and the construction fails.

**The duration credential sits *after* the evidence:** *"I've been saying the same thing for 20 years, and I'll say it again today: Your list is your business."* Unverifiable, but positioned so it reads as confirmation of something the reader has just been shown rather than a demand to be believed on trust. **Move that sentence to the top and it becomes a boast.**

**The reader is absolved rather than indicted.** *"If you've ever envied a creator's follower count… now you know."* The reader who was wrong is treated as a fellow mark, not a defeated opponent.

**Well hedged for a fast-moving platform story.** *"Meta executed what's being called the 'Great Purge of 2026'"*, the attributive *what's being called* distances the writer from a coinage he can't own. *"Some lost millions. Many lost more than they'd want to admit."*, vague quantifiers, unfalsifiable.

**And it is claim-split.** The hard number is at the top; the payload, *"Your list is your business. Everything else is rented."*, is date-free. **If the follower figure turns out to be 12 million rather than 15, the post is untouched.** That is the structural hedge that matters more than any wording.

<details open>
<summary><b>Full post</b></summary>

```
Kylie Jenner lost 15 million Instagram followers yesterday.

Not in a year. Not in a month.

In a few hours.

Meta executed what's being called the "Great Purge of 2026" — a platform-wide sweep that wiped bots, inactive accounts, and click-farm followers off Instagram overnight.

Kylie wasn't alone. Cristiano Ronaldo, Taylor Swift, Selena Gomez, BLACKPINK, BTS — all hit. Some lost millions. Many lost more than they'd want to admit.

If you've ever envied a creator's follower count… now you know.

A lot of those numbers were never real.

But here's the part nobody is saying out loud:
This isn't a Kylie Jenner problem. This is an "everyone who built their business on a platform they don't own" problem.

Instagram giveth. Instagram taketh away.

Same with TikTok. Same with YouTube. Same with X.

I've been saying the same thing for 20 years, and I'll say it again today:
Your list is your business.

Everything else is rented.

The day Meta decides your account isn't authentic enough… the day the algorithm changes… the day a bot purge runs… all those followers, all that reach, all that "leverage" can disappear in a few hours.

But your email list? Your phone numbers? Your direct relationships?

Nobody can take those from you.

Build your list before you need it.

What's one thing you've built that nobody can take away from you?

# The Retrieval

## Post 169: 83 words, no analysis, and the whole payload is one 1981 fact

**83 words**

**News share: ~18%.** Crossing sentence: *"Was a big source of inspiration to me back in the day."*

**Why it's here.** The writer adds no analysis whatsoever: no claim about media, streaming, or attention, and the post still lands, because the retrieved fact does everything:

> *"Ironic fun fact: MTV launched in 1981 with 'Video Killed the Radio Star' by The Buggles. That was basically their mission statement from day one."*
>
> *"And now a couple decades later, the internet/social media killed the video star."*

The close is a substitution cipher on a song title. **The reader performs the extrapolation; the writer never says what it means for them.** That works at 83 words and would collapse at 250.

**The precondition is strict:** does the subject's own documented origin contain a specific artefact, a slogan, a first product, a founding line, that, read today, describes how it ended? **If the origin fact needs a sentence of explanation to become ironic, this construction is unavailable**, and no amount of writing fixes it. The tell is a draft containing the words "which is ironic because."

**Standing matched exactly to the claim.** *"The music, videos, the design and branding played a big part in my early days as a designer."* Formed-by-the-subject, and the post claims no expertise in media economics and makes no economic claim.

**The clock is unusually generous** because the news is an announcement of a *future* event, so the window runs from announcement to closure. And the durable half, a 1981 launch and a song title, can't move at all.

## Post 211: Borrowing someone else's neck

**175 words**

**News share: ~74%**: the highest in the corpus, and 99 of the 175 words are two Cuban quotes.

**Why the reporting runs so long:** the meaning lives in the exact wording. An investor's stated thesis can't be paraphrased without destroying the evidence.

**The load-bearing phrase is the bridge:** *"This comes a few months after his prediction on the resurgence of live events."* That single clause converts a transaction into evidence of a worldview. **A prediction alone is not news; a prediction plus a later action by the same person that executes it is a post.**

**The risk is fully transferred.** The falsifiable claim is Cuban's, in quotation marks, *"Within the next 3 years, there will be so much AI…"* Three years, a named mechanism, a testable outcome. If it's wrong, Cuban is wrong. The writer's own assertions are deliberately unfalsifiable.

**Borrowed prediction buys credibility at zero downside: and caps the upside symmetrically.** The writer patches that by supplying the reading: *"This is the contrarian play: the more AI we get, the more we will crave human connection and *real* things."* and *"Everyone thinks AI replaces human connection. Cuban thinks it creates more demand for it."*

**The weakest joint is the final line:** *"Nothing will replace the human experience of being with others in person."* One investment plus one prediction does not support "nothing will replace." **It survives only because the arguable version is Cuban's and the flat version is the writer's**, the reader has already accepted the argument before the overreach arrives. That's a real technique and also a real risk.

**Also worth noting:** *"a few months after"* is a retrospective clock. It buys narrative structure instead of urgency, which is what a late writer uses.

**And the retrospective clock plus a backward-looking construction is also what makes this post durable for weeks**, the hook is a contrast structure, not a date.

<details open>
<summary><b>Full post</b></summary>

```
While everyone's building AI companies, Mark Cuban just invested 7-figures in... an events company.

His thesis:

"It's time we all got off our asses, left the house and had fun. Alex and Ethan know how to create amazing memories and experiences that people plan their weeks around. In an AI world, what you do is far more important than what you prompt."

This comes a few months after his prediction on the resurgence of live events:

"Within the next 3 years, there will be so much AI—in particular AI video—people won't know if what they see or hear is real. Which will lead to an explosion of face-to-face engagement, events and jobs. Those that were in the office will be in the field. Call it the Milli Vanilli effect."

This is the contrarian play: the more AI we get, the more we will crave human connection and *real* things.

Everyone thinks AI replaces human connection. Cuban thinks it creates more demand for it.

Nothing will replace the human experience of being with others in person.
```

</details>

# The specialised cases

## Post 21: Predicting the reactions, not the event

**172 words**

**News share: ~6%.** There is no event yet: the post is 162 words of forecast about other people's forecasts.

**Why it's here.** It is the corpus's only pre-event post and its risk profile is worth understanding exactly.

**The central trick: it does not predict the thing. It predicts the reactions to the thing.** Predicting a model's benchmark performance is falsifiable and dangerous. Predicting that bulls will be bullish is structurally guaranteed. **Maximum apparent nerve, minimum actual exposure.**

Its one genuinely specific line, *"the PDF they made 5 minutes ago"*, is satire, so it cannot be checked and cannot be wrong. **Specificity is spent where verification can't reach.**

***"The reality is more boring."*** does four jobs at once:

1. **Positioning.** Three tribes have just been named; this line places him outside all three. The taxonomy exists to create an empty fourth slot, which he then occupies. That's the actual payload.
2. **Hedge.** *"more capable, especially at coding, and also more expensive to use"* is the base rate for every frontier release, and *"longer than most people think"* has no date and no referent for "most people," so it can never be scored.
3. **Inoculation.** "Boring" pre-empts the charge that mocking hype on launch day is itself hype.
4. **Stake-capping.** *"Nothing to see here, back to work 🫡"* sets the wager low.

**Standing is demonstrated, not claimed.** Zero credential sentences; the right to close is earned by command of the discourse, *"a scam that has worse performance and burns more tokens."* Nobody outside the field writes that line.

**The hard timing gate.** *"this afternoon."* Publish at 6pm and the post is worthless. And even in the success case it becomes either obvious or wrong by the next morning, never impressive. **This construction trades all shelf life for one afternoon of being first.**

## Post 268: Criticising a friend, by name, in public

**325 words**

**News share: ~9%**: and the post crosses from report to reading *fourteen words into its first sentence*.

**Why it's here.** The most technically careful post in the corpus. It publicly second-guesses a named friend's funding round and does not read as an attack, because of six specific devices:

1. **Conflict disclosed up front, including the unflattering part.** *"The guy joined my community last year, took my Linkedin course, and from zero created what I thought was a better version of both my Linkedin content AND my RB2B product!"* Pre-empting the sour-grapes charge by *stating* it.
2. **Specific, unstinting praise before the criticism.** *"My absolute favorite thing he did was this forward looking statement that gave him MASSIVE credibility on every post"* … *"Genius marketing."*
3. **The criticism is a question, not a verdict.** *"Is anybody else confused by that statement?"* *"Here's my question. Do these investors ACTUALLY think Claude and Clay can be taken down with $6m?"* A wrong figure inside a question is a slip; inside an assertion it's a mistake.
4. **Target substitution.** *"But people ask me why I hate VC so much and this is an example."* By the second half the criticism has moved to the investors.
5. **A relationship receipt.** *"As I replied to Amos, a long and prosperous journey."* Proof the criticism was already delivered privately.
6. **Warm sign-off.** *"Amos, you're a G. Keep building."*

**The proportions matter:** roughly 81 words of credit against 48 of critique, plus 87 words of de-escalation at the close. **Insider Dissent is the only construction that must spend words repairing what it strains.**

**The precondition is hard:** you need non-public material about this exact event that you can quote. *"When I told him I was sad to lose another bootstrapper to the VC dark side, he said something that totally dumfounded me."* Without a real unresolved question you have a congratulations post with a complaint stapled on.

**Hedged almost entirely by grammatical mood**, plus explicit epistemic markers: *"I'm guessing they do not"*, *"he had like 10 customers"*, the *like* is doing real work.

## Post 287: 47 words, and the one that gets away with it

**47 words**

**News share: ~17%**: eight words of fact.

**Why it's here.** It is the corpus's shortest post, its only post with no time marker, no name and no source, and it demonstrates both the Single-Datapoint precondition and its limit.

**Why the fact stands alone.** *"1,000,000 views on a 6hr 22min YouTube video is absolutely insane to me."* The number carries its own surprise; no interpretation is needed to feel it, and nothing is contested. The reader doesn't need to know which video, because the argument doesn't depend on any property of that video beyond its length and view count. **Adding the title would add zero.**

**The precondition:** can the whole event be stated as one number in under fifteen words with zero explanation, and does that number violate an assumption the audience holds? If the number needs a second sentence of setup, this construction is closed.

**And here's the limit, stated honestly.** Nothing carries across the bridge. The anomaly is the *duration*, 6hr 22min, and the duration never reappears. The takeaway is about views versus customer problems, which the datapoint doesn't entail. **It survives because 47 words is too short to audit.** At 200 words the same gap would be visible and fatal.

**Standing: none at all.** The only self-reference is *"is absolutely insane to me"*, and *"The takeaway:"* assumes the teaching position with no basis whatsoever. **That is viable only because the claim being made is small.** Scale the claim and the standing has to scale with it.

**Near-zero exposure.** Nothing attributed, nothing dated, nobody named. The post has no expiry because it has nothing to expire against.

<summary><b>Full post</b></summary>

```
1,000,000 views on a 6hr 22min YouTube video is absolutely insane to me.

The takeaway: stop writing content with the goal of getting the most views and start writing content with the goal of solving the biggest problems for your customers. 

Whoever provides the most value wins.
