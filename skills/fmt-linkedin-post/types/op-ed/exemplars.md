## Post 224

*262 words · 842 reactions · 97 comments (12% of reactions) · 30 reposts*

Surface reading first, "At first glance, these look like depressing confessionals", then the named mechanism (Garden Path Sentences, Prediction Error), then the mechanism handed back as a test on the reader's own work. The mechanism is self-demonstrating: you feel it happen on the sentence in front of you.


> A Swiss insurance company ran a campaign with 0 photos, 0 colors, and 0 celebrities. It turned out to be the one of the smartest ads I've seen:
> 
> CONTEXT:
> 
> A few years ago, an insurance company called Swiss Life ran a print campaign that proves you don't need a million-dollar budget to get attention. You just need a sentence that breaks the reader's brain.
> 
> At first glance, these look like depressing confessionals.
> 
> "She's my everything went wrong." 
> 
> "I like working with you is impossible."
> 
> But look closer.
> 
> These are called "Garden Path Sentences." They lead your brain down one path, then suddenly twist you down another.
> 
> Your brain is a prediction machine. When you read "She's my everything...", your brain auto-completes the thought ("...and I love her"). When the sentence continues "...went wrong," your brain suffers a Prediction Error. It wakes up. You must re-read it to make sense of it.
> 
> Here's how you can apply this Swiss Life logic to your next headline or opening line:
> 
> Start with a cliché, then break it.
> 
> Predictable: "Marketing is hard work." (Boring).
> 
> The Twist: "Marketing is hard work is a lie bad agencies tell you." (Wait, what?).
> 
> Predictable: "We need to talk." (Scary).
> 
> The Twist: "We need to talk is the worst way to start a meeting." (Relatable).
> 
> Look at your latest subject line. If I can guess the end of the sentence before I finish reading it, you have lost me.
> 
> Rewrite it so the second half contradicts the first half.
> 
> "I love my job...
> 
> ...is something I haven't said in years." 
>
