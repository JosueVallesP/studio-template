# Type: owned story

A post built from something the person did, ran, attended, decided, or was wrong about. Method by Josué Valles, built from 32 high-performing posts sorted by how they're built. The person being in the material is the whole asset: it's the one thing a competitor can't reproduce by reading the same sources.

## What it's for

Being seen as a person, trust, and relationships, through what the person did rather than what they claim. Each path sells something different (step 1). It's also the type to reach for when the person has raw material about themselves and doesn't know what shape it should take: the routing question says which construction the material can carry, and says so when it can't carry any.

## Where it sits in the library

- **Hook moves:** 4 (put the reader in the room) for a located moment; 5 (then and now) and 15 (the admission) for a reversal; the experiment variant of 5 for a practice that ended; 9 (the line someone said) when the other person's line opens the post.
- **Structures:** S3 (the story arc) for a located moment and a reversal; S6 (the experiment report) for a practice that ended; S4 (the argument) or S3 for a practice still running, since its middle is a mechanism; S2 (the explained list) for a field report.
- **Inside moves:** M2 (the turn), M8 (the subject's own words, here the other person's line), M9 (the meaning line), M7 (the other side, taken seriously) for the concession.
- **Inventory slots:** STORY, EXPERIMENT, ADMISSION, LINE, sometimes RESULT.

What this file adds to cards 4, 5 and 15: the routing question, the constraints that hold across all 32 posts, where to spend detail, where the turn goes, and the gate for each path.

## Facts and sources

Everything about the person in this type is a fact: the scene, the line someone said, the number, the cost, the old belief. All of it comes from the four sources in `CLAUDE.md` rule 1 and is cited in the source map. Nothing gets added to make a path work: not a quote, not a time or place, not a boundary on an experiment, not a feeling. When a path needs something the material doesn't hold, ask for it in one question about the event ("What did she say, as close to her words as you remember?"). If the person doesn't have it, take the next path.

## Step 1. Route

**One question: who or what was the source of resistance?** It can be answered from raw notes before writing a word, and the path follows from it.

| Resistance | Path | Material test |
|---|---|---|
| Another person who could refuse, embarrass or surprise them | **Located moment** | Is there a line in the material the other person would recognize as theirs? |
| A question they didn't know the answer to | **Deliberate practice, ended** | Can the boundary be written as a sentence with a number in it, and can they finish "before this I didn't know whether ___"? |
| An assumption about how the work is normally done | **Deliberate practice, ongoing** | Can they say how often and for how long, and state a mechanism the reader wouldn't guess? |
| Nobody: they had access others didn't, and reported it | **Field report** | Can they name the occasion with a date, a headcount and a place, and list six or more items that stand alone? |
| Their own past self | **Reversal** | Can the old belief be written in the words they held it in, and is there a sentence where they end up worse off? |

**If nothing passes, the material isn't an owned story yet.** Say so and say exactly what's missing. That sends the idea back to the bank with a specific ask instead of producing a weak post. The missing piece is almost always the same: nobody wrote down a line someone said.

**If two paths pass, choose on what the post should sell:** a located moment sells the person (character seen in what they did while someone watched); deliberate practice sells the method, with its cost; a field report sells their judgement, the ability to notice; a reversal sells judgement backed by having been wrong.

**Ended or ongoing.** These are one path with a switch, because they sell the same thing and differ on whether the thing stopped. Keeping them together prevents reporting a habit as if it concluded, or an experiment as if it's a lifestyle. The switch flips three things:

| | Ended | Ongoing |
|---|---|---|
| First line | Declare the boundary, report a result | Declare the contrast, report a mechanism |
| Close | Open: implies a next run | Hands the practice to the reader |
| Numbers | Bound the claim | Show how long it has run |

**Hard gate on the ongoing branch: no mechanism paragraph, no post.** A practice with no explanation of why it works is a status update.

## Step 2. Draft

### What holds across all 32 posts

Each of these is doing a job on the reader:

- **Open on something the reader could photograph, not something they'd have to agree with.** An object enters the mind without a truth check; an abstraction makes the reader decide whether they agree before they read on, and disagreement is an exit. None of the 32 opens on a definition, an industry statistic, or "most people think X."
- **Hold back the cost, not the outcome.** The reader can leave at any second, so suspense about what happened is a bad bet. Suspense about what it cost survives giving the plot away. One corpus post gives away everything in two lines and holds back only the embarrassment. (This isn't the drum roll house rule H18 bans; the cost arrives in the body, in full.)
- **Name the price before the lesson.** The reader's default question is "what are you selling?" A loss they didn't have to disclose reads as honest. Two corpus posts share a structure; the one where every item has its price outperforms the other about four to one.
- **Let someone else say the thesis first.** A quoted line arrives as evidence, not assertion, so the reader draws the conclusion and the person's restatement confirms it. A speaker also puts a body in a place: a quote is a scene.
- **Grant the usual view before breaking it.** The concession is the move. A claim that has already granted its strongest objection has nowhere left to be attacked (M7).
- **One detail nobody else could have per beat.** A single strange detail reads as memory; a pile reads as description.
- **Start in "I", end in "you", and let the change mark the turn.** "I" earns permission, since their experience isn't disputable; "you" turns it into something for the reader. The "you" is the reader, addressed directly (house rule H13), never a general "you" that drifts in mid-story.
- **Put a number where a claim would otherwise be unverifiable, and nowhere else.** "The first morning in 1,735 days" works because "almost five years" would be a habit and 1,735 is a dependency. A number that isn't settling anything is noise.
- **End on the sentence they'd say out loud, shorter than the one before it.** The mean closing line in the corpus is about fourteen words; a third are under ten.
- **Change the sentence shape at the turn,** so a reader scanning at speed notices it: a short sentence after long ones, a two-word question.
- **Let the strongest line be the one that costs them.** Self-indictment is the one assertion a reader can't accuse them of making for gain. The three highest comment-to-reaction ratios in the corpus belong to posts whose central admission is still unresolved at the end.
- **A closing question asks the reader to remember, not to agree.** Comments come from questions aimed at the reader's own experience; a question with an obvious answer gets reactions instead (C2).

### Where to spend detail

Detail is front-loaded, roughly 40% denser in the first third, and it thins on purpose: it buys entry and proves presence, and once presence is proved, more detail is a tax. Roughly 70/30 specific to general in the first half, 25/75 in the last quarter, flipping at the turn.

Give the most resolution, in this order, to: anything the reader might not believe, then the cost, then the embarrassing detail, then one piece of sensory texture near the turn. That texture comes from the story's `scene` field, the material, or a question to the person ("what could you see or hear right then?"), never from the writer: an invented sound or smell is an invented fact (rule 1). With no scene on file, ask; if they don't remember one, the story runs without it.

Hold detail back from three things:

- **The antagonist.** Never named in any of the 32. Naming makes it a grievance; leaving them unnamed makes a category the reader fills with their own.
- **The general point.** State the particular precisely, then let the lesson go plain.
- **What actually made it work,** when saying it would turn the post into a tutorial.

(When the body is a list, detail is the content and doesn't thin. The list is the proof.)

### Where the turn goes

Turns land either at 31 to 54% of the post or at 72 to 96%, almost never in between. Early enough to frame what follows, or late enough to be the reward. Late turns belong to the located moment, deliberate practice and the field report (where the material is the subject); early turns belong to the reversal (where the turn is the subject and the rest is evidence).

Give the turn its own line (26 of 32 do; the ones that bury it are the flat ones). The strongest signposted turn sets the expected reading aside before giving the real one, and it does it in a full sentence that carries both, with the reason: "The experiment showed me that people want to know their neighbors; the popsicles were the excuse." A bare "Not X. Y." pair fails H17. Saying the turn twice, first concrete or spoken and then plain and compressed, is the strongest closing pattern in the corpus; the second time is the post's one meaning line (M9).

### Keeping the reader moving

Every device that works opens a loop whose shape the reader can see: a question asked early and answered late; a counted exception ("148 said yes. Then there was this one guy"); a denied expectation in the hook ("I ran 200 miles and didn't learn a thing about marketing"); an uncertainty left open, which earns the most comments; a cost named but not yet priced. The weakest is an announced count ("my six takeaways"), which creates obligation, not curiosity.

Never open a loop in the last lines and leave it. "Full breakdown in the comments" produced the lowest comment ratio in the corpus.

### Shape by path

- **Located moment.** Drop into a scene that can be placed → only the context needed to understand the room → the episode in order → the hinge is a line in quote marks → the consequence, flat → often a second turn → the rule, named. The first line fixes time, place and what was at stake. At least 55% scene, or it stops being located. Usually 180 to 420 words.
- **Deliberate practice.** The boundary or the contrast → why it existed → what they didn't know, or what the reader assumes → the mechanics in enough detail to repeat → what came back → what it was really about → handing it to the reader. An ended run declares its protocol and boundary before any outcome; stated after, it reads as retrofitted. An ongoing one opens on the contrast that justifies 250 words about an ordinary habit. Usually 200 to 440 words.
- **Field report.** The occasion with its dimensions → the payload up front → independent items → at least one only being there could produce → what the occasion belongs to. There's no turn; the post builds up. Scene-setting under 15%, at most fourteen items, usually 300 to 510 words. This path rests on three posts, so its rules are less settled than the others.
- **Reversal.** The old self or the old number → the belief in the words they held it in → it applied, at length and in good faith → the cost, unsoftened → the turn, often a build-up rather than a moment → what they do instead → the corrected belief, compressed. The old position at full strength, without irony, or the drop costs nothing. The claim goes at the end. Usually 160 to 550 words.

Length follows the number of units, about 35 words per unit, not the path. If a draft runs long, the question is how many units it's carrying and whether each earns its place.

### Switching paths

When the material fits one path and the purpose calls for another. Each switch needs facts, so each starts with a question to the person; when they don't have the answer, the switch is off.

- **Located moment to deliberate practice (ended).** Needs a boundary and a prior: "Did you set a limit on it, and what did you expect before you started?" Then cut the other person's dialogue (a run has no antagonist), and open on the boundary.
- **Field report to located moment.** Keep the one item where a specific person said or did something to them, and ask for the day, the room, who else was there, and the line. Cut the other items; kept as a list underneath, they undo the moment.
- **Ongoing to ended.** Only if the practice really did have a checkpoint ("Was there a point where you counted what had changed?"). Then the mechanism becomes findings and the close becomes an open thread.
- **Located moment to reversal.** Needs the belief they held before the scene, in their words, and the sentence where they ended up worse off. Ask for both. The other person shrinks to the one line that triggers the turn. If there's no worse-off sentence, drop the switch.

## Checks the judge adds

On top of the stage's checks, each a FAIL with the line quoted:

- **OS1. The path's tell.**
  - Located moment: the words after the scene closes outnumber the scene's words (the moment became a launchpad for a CV); or numbers in the last third that come from after the moment.
  - Ended practice: the findings name the person's own people or products instead of what was learned; or the numbers describe how tight the filter was, not how sure the result is. That's an announcement dressed as an experiment.
  - Ongoing practice: the draft sums up as "I do a nice thing" with no answer to why it works; or the last line asks the reader to admire rather than to try.
  - Field report: two items could be swapped for two others from the same occasion without changing the meaning; or an item is a noun phrase, not a claim.
  - Reversal: there's no sentence where the person is worse off at the end of the admission than at the start (a résumé with a modest opening); or the admission and its rebuttal sit within three lines of each other.
- **OS2. Scene share.** Under about 35% scene and the post leans on outside authority to stand in for evidence; over about 85%, it needs to be cut into units or put the meaning in someone's mouth. The stable band is 50 to 75%.
- **OS3. Nothing supplied.** Every quote, time, place, number, boundary and feeling traces to the source map. A detail added for texture is invented (check 18).
- **OS4. Opening.** The first line is something the reader could photograph, not a claim to agree with.
- **OS5. The turn** has its own line and sits in the band its path calls for.

## Things that look like rules and aren't

These vary because people write differently, and copying them makes a template: one sentence per line, emoji, labelled takeaway blocks ("TAKEAWAY:", "Moral of the story"; the posts with the strongest turns just say it), bullet glyphs, whether the closing rule is an imperative, a saying or a question (what is structural: deliberate practice ends open, every other path ends closed), P.S. lines, dividers, register, and "Your turn:" blocks.
