# Arbitraged Content: Example Library

> Reference for the `arbitraged-content` type (`../arbitraged-content.md`). Method by Josué Valles. The posts quoted here are other people's, kept as they were published: learn the move, never copy the wording or the tics (dashes, stacked fragments, labelled takeaways). Every number, name and example in a post the studio writes comes from the person's sources or is a real case marked `borrowed: verify`; the figures in this file show the level of detail, never material to reuse.


This file contains 18 real examples of arbitraged content, annotated by structure type. Use these for voice calibration and structural reference, not as templates to copy verbatim.

## Structure Types at a Glance

| Type | Description | Examples |
|------|-------------|----------|
| Quote/Memo Anchor | Opens with a specific artifact (memo, quote, rule set), unpacks it, adds commentary | #1, #10 |
| Book Distillation | Takes a book's ideas, distills to principles, adds the author's own spin | #2, #11 |
| Biography/Timeline | Tells a person's story chronologically, extracts a lesson from the arc | #3, #16, #17 |
| Personal Observation | Short, punchy observation about someone's habits/approach → lesson | #4, #5 |
| Signal Boost | Points to a free resource, adds brief context on why it matters | #6 |
| Concept + Story | Explains an idea/law/concept, anchors it in a concrete story or example | #7, #13, #14, #15 |
| Personal + Source Parallel | Weaves the author's personal story alongside the source material | #8 |
| Event/Workshop Recap | Recaps content from a paid event, distills key takeaways | #9 |
| Brand Case Study | Tells a brand's marketing stunt/campaign story → marketing lesson | #12 |
| Leadership Case Study | Business leadership story with specific results → management lesson | #18 |

## Example #1: Quote/Memo Anchor
**Source:** David Ogilvy's 1982 internal memo on writing
**Structure:** Hook (specific claim about Ogilvy) → the actual memo content → author's commentary → engagement question
**What works:** Opens with a dollar figure and "secret weapon" framing. Reproduces the actual artifact (the 10 rules), which gives the reader something concrete and shareable. The commentary at the end ("AI is making it worse") ties it to the present moment. Closes with a personal vulnerability ("I'm definitely guilty of...") that invites reciprocity.
**Voice note:** Uses "---" divider to separate the artifact from the commentary. The commentary is only 3 sentences, doesn't over-explain.

## Example #2: Book Distillation
**Source:** "Made to Stick" by Chip and Dan Heath
**Structure:** Hook (bold recommendation) → 7 distilled principles, each with examples and the author's reframing → single-line takeaway
**What works:** Each principle gets a memorable label ("The Curse of Knowledge," "The Sinatra Test"). The author doesn't just summarize, they add their own examples and reframe in business language. "Stop selling 'solutions' and sell 'bathtubs and ice'" is the author's line, not the book's.
**Voice note:** Numbered list structure works because it mirrors the source (a framework book). Bold subheadings for each principle. The "TAKEAWAY" at the end is a clear call to action.

## Example #3: Biography/Timeline
**Source:** David Ogilvy's career arc
**Structure:** Hook (dollar figures, "French castle") → numbered chronological milestones → bridge line → lesson
**What works:** Each milestone is 2-3 sentences max: fact + insight. The timeline creates momentum ("what happened next?"). The bridge ("He became the best *because* of it") reframes the entire story. The closer ("You can't write interesting copy if you have boring inputs") is a proverb-quality takeaway.
**Voice note:** The numbered format controls pacing. Short sentences within each point. No filler between milestones.

## Example #4: Personal Observation
**Source:** Andrew Carnegie's work habits
**Structure:** Opening claim (2 hours/day) → Carnegie's own quote → author's personal reflection on why they study history → lesson about designing your own reality
**What works:** The Carnegie material is brief: it's the launchpad, not the whole piece. The author quickly pivots to their own POV about business history. Honest admission ("me included") builds trust.
**Voice note:** More conversational than the other examples. Feels like thinking out loud. "So the reason I'm obsessed with history is..." reads like spoken language.

## Example #5: Quick Observation
**Source:** Seth Godin's deliberate omissions
**Structure:** Observation (what Seth doesn't do) → context (world of tool obsession) → one-line lesson
**What works:** The entire piece is 5 sentences. It's proof that arbitraged content doesn't need to be long. The power is in the contrast, everyone else is doing X, Seth doesn't, and he thrives. The closer ("There are no real rules, so make rules that work for you") is clean and memorable.
**Voice note:** Shortest example in the set. No formatting, no lists, no dividers. Just observation → lesson.

## Example #6: Signal Boost
**Source:** Brandon Sanderson's free YouTube lectures
**Structure:** Context (Sanderson's Apple TV deal) → the free resource → promise of author's own value-add
**What works:** Minimal: this is the "lightest" form of content arbitrage. The author adds value by curating (pointing to something the audience might not know about) and promising their own notes. "An insane amount of value" is the only editorial line.
**Voice note:** Very short. This format works when the source material is genuinely remarkable and doesn't need much framing.

## Example #7: Concept + Story
**Source:** Conway's Law (1968)
**Structure:** Opening principle → origin story → what it means in practice (multiple examples) → implications → call to examine your own org
**What works:** This is the longest example and the most idea-heavy. It succeeds because every abstract claim is immediately grounded in a concrete example ("Three engineering groups that rarely talk..."). The bridge section ("you ship your org chart") gives the concept a sticky phrase. Ends with a practical exercise, not just a lesson.
**Voice note:** Longer paragraphs than other examples: appropriate for a concept-heavy piece. Still uses short declarative sentences for emphasis ("Not sometimes. Every time." / "That's what makes it a law.").

## Example #8: Personal + Source Parallel
**Source:** James Van Der Beek's colon cancer diagnosis
**Structure:** Disclaimer about celebrity news → Van Der Beek's story → "This could have been me" pivot → author's parallel story → health information → call to action
**What works:** The most emotionally intense example. The parallel structure (his symptoms, my symptoms, his diagnosis, my diagnosis) creates a powerful rhythm. Raw honesty ("I can *smell* blood in my poop") builds enormous trust. The health information at the end feels earned because of the personal stakes.
**Voice note:** More vulnerable than other examples. Uses profanity ("sneaky f*cker") deliberately. The "(pic taken the day before surgery)" closer is devastating in its understatement.

## Example #9: Event/Workshop Recap
**Source:** Greg Isenberg's Offline Mode workshop ($5,375)
**Structure:** Price tag hook → 5 numbered takeaways, each with sub-points → personal photo context
**What works:** The price tag in the hook creates instant curiosity ("what's worth $5,375?"). Each takeaway is a mini-framework with actionable detail. The specificity (8% opt-in rate, $2-3 subscriber value) gives it credibility.
**Voice note:** Heavy use of sub-bullets and formatting: appropriate for a "distilled wisdom" piece. The arrow (→) notation keeps sub-points scannable.

## Example #10: Quote/Memo Anchor (Leadership)
**Source:** Marcus Aurelius / Meditations
**Structure:** Contrarian hook (you're NOT Jobs/Musk) → 10 Aurelius quotes with modern business commentary → takeaway
**What works:** The anti-hero opening differentiates from the typical "be like Elon" post. Each Aurelius quote is paired with a modern reframe in plain business language. "Drop the drama. Consistency and honesty beat 3 a.m. intensity every time" is the author's voice, not Marcus Aurelius's.
**Voice note:** The numbered format mirrors Example #1 but the commentary is longer per point. Bold quotes stand out visually.

## Example #11: Book/Program Recommendation
**Source:** Ray Bradbury's reading program for writers
**Structure:** Recommendation hook → Bradbury's prescription → author's personal experience following it → what they gained → invitation
**What works:** The author didn't just describe the program, they actually did it and shares what happened. Name-dropping specific authors they discovered (Seneca, Dickinson, Melville) adds texture. Ends by asking for recommendations, which invites engagement.
**Voice note:** More reflective tone. Uses the arrow (→) for the three-part reading list. Parenthetical asides feel natural.

## Example #12: Brand Case Study
**Source:** Heinz ketchup-dispensing billboards in Chicago
**Structure:** What they did → how it worked → historical context (2017 troll) → one-line lesson
**What works:** The story itself is entertaining enough to carry the piece. The 2017 "Chicago Dog Sauce" callback adds depth. The lesson ("Pay attention to where people argue about you") is sharp and universally applicable beyond ketchup.
**Voice note:** Playful tone matches the subject. Emoji used deliberately (just two). Bold formatting for the lesson makes it pop.

## Example #13: Concept + Story (Founder Mindset)
**Source:** Tim Draper on missionary vs. mercenary founders
**Structure:** Opening confession line → Draper's philosophy → contrasting bullet lists (mercenary tells vs. missionary tells) → the test → closer
**What works:** The parallel bullet structure makes the contrast visceral. "Would you still work on this if capital markets closed tomorrow?" is a devastating litmus test. The piece works because it doesn't just describe the concept, it gives you a way to test yourself against it.
**Voice note:** Shorter paragraphs than #7. The bullet lists are 1 line each. The "test for founders" section shifts from observation to direct challenge.

## Example #14: Concept + Story (Hiring)
**Source:** Garry Tan's "hire out of pain" philosophy
**Structure:** The rule → why it works (three reasons) → practical exercise → gut check → engagement question
**What works:** Breaks down a simple-sounding rule into the non-obvious reasons it works. Each reason gets its own mini-explanation. The "try this" section gives the reader a concrete action. Ends with a direct question.
**Voice note:** "Pain is information" is a perfect bridge line, three words that reframe the entire concept. The progressive structure (rule → why → how → check) guides the reader.

## Example #15: Concept + Story (Productivity)
**Source:** Kevin Kelly's life advice / "Excellent Advice for Living"
**Structure:** Opening reframe (being stuck isn't about intelligence) → 5 selected principles with deep commentary → final line
**What works:** The author doesn't just list Kelly's advice, they add substantial commentary that doubles the value. Personal experience woven in ("When we weren't seeing true word-of-mouth growth at Gamma...") adds credibility. Each principle gets enough space to breathe.
**Voice note:** Longer entries per point than #10. The author's commentary often exceeds the length of Kelly's original advice, that's the arbitrage working.

## Example #16: Biography/Timeline (Founder)
**Source:** Hiroshi Mikitani / Rakuten origin story
**Structure:** Setting (1995, prestigious bank job) → the earthquake → decision → outcome → author's lesson about waiting
**What works:** The emotional core (earthquake, finding relatives at a morgue) earns the right to the lesson about not waiting. The pivot from story to direct address ("If you're waiting to start something, you already know the trap") is clean. Multiple instances of Mikitani starting "when people said" something dismissive creates a pattern.
**Voice note:** "Not someday, but now" in italics gives it a mantra quality. The repetition of "Each time, everyone said wait. Each time, they were wrong" has rhythmic power.

## Example #17: Biography/Timeline (Late Starter)
**Source:** Bob Parsons / GoDaddy origin story
**Structure:** Age hook (47 when he started) → military backstory → career path → GoDaddy founding → the Hawaii valet moment → PTSD revelation → lesson
**What works:** The most emotionally complex example. The valet parking revelation is a perfect story-within-a-story. The PTSD section adds depth that most business content avoids. The final contrast ("Some people start late because they're behind. Others start late because they're finally whole") is the best bridge line in the entire set.
**Voice note:** Longer than most examples. The emotional weight justifies the length. "Scar tissue" as a metaphor for experience is vivid and original.

## Example #18: Leadership Case Study
**Source:** Alan Mulally's Ford turnaround
**Structure:** Situation (bleeding $12.7B) → the rule (mandatory meetings) → the confrontation story → results (specific numbers) → lesson
**What works:** The "We can still be friends. But you can't work here" quote is the centerpiece, it's dramatic, specific, and reveals character. The results section uses three concrete metrics. The lesson ("Most CEOs avoid confrontation. Mulally made it the cornerstone") is a clean contrast.
**Voice note:** Relatively short for a case study. The quote does most of the work. Numbers in the results section are formatted for scanning.
