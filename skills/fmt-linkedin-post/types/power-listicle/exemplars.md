# Exemplars

> Reference for the `power-listicle` type (`../power-listicle.md`). Method by Josué Valles. The posts quoted here are other people's, kept as they were published: learn the move, never copy the wording or the tics (dashes, stacked fragments, labelled takeaways). Every number, name and example in a post the studio writes comes from the person's sources or is a real case marked `borrowed: verify`; the figures in this file show the level of detail, never material to reuse.


Twelve complete posts grouped by path, plus three anti-patterns. Read the ones for your path before drafting.

Two of the anti-patterns are near-duplicates of exemplars, same list, different execution. Those pairs are the most useful thing in this file, because they isolate one variable each: post 302 against 222 isolates *where you cut*, and post 310 against 214 isolates *the opener*. Read both pairs whatever path you're on.

Every exemplar has its weakest item flagged. Knowing where a good list sags is more useful than a clean template.

## Post 217

*295 words · 2,481 reactions · 110 comments (4%) · 157 reposts*

Canon source: someone else's closed set, so the writer only has to gloss, which is what lets it run to 10 items at 26 words each. Every item is "Good design is X" plus one self-contained gloss carrying a date, a product, or a translation. Terminal item is the canon's own motto, so no close is needed and none is given.

**Its weak item is #6**: "Good design is honest. It doesn't make a product appear more than it is." Fifteen words, the shortest in the post, and the only one that restates its own title. Its neighbours all carry an anchor: #5 has a consequence, #7 has Vitsoe and 1960, #9 has a historical placement. This is the clearest filler item in the corpus.


> Jony Ive said Dieter Rams' work was the foundation for almost everything Apple designed. Rams was head of design at Braun for 34 years and designed over 500 products. Every one followed the same 10 principles:
> 
> 1. Good design is innovative. Not different for the sake of it. Innovative in the sense that it solves a real problem in a way nobody thought to try.
> 
> 2. Good design makes a product useful. People shouldn't have to read a manual to understand your work.
> 
> 3. Good design is aesthetic. Rams didn't separate beauty from function. He believed that things you use every day shape your environment, and ugly tools make an ugly life.
> 
> 4. Good design makes a product understandable. The product should explain itself. The form should make the function obvious.
> 
> 5. Good design is unobtrusive. Design is not art. It doesn't exist to express the designer. It exists to serve the person using it. The moment someone notices "the design," you've already failed.
> 
> 6. Good design is honest. It doesn't make a product appear more than it is. 
> 
> 7. Good design is long-lasting. Rams designed a shelving system in 1960 that Vitsoe still sells today...unchanged. He was designing things that would never need to be redesigned.
> 
> 8. Good design is thorough down to the last detail. Nothing is arbitrary. Nothing is left to chance. If a detail doesn't serve the whole, it doesn't belong.
> 
> 9. Good design is environmentally friendly. He said this in the 1970s. Decades before sustainability became a talking point, Rams argued that wasting resources through thoughtless design was a form of disrespect.
> 
> 10. Good design is as little design as possible. His motto: "Less, but better." Strip away everything that doesn't serve the purpose, and what remains is the design.

## Post 222

*507 words · 742 reactions · 115 comments (15%) · 66 reposts*

Distillation source: a book the writer actually read, reduced to seven items *the writer renamed*. The gate this path has to pass is the double requirement, each item carries both the source's own evidence AND a place it applies in the reader's work. "Concrete: Use 'Velcro'" gives you the kidney-heist bathtub, then "Stop selling 'solutions' and sell 'bathtubs and ice.'"

Also the best instance of the `Category: Action` headline device, the noun preserves the book's framework, the second half delivers payload.

**Read this against post 302 below.** Same list, same author-voice, evidence trimmed out.


> Anyone who communicates ideas for a living should read Made to Stick at least once a year. I distilled the book from 304 pages to 7 principles that changed how I write, think, and sell new ideas:
> 
> 1. Fight "The Curse of Knowledge"
> 
> Once you know something, you can't imagine not knowing it. You subconsciously tune your frequency to "Expert," using jargon and skipping foundational steps. 
> 
> To win, write like you are explaining things to your smart but impatient grandmother.
> 
> 2. Simple: Find the Core
> 
> Proverbs survive for centuries because they pack maximum wisdom into minimum words. "A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush" beats a 500-word essay on risk management. 
> 
> Southwest Airlines didn't have a 10-point strategic plan; they had one proverb: "THE low-cost airline." If a decision didn't help them be the low-cost airline (like serving chicken salad), they didn't do it. 
> 
> If you can't explain your value prop in one sentence after three beers, it is too complex.
> 
> 3. Unexpected: Break the "Guessing Machine"
> 
> Violate expectations. Instead of "excellent customer service," say "We don't answer emails on weekends because we want our staff to love their lives." Instead of a statistic about safety, show a safety video that gets interrupted. 
> 
> 4. Concrete: Use "Velcro"
> 
> Never say "we offer high-performance solutions." 
> 
> Say "our battery saves you 47 minutes of charging time per day." 
> 
> The Heaths use the example of the "kidney heist" urban legend. We remember it because it has a bathtub, ice, and a note. It is visceral. Stop selling "solutions" and sell "bathtubs and ice."
> 
> 5. Credible: The "Sinatra Test"
> 
> You don't need a PhD to be credible; you just need to pass the "Sinatra Test": 
> 
> If I can make it there, I can make it anywhere.
> 
> If you catered a dinner for the White House, you don't need to tell me your food is safe and delicious. The credential speaks for itself. Don't drown people in statistics. Give them one immense, verifiable proof point. "We power the security for the Pentagon" beats a 20-page whitepaper on encryption standards every time.
> 
> 6. Emotional: The "Mother Teresa" Principle
> 
> "If I look at the mass, I will never act. If I look at the one, I will."
> 
> Stop trying to get people to care about "the industry" or "the metrics." Make them care about one person. Don't say "We help companies reduce turnover." Say "We help the VP of HR stop dreading exit interviews." 
> 
> Lead with the feeling, then justify with the logic.
> 
> 7. Stories: The Flight Simulator
> 
> A great story is a flight simulator for the brain. When you tell a story about how a problem was solved, the listener mentally rehearses that success.
> 
> So walk the prospect through the simulation: "Imagine it's Monday morning. You open your laptop. Instead of 50 unread tickets, you see zero. You pour your coffee..." You are letting them "practice" the solution before they’ve even bought it. 
> 
> TAKEAWAY
> 
> Test your current messaging against these 7 rules.
> 
> If it fails half of them, rewrite it.

## Post 220

Ten Ogilvy rules at 13 words each, no commentary anywhere, and it works because the frame buys authority with a number about the source ("$363.2 million advertising empire").

The move worth stealing is position N: item 10 reverses the whole premise, "If you want ACTION, don't write. Go and tell the guy what you want." Inventories punch at the end. Promote that item to first and the list ends on an anticlimax.


> David Ogilvy built a $363.2 million advertising empire with one secret weapon: writing. In 1982, he sent this internal memo to 3,000+ employees that's better advice than anything you'll find on LinkedIn today:
> 
> Here's his exact advice:
> 
> "The better you write, the higher you go in Ogilvy & Mather. People who think well, write well. Woolly minded people write woolly memos, woolly letters and woolly speeches."
> 
> Then he gave them 10 rules:
> 
> 1. Read the Roman-Raphaelson book on writing. Read it three times.
> 
> 2. Write the way you talk. Naturally.
> 
> 3. Use short words, short sentences and short paragraphs.
> 
> 4. Never use jargon words like reconceptualize, demassification, attitudinally, judgmentally. They are hallmarks of a pretentious as*.
> 
> 5. Never write more than two pages on any subject.
> 
> 6. Check your quotations.
> 
> 7. Never send a letter or memo on the day you write it. Read it aloud the next morning - and then edit it.
> 
> 8. If it is something important, get a colleague to improve it.
> 
> 9. Before you send your letter or memo, make sure it is crystal clear what you want the recipient to do.
> 
> 10. If you want ACTION, don't write. Go and tell the guy what you want.
> 
> ---
> 
> Most business writing today is terrible, and AI is making it worse. Ogilvy understood that clear writing is clear thinking, and clear thinking builds better businesses.
> 
> P.S. Which of these 10 rules do you break most often? I'm definitely guilty of publishing stuff the same day I write it. What about you? 👇

## Post 47

*161 words · 495 reactions · 84 comments (17%) · 11 reposts*

Five steps where order is the entire content. Swap steps 2 and 4 and you get "How you learned the thing is the hook" before there is any learning story, the sentence has no referent. That's the ladder test: shuffle two rows and see whether a sentence becomes *false*.

**Note the structural defect it shares with every ladder in the corpus:** the last rung is under-specified, six words, because there's no next stage to fall short of. Budget extra for it.


> I ghostwrite for a bunch of LinkedIn accounts. Which means I get a first-hand view of what actually gets leads here. Here's a framework I've started using because the new algo seems to love it: 
> 
> Step 1. Think of a difficult question that you've genuinely been asked by your ICP. (Please, no more of those "Founders always ask me" obvious BS posts.) I go through discovery call transcripts to get these. 
> 
> Step 2. Write down your best answer. 
> 
> Step 3. Articulate how you learned that answer. Did you get something wrong and learn the hard way? Did someone smart tell you? Did you do something and it worked really well? 
> 
> Step 4. How you learned the thing is the hook. 
> 
> Step 5. Then share the thing you learned. 
> 
> Save this, try it out, and see if it works for you too! (And if it does let me know :) I always want to know if I'm giving good advice or not.)


## Post 391

*275 words · 237 reactions · 24 comments (10%) · 20 reposts*

Three developmental stages, and the item anatomy that distinguishes this path: stage name → what it is → **the failure mode of staying here**. "If you stay here when you have 20+ employees, you become the 'Chief Fixing Officer.'"

Item 3 is written to be read after item 1, "You can't be the Monarch if you are still addicted to the adrenaline of being the Warrior."


> There’s only three modes of leadership required to scale a company from $0 to $100M+:
> 
> 1. The Brave Warrior (Start-up) 
> 
> This is the "do-it-all" mode. 
> 
> You are on the beach with a sword, fighting for survival. You lead from the front, rely on high energy, and fix every problem yourself. This is essential for getting to product-market fit. 
> 
> If you stay here when you have 20+ employees, you become the "Chief Fixing Officer." You create a culture of dependency where no one moves until you give the order.
> 
> 2. The Considered Architect (Scale-up) 
> 
> This is the shift that kills most founders. You have to put down the sword and pick up the blueprints. You stop "doing" the work and start designing the systems, processes, and teams that do the work.
> 
> If you find yourself saying, "It's faster if I just do it myself," or "We don't need process, we need speed," you are failing to make this shift. You are trying to build a skyscraper with a hammer.
> 
> 3. The Wise Monarch (Grown-up) 
> 
> This is the ultimate destination. The Monarch doesn't fight the battle or build the castle; they hold the space. They focus on Vision, Culture, and Capital. They lead through influence, not action.
> 
> You can't be the Monarch if you are still addicted to the adrenaline of being the Warrior.
> 
> ---
> 
> Most founders think that working harder (Warrior mode) is the answer to every problem.
> 
> But at the Scale-up stage (and beyond), your hard work is actually the problem.
> 
> You have to stop being the hero who saves the day, and start being the architect who designs a business that doesn't need saving.

# List-Spined Argument


## Post 204

*274 words · 474 reactions · 43 comments (9%) · 8 reposts*

Phrase exegesis, the sharpest sub-form. A quoted line supplies the items and each item unpacks one word of it: "Kindly means… Straight means… Not about you means… Be gone means…" The closer restates the position as a chant: "Be kind. Be straight. Be gone."

Note the frame is the largest of any path, about 110 words before item 1, and that the list is *interior*, with more prose after it than before. That's the distinguishing feature, and also the reason this path is where posts drift out of the archetype.


> The best advice I've ever got about delivering bad news was from the head of people at Morrisons. He said: 
> 
> “When you've got to deliver bad news, deliver it kindly, deliver it straight. It's not about you and how uncomfortable you are. Just tell them, be kind, be gone.”
> 
> What this means:
> 
> Most managers fail at hard conversations because they're trying to manage their own discomfort in the room. So they soften the news until it's unrecognisable:
> 
> They bury the headline under twelve minutes of preamble. They open with "how's your weekend?" when the person on the other side already knows something is wrong. They apologise three times. They ask if the person is "okay" before the person has even processed what they've been told.
> 
> All of that looks like kindness, but it’s not. 
> 
> It's the messenger trying to feel better about being the messenger.
> 
> * Kindly means: this person matters. Their dignity matters. You don't strip them of it.
> 
> * Straight means: they get the actual news, in the first sentence, in plain English. Tell them what happened, what it means, and what comes next.
> 
> * Not about you means: your job is not to be reassured that you handled it well. Don't fish for "I understand, it's not your fault." That's making them comfort you.
> 
> * Be gone means: once the news is delivered, give them the room. Don't linger. Don't keep talking to fill the silence. The conversation isn't yours anymore. It's theirs.
> 
> The reason most leaders dread these conversations is they're treating themselves as the patient.
> 
> But you're not the patient.
> 
> You're the doctor.
> 
> Be kind. Be straight. Be gone.

## Post 377

*471 words · 251 reactions · 56 comments (22%) · 22 reposts*

Eight items, every one true and well-phrased, and **every one passes the stranger test**, a competent outsider with a search engine could have produced all eight. None of them required the "20+ years of coaching over 1,000 founders" the opener claims.

Coefficient of variation on item length: **0.06.** Six of eight items are built identically. Real knowledge is lumpy; when every item weighs the same, a process set the length rather than a knowledge base. Item 3 also restates item 1.

This is what assembled looks like when it's competent.


> After 20+ years of coaching over 1,000 founders backed by all the major VC funds, I've boiled down my philosophy to 8 core principles:
> 
> 1) You can't fix scale-up problems with start-up leadership
> 
> Most founders double down on what made them successful in the start-up phase. Wrong approach. The scrappy, do-everything energy that got you to product-market fit becomes toxic when you need to manage 50 people. What got you here won't get you there.
> 
> 2) Every founder needs three different leadership styles, not one
> 
> Visionary leadership gets you started. Operational leadership scales the business. Strategic leadership builds lasting value. Most founders are stuck in one style. The survivors learn to switch between all three based on what the business needs.
> 
> 3) Your biggest strength becomes your biggest liability
> 
> I've seen brilliant entrepreneurs destroy their scale-ups because they couldn't stop micromanaging when they needed to delegate. Your superpower got you this far, but you need a full toolkit to go the distance.
> 
> 4) Most "people problems" are communication problems
> 
> Founders complain constantly about their teams, investors, and boards. But when you dig deeper, it's usually because they can't give feedback effectively or inspire followership. You need both management communication and influence skills. Most founders only have one.
> 
> 5) Your emotional baggage is running your business, not you
> 
> Your need for control, approval, or freedom that developed in childhood is making decisions for your company. That insecurity you've carried since you were 7? It's sabotaging your board meetings. Self-awareness isn't optional for leaders.
> 
> 6) Physical and mental stamina is a competitive advantage
> 
> Founding a company is a marathon, not a sprint. The founders who crash out aren't less talented - they're less disciplined about managing their stress, sleep, and emotional health. Taking care of yourself isn't selfish, it's strategic.
> 
> 7) What you permit, you promote as a leader
> 
> Every interaction is building your reputation. The late-night email you send while stressed, the way you walk into the office, how you handle conflict - it's all communicating to your team what you expect. Leadership is always "on."
> 
> 8) The best founders treat themselves like their most important hire
> 
> You wouldn't hire a CEO who had zero self-awareness, couldn't manage their emotions, or burned out every six months. Yet most founders never invest in developing themselves. Your business can only grow as much as you do.
> 
> TAKEAWAY:
> 
> Most founders think success is about product-market fit, fundraising, and hiring.
> 
> Those are table stakes.
> 
> Real success is about scaling yourself as you scale your business.
> 
> The founders who master these 8 principles don't just survive the scale-up journey.
> 
> They thrive through it.
> 
> P.S. If you're a Series A+ founder and this hit a nerve, I work with founders (and their co-founders and leadership teams) on exactly this stuff. DM me if you want to know more.

> Found this 1980 ad about writing clearly. 45 years later, it's still the best writing advice I've ever seen:
> 
> 1) Know exactly what you want to say before you start
> 
> Most people start writing and figure it out as they go. That's why most writing sucks. Thompson says outline first, write second. Revolutionary concept, apparently.
> 
> 2) Start where your readers are, not where you are
> 
> Don't assume people know what you know. Meet them at their level of understanding, then bring them along. Most "experts" write for other experts and wonder why nobody gets it.
> 
> 3) Use familiar word combinations
> 
> Thompson's example: 
> 
> A scientist wrote "The biota exhibited a one hundred percent mortality response." Translation: "All the fish died."
> 
> Stop trying to sound smart. Start trying to be clear.
> 
> 4) Arrange your points logically
> 
> Put the most important stuff first. Then the next most important. Then the least important. Seems obvious, but most people do it backwards.
> 
> 5) Use "first-degree" words
> 
> Thompson says some words bring immediate images to mind. Others need to be "translated" through first-degree words before you see them.
> 
> "Precipitation" => "Rain"
> "Utilize" => "Use"
> "Facilitate" => "Help"
> 
> 6) Cut the jargon
> 
> Thompson warns against words and phrases "known only to people with specific knowledge or interests." If your mom wouldn't understand it, rewrite it.
> 
> 7) Think like your reader, not like yourself
> 
> Thompson asks: "Do they detract from clarity?" Most writers ask: "Do I sound professional?" Wrong question.
> 
> ---
> 
> This ad is from 1980.
> 
> The internet didn't exist. Social media wasn't even a concept. But the principles of clear communication haven't changed.
>

> 2) Start where your readers are, not where you are
> 
> Don't assume people know what you know. Meet them at their level of understanding, then bring them along. Most "experts" write for other experts and wonder why nobody gets it.
> 
> 3) Use familiar word combinations
> 
> Thompson's example: 
> 
> A scientist wrote "The biota exhibited a one hundred percent mortality response." 
> 
> Translation: "All the fish died."
> 
> Stop trying to sound smart. Start trying to be clear.
> 
> 4) Arrange your points logically
> 
> Put the most important stuff first. Then the next most important. Then the least important. Seems obvious, but most people do it backwards.
> 
> 5) Use "first-degree" words
> 
> Thompson says some words bring immediate images to mind. Others need to be "translated" through first-degree words before you see them.
> 
> "Precipitation" => "Rain"
> "Utilize" => "Use"
> "Facilitate" => "Help"
> 
> 6) Cut the jargon
> 
> Thompson warns against words and phrases "known only to people with specific knowledge or interests." If your mom wouldn't understand it, rewrite it.
> 
> 7) Think like your reader, not like yourself
> 
> Thompson asks: "Do they detract from clarity?" Most writers ask: "Do I sound professional?" Wrong question.
