# Borrowed Story: exemplars

> Reference for the `borrowed-story` type (`../borrowed-story.md`). Method by Josué Valles. The posts quoted here are other people's, kept as they were published: learn the move, never copy the wording or the tics (dashes, stacked fragments, labelled takeaways). Every number, name and example in a post the studio writes comes from the person's sources or is a real case marked `borrowed: verify`; the figures in this file show the level of detail, never material to reuse.


Sixteen complete posts from the 358-post library. Every one is a keeper, nothing here is a bad post.

**Read the annotation before the post.** Each entry carries three things:

- **The fact-check verdict**, where one exists. Nineteen of the thirty-one posts in this corpus were audited against primary sources; seventeen came back with a defect. `NOT AUDITED` means exactly that, it does not mean clean.
- **Why the post is here**, the construction it demonstrates.
- **Its weakest move.** Knowing where a strong post goes soft is more useful than a clean template.

Several entries are paired deliberately. Posts 396 and 329 are structurally the same post with opposite verdicts; read them together. Posts 303 and 311 are the same construction executed well and badly.

# Decision and Dividend

## Post 392: The cleanest binding in the corpus

**179 words · fact-check: MINOR: 'Then talk to them' is a paraphrase inside quote marks; 'middle managers panicked' overstates the pushback. The story and the claim are sound; the defect is two decorations.**

**Why it's here.** Tightest story-to-claim binding of the 31. The claim is *"We often create rules to avoid having conversations."* The story is a director asking for a rule instead of having a conversation. **Both operative nouns, rule, conversation, are already in the story.** Nothing has to be argued; the claim is a description of what the reader just watched.

**And it has no outcome block.** This is the shape's whole risk profile in one comparison: the library contains a second post telling this same Barra story that bolts on a numbered results block. The audit note on this one reads *"this is the CLEAN version."* Same story, same writer instinct, and the block is the only difference between clean and defective.

**Weakest move.** *"Then talk to them"* in quotation marks. It's a paraphrase, and unquoting it costs nothing. Same for *"The middle managers panicked"*, one director complained; "middle managers panicked" is a scene the writer supplied.

**Steal:** the close does the work without a results block at all. *"You cannot expect people to act like adults if you treat them like children."* No numbers, no dividend, and the post is complete.

<details open>
<summary><b>Full post</b></summary>

```
When Mary Barra took over HR at General Motors, she replaced the entire 10-page document with two words: "Dress appropriately."

The middle managers panicked.

A director immediately complained to her, arguing that his team would start wearing jeans to meet with government officials. He demanded she write a specific rule to stop them.

Barra refused. She told him: "Then talk to them."

She realized the director was using the employee handbook as a shield to avoid a simple, awkward conversation.

Forced to actually manage, the director spoke to his team. They solved the problem instantly on their own: they agreed to keep a pair of nice pants in their lockers for big meetings.

The Lesson: 

We often create rules to avoid having conversations.

It is easier to write a policy than it is to look someone in the eye and tell them their judgment is off. But every time you write a rule to prevent a mistake, you also prevent people from thinking for themselves. 

You cannot expect people to act like adults if you treat them like children.
```

## Post 396: The survivor, and what 74 words of hedging bought

**225 words · fact-check: VERIFIED: clean. The audit's only notes are optional: unquote the Gallup paraphrase, change 'line cook' to 'line worker'.**

**Why it's here.** This post and the Ford post below are structurally the same post: CEO takes over a failing company, institutes one humble habit, three numbered results, contrast aphorism. Ford is 151 words. This is 225. **The 74-word difference is almost entirely hedges, and it is the whole difference between VERIFIED and MAJOR.**

Count them:

- *"roughly 10 notes every single day"*, an approximation marked as one.
- *"Some might call this a waste of a CEO's time."*, an opinion attributed to nobody, rather than asserted.
- *"**This was the cornerstone of a broader turnaround built on 'people first' principles.**"*, the scope limiter, and the most important sentence in the post. It states outright that the notes were one component of a larger programme. Without it, the post is claiming that handwritten cards produced a Fortune 500 turnaround.

**Weakest move.** *"Conant proved that high performance starts with high connection."* One CEO, one company, ten years. That is not a proof of a general law, it's a demonstration of a possibility. Swap "proved that" for "bet that" and the sentence becomes true.

**Also note:** the numbered block still has no source on any line, and the post survived anyway, because the scope limiter above it stops the block from carrying a causal claim. The block isn't the sin. The unhedged causal attribution is.

<details open>
<summary><b>Full post</b></summary>

```
When Douglas Conant took over Campbell Soup in 2001, the company was underperforming and deeply disengaged. Gallup called it "the worst workforce engagement in the Fortune 500." 

Conant didn't start with mass layoffs or a complex reorganization. 

He started with a pair of comfortable walking shoes.

He made a simple rule for himself: Be visible.

He walked 10,000 steps a day around the office and factory floors. And he introduced one signature habit that defined his leadership:

The Handwritten Note.

If he saw you doing a good job (whether you were an executive or a line cook) he would sit down and write you a personal thank-you card.

Not an email. 

A real card.

Over his 10-year tenure, he wrote more than 30,000 handwritten notes.

That’s roughly 10 notes every single day, for a decade.

Some might call this a waste of a CEO's time. 

Conant knew it was the foundation of trust.

This was the cornerstone of a broader turnaround built on "people first" principles.

And it worked.

1. Campbell's engagement scores climbed from the bottom of the Fortune 500 to world-class levels.

2- The stock price outperformed the S&P 500.

3. Employees stopped hiding from management and started helping them.

Conant proved that high performance starts with high connection.

Most leaders try to command respect. 

Conant earned it, one note at a time.
```

## Post 329: ANTI-PATTERN, the same post without the hedges

**151 words · fact-check: MAJOR: 2014 net income was $3.2B (restated to $1.2B), not $6.3B; that figure was pre-tax ex-items. 2014 was a DOWN year: profit fell 56%. Mulally left 1 July 2014. The 'we can still be friends' quote is leadership-blog lore.**

**Read this immediately after the Campbell post.** Same construction, 74 fewer words, four defects.

**Where each defect sits:**

- The **hook** is fine, Ford's 2006 loss really was $12.7B.
- The **quotation** is invention. *"That's okay. We can still be friends. But you can't work here."* is a line that circulates on leadership blogs. It is a closed-room quote with no attribution verb in front of it.
- The **outcome block** is where the post breaks. *"Ford went from a $12.7B loss in 2006 to a $6.3B profit in 2014"*, wrong figure, and 2014 was a down year. The block announces itself with *"The results were staggering:"*, runs exactly three items soft→hard, and carries no agent, date or source on any line. That is the signature.
- The **close** is an unpaid reframe: *"Most CEOs avoid confrontation. / Mulally made it the cornerstone of his turnaround."* "The cornerstone of his turnaround" is a business-book phrase that could close any post ever written. Nothing from the story appears in it.

**The one-sentence fix that would have saved it:** a scope limiter of the kind post 396 has. *"The weekly meetings were one piece of a turnaround that also included [X]."* That sentence converts a causal claim into a contributing one, and the causal claim is what the audit destroyed.

<summary><b>Full post</b></summary>

```
When Alan Mulally took over as Ford's CEO in 2006, the company was bleeding $12.7 billion a year. So he made one simple rule: 

Everyone HAD to attend his weekly meetings. No exceptions.

Every leader had to report their progress.

When a prominent executive skipped the first meeting, Mulally confronted him.

"That's okay. We can still be friends. But you can't work here," he said.

The results were staggering:

1. Ford went from a $12.7B loss in 2006 to a $6.3B profit in 2014.

2. The stock price soared 1,640% from its crisis low.

3. Ford became the only Detroit automaker that didn't need a government bailout.

Mulally's weekly meetings created what he called a "One Team" culture.

He forced executives to stop playing politics and start solving problems together. He built accountability through radical transparency and relentless implementation.

Most CEOs avoid confrontation.

Mulally made it the cornerstone of his turnaround.

## Post 53: ANTI-PATTERN, zero hedges, and it shows

**88 words · fact-check: UNVERIFIED: no documented basis for a mandatory cleaning requirement for new or senior hires. 'Toilets', 'new employees' and 'no exceptions' are embellishment. The real programme was voluntary ('Walk-a-Mile'). What is true: Southwest had no cleaning crew during the 10-minute turn and nobody was above taking out trash.**

**Why it's here.** 88 words, **not one qualifier anywhere in it**, and the corpus's most embellished specimen. That is not a coincidence, a hedge is a writer noticing a limit on what they know, and a post with none is a post whose author never noticed one.

**Watch the embellishment escalate in three lines:**

> *"he made new employees clean planes...including the toilets."*
> *"Even senior hires."*
> *"No exceptions."*

Each line raises the stakes and none adds information. The true version, nobody at Southwest was above taking out trash during a ten-minute turn, is a good story. The escalation converts it into a policy that never existed.

**The outcome block is pure assertion:** *"1. Flight delays dropped / 2. Employee turnover stayed low / 3. Southwest became the most profitable airline in history."* Soft, softer, superlative. No dates, no figures, no source. This is the block in its purest form.

**Steal one thing:** *"Most companies teach culture in a PowerPoint. / Herb taught it in a mop bucket."* This is the **only paid reframe** among the five Decision-and-Dividend closes in the corpus, because "mop bucket" is a concrete object lifted straight out of the story. The other four substitute an abstraction. Note what that means: the best line in the corpus's least reliable post.

<details open>
<summary><b>Full post</b></summary>

```
When Herb Kelleher ran Southwest Airlines, he made new employees clean planes...including the toilets.

Even senior hires.

Because he wanted everyone to understand the business from the ground up.

No exceptions.

Here’s what happened:

1. Flight delays dropped
2. Employee turnover stayed low
3. Southwest became the most profitable airline in history

Kelleher believed you can’t lead what you don’t understand.

So he built a culture where even the execs rolled up their sleeves.

Most companies teach culture in a PowerPoint.

Herb taught it in a mop bucket.

# Refuted Premise

## Post 114: The right construction, unsourced

**296 words · fact-check: MAJOR: '$700B company' is stale by ~3× (TSMC is ~$2T mid-2026). The Chang quote is UNDOCUMENTED, do not publish. 'Average successful founder is 42' should be 45 (Azoulay et al.). Tesla's FSD chips were Samsung, not TSMC.**

**Why it's here.** It is the corpus's one instance of a writer noticing that n=1 has a ceiling and solving it correctly.

The claim is a general one, age is not a barrier to founding. One founder cannot carry a general claim; a single case licenses only *possibility*. The post knows this, and imports population data to lift possibility into law:

> *- The average successful founder is 42*
> *- A 50-year-old is nearly twice as likely to build a high-growth company as a 30-year-old*

**That is exactly right, and it is exactly where the MAJOR came from.** The figure is wrong (45, not 42) and no source is named. The construction is correct; the execution is unsourced. That sentence describes this entire archetype.

**The opening is the corpus's best.** Seven words of bare dialogue, *"I'm too old to start a company."*, puts a sentence in the reader's mouth before the post says anything. Compare a paraphrased version ("Many people believe they've aged out of entrepreneurship") which nobody recognises as their own thought.

**The undocumented quote.** *"I could not have done it sooner… Because I was the first one."* reads like a Chang quote and cannot be found. This is the single most common way this archetype publishes something false: a sentence that is exactly what the person would have said.

<details open>
<summary><b>Full post</b></summary>

```
"I'm too old to start a company."

If Morris Chang had believed that at 55, we wouldn't have TSMC - the $700B company whose chips are the invisible foundation of our entire digital world.

He'd already been passed over for promotion. Failed at another company. In Silicon Valley terms, his career was supposed to be over.

Instead, he moved to Taiwan and created not just a company but an entirely new business model that revolutionized how technology is built.

The youth-obsessed tech narrative tells us innovation belongs to 20-somethings in hoodies: Gates (19). Jobs (21). Zuckerberg (19).

In reality, the data shows a different story:

- The average successful founder is 42
- A 50-year-old is nearly twice as likely to build a high-growth company as a 30-year-old
- The least successful founders? Those we glorify most? Early 20-somethings

Chang's genius wasn't technical brilliance, either.

It was seeing what everyone else missed after 30 years in semiconductors—that companies designing chips and companies manufacturing them could be separate entities.

"I could not have done it sooner," Chang said. "I don't think anybody could have done it sooner. Because I was the first one."

This insight (which transformed the entire industry) wasn't born from “youthful ambition,” but from decades of understanding problems deeply enough to see their solutions.

Because experience isn't baggage:

It's context.
It's pattern recognition.
It's knowing which risks are actually worth taking.

Today, every iPhone, every AI server, every Tesla runs on chips manufactured by the company Chang built after most would have settled into retirement.

So the next time you think your moment has passed - remember that your greatest contribution might still be ahead, built on the foundation of everything you've learned so far.

What could you build with all your hard-earned wisdom?
```

## Post 13: One noun, 166 words, and it holds

**166 words · fact-check: MINOR: '2-3 shows a week every week for 10 years' is wrong arithmetically (1,000/10yr = 1.9/wk) and factually: 1990–92 were near-dark years (19, 7 and 17 shows). The 1,000-shows figure itself is sound.**

**Why it's here.** The smallest complete instance of the archetype in the corpus, and proof that binding is a matter of a single word.

The claim is that entrepreneurs don't **tour** their idea. "Tour" is in the story literally, Bon Jovi toured. The metaphor carries the entire post because the operative noun already exists in the borrowed material. Nothing needs arguing.

**Run the substitution test on it.** Swap Bon Jovi for any other hard-working band and the claim survives, so on that test it fails. But the shared-noun test is what's holding the post up, and it passes emphatically. **The tests are independent, and one strong binding is enough.**

**The defect is in the elaboration, not the fact.** *"1000 live shows in their first decade"* is sound. *"An average of 2-3 shows a week every week for 10 years"* is the writer doing arithmetic on a figure to make it feel more concrete, and getting it wrong twice. **The elaboration was added for texture and it is the only false thing in the post.**

**Weakest move.** The workshop plug bolted to the bottom. It's not a craft feature; ignore it.

## Post 235: The fact-pair opening

**186 words · fact-check: NOT AUDITED**

**Why it's here.** A different opening that works, and the corpus's only instance of it.

> *"Figma's stock is down 86% from its peak. Their revenue growth just accelerated to 46%. Both are true. And the gap between those two numbers is maybe the best positioning lesson of the year."*

Two independently verifiable facts and the unexplained tension between them. The post exists to close the gap, so the reader has a reason to keep reading that isn't a promise. **Compare the compressed causal hook**, *"sold his agency and bought a castle with the profits"*, which asserts a connection instead of presenting one. The fact pair is safer because it makes no causal claim: it presents a gap and then explains it.

**And note the mechanism is stated, not implied:** *"Repositioning starts by conceding what everyone can already see. Then it tells you what that fact makes valuable now."* That is a one-sentence statement of how the story produces the claim. Most posts in this corpus juxtapose and hope.

# Annotated Case

## Post 303: Three readings that aren't the same reading

**457 words · fact-check: MINOR: Ad Age named it Commercial of the DECADE (1980s); 'greatest of the 20th century' was TV Guide. The '$155M in three months' figure is uncited (the documented figure is 72,000 Macs in 100 days). The $800k was the 60-second slot alone.**

**Why it's here.** Annotated Case requires **at least two readings that aren't restatements of each other.** This post has three genuinely distinct ones, and one of them argues against the post's own thesis.

1. Approval steps kill bold work, a claim about process.
2. When the argument isn't working, change what the decision means, a claim about tactics.
3. **The board wasn't wrong.**, a claim that undercuts 1 and 2.

**Reading 3 is the reason this post is here.** *"Their objections were reasonable by every standard measure."* Then: *"Is this survivorship bias? Maybe. For every '1984' that broke through, there's probably a dozen bold ads that got killed and deserved to be."* The writer names the strongest objection to their own post, and the post survives it. Almost nothing else in the corpus does this.

**Reading 2 names its mechanism.** *"The second he offered his own money, the question went from 'is this ad worth the risk' to 'are we really going to let our co-founder pay out of pocket because we're too cautious to run our own ad?'"* That sentence says *how* the story produces the claim. Juxtaposition is not an argument; this is.

**Weakest move.** *"$155 million worth of Macintoshes in three months"* is uncited and the documented figure is a unit count. It's a load-bearing number sitting in a sentence with three other verified ones, which is exactly how an unchecked figure survives a draft.

<details open>
<summary><b>Full post</b></summary>

```
Apple's "1984" is considered the greatest commercial ever made. What's less discussed is that the board killed it, the agency LIED about selling the airtime, and Steve Wozniak offered to pay $400,000 for it out of pocket. Here's the full story (and what it taught me about getting bold work into the world):

In 1983, Chiat\Day created a Super Bowl commercial for the Macintosh launch that didn't show the computer. The whole thing cost $500,000 to produce, plus $800,000 in Super Bowl airtime they'd already purchased.

Apple's board saw the finished ad in December 1983 and hated it. 

Not a single outside board member liked it. 

They ordered Chiat\Day to sell off both time slots (a 60-second spot and a 30-second spot) immediately.

Jay Chiat, the agency's founder, told his media director to sell only the 30-second slot. She sold it. When it came to the 60-second spot, nobody at the agency tried very hard to find a buyer. 

The big slot went unsold, and the airtime was already paid for.

Wozniak (who had no formal role in the decision) offered to personally pay half the $800,000 airtime and Jobs offered the other half.

Two Apple marketing execs decided to run it. 

The board had said no, but the marketing team ran it anyway. 

The ad went on to air once during Super Bowl XVIII, generated $5 million in free press coverage, and sold $155 million worth of Macintoshes in three months. Advertising Age later named it the greatest commercial of the 20th century.

My takeaways:

1. The more approval steps bold work has to survive, the less likely it survives. The agency didn't ask for a second board meeting. If you believe in the work, think carefully about when in the process you're asking for permission...and whether you even need to.

2. When the argument isn't working, change what the decision means. Wozniak had no authority over marketing. But the second he offered his own money, the question went from "is this ad worth the risk" to "are we really going to let our co-founder pay out of pocket because we're too cautious to run our own ad?"

3. The board wasn't wrong. The ad didn't show the product, didn't list features, didn't include a price. Their objections were reasonable by every standard measure. The lesson isn't "ignore the client." It's that reasonable objections can still kill unreasonable work....and sometimes unreasonable work is exactly what's needed.

Is this survivorship bias? Maybe. 

For every "1984" that broke through, there's probably a dozen bold ads that got killed and deserved to be. 

But I'd rather work in a system where risky work has a chance of surviving than one where it's guaranteed to die in committee.

WDYT? 👇
```

## Post 311: ANTI-PATTERN, one reading, seven times

**300 words · fact-check: MAJOR: Bought 1977, not 1978. The '7.5 degree kitchen floor' is invented (the tilted element was the glass cube skylight). 'Stopped taking corporate work entirely' is FALSE, Santa Monica Place 1980, Loyola through 1984. No successful lawsuit. All seven 'here's what happened' outcomes are retrofitted business-coaching narrative.**

**Why it's here.** The corpus's weakest post and its lowest performer (34 reactions), and the two facts are related.

**Read the seven headers in sequence:**

> 1) He stopped trying to please everyone
> 2) He alienated his corporate clients on purpose
> 5) He proved that distinctive work attracts the right clients
> 7) He learned that pushing away wrong people finds the right ones

**Items 1, 2, 5 and 7 are the same sentence.** There was one reading available and the shape demanded seven, so the writer restated. Then, because seven restatements need seven pieces of evidence and only one existed, the evidence was manufactured, which is precisely what the audit found.

**This is the causal chain worth internalising: too many readings forces invented support.** The fabrication wasn't a lapse of integrity; it was structurally required by a shape the material couldn't fill.

**"7.5 degrees" is the corpus's clearest instance of invented precision.** The gist is true, Gehry tilted things. The decimal is manufactured, and it was manufactured *for texture*, because unrounded numbers read as researched.

**The one good line:** *"Gehry proved that your weirdest ideas **might** be exactly what the right clients are looking for."* That "might" converts a general law back into a possibility claim, which is the only thing one architect's house can carry. Item 5 says "proved that distinctive work attracts the right clients" with no hedge and is false.

<details open>
<summary><b>Full post</b></summary>

```
In 1978, architect Frank Gehry was drowning in safe, boring projects that paid bills but killed his soul. So he bought a pink bungalow and did something that would either make or break his career. Here's what happened:

1) He stopped trying to please everyone

Gehry wrapped his house in corrugated steel, added chain-link fencing, and made the kitchen floor tilt at 7.5 degrees. While other architects were horrified, artists and film directors got what he was doing.

2) He alienated his corporate clients on purpose

The same work that drove away his "sensible" clients became a beacon for people who wanted something extraordinary. Within 5 years, he stopped taking corporate work entirely.

3) He turned his weirdest ideas into premium services

His new clients - museums, concert halls, foundations - didn't just pay more. They gave him complete creative freedom because they valued his distinctive approach.

4) He used controversy as marketing

His neighbor's lawsuit over the "ugly" house? Best marketing he ever got. Today that house is studied in architecture schools worldwide.

5) He proved that distinctive work attracts the right clients

The math isn't: Good Work = More Clients
It's: Distinctive Work = Right Clients

6) He made his constraints into features

Those tilted floors and metal walls weren't mistakes. They were signals to his real audience - people who wanted something nobody else could deliver.

7) He learned that pushing away wrong people finds the right ones

By refusing to water down his ideas, Gehry attracted clients who appreciated exactly what made him different.

---

Most of us live in a prison of our own making.

We water down our ideas to please everyone and call it "being professional."

But Gehry proved that your weirdest ideas might be exactly what the right clients are looking for.
```

# Chronicle

## Post 223: The densest chronicle, and a hook with three errors in it

**426 words · fact-check: MAJOR: He did NOT sell; WPP's 1989 takeover was hostile and he publicly opposed it. He bought Château de Touffou in 1966, 23 years earlier. It is 30 ROOMS, not 30 bedrooms. The 1948 firm was Hewitt, Ogilvy, Benson & Mather; the O&M name dates to 1964.**

**Why it's here.** Twelve numbered events and **every one of them carries something that isn't a job title**, an amount, an hour count, a physical object, a consequence. *"He worked 63 hours a week prepping meals for the hotel dogs."* *"He personally conducted 424 interviews."* *"He bought a $0.50 eyepatch from a drugstore."* That is what separates a chronicle from a résumé.

**And the hook contains three errors in nineteen words:**

> *"In 1989, David Ogilvy sold his agency for $864 million and bought a 30-bedroom French castle with the profits."*

Didn't sell. Bought the castle twenty-three years earlier. Thirty rooms, not bedrooms.

**Look at what's doing the damage: "with the profits."** The hook joins two real facts with a causal connective, and the connective is a third claim that nobody checked. **Any hook joining two facts with "and," "so," or "with the profits" is asserting a joint, and the joint is the part that's wrong.**

**Also note the eyepatch price.** This post says $0.50; post 323 in this same corpus says 50 cents; the documented figure is $1.50. Two posts, same wrong number, because both are copying the same secondary retelling. **The borrowed-story pool is small and heavily circulated, and the errors circulate with it.**

**The close is excellent and costs nothing:** *"Ogilvy didn't become the best despite being a chef, a spy, and a farmer. He became the best because of it. You can't write interesting copy if you have boring inputs."* Three nouns from the story, in the reframe. Paid.

1. In 1931, at age 20, he was thrown out of Oxford University. He couldn't pass the exams. His GPA was a disaster.

2. He moved to Paris and worked in the Hotel Majestic kitchen. He worked 63 hours a week prepping meals for the hotel dogs. He saw the head chef fire a man for plating a dish 2 minutes late. Learned that "standards" are the only currency that matters.

12. In 1989, WPP acquired his agency for $864,000,000. He retired to Château de Touffou in France.

Ogilvy didn't become the best despite being a chef, a spy, and a farmer. He became the best because of it.

## Post 50: How to write about someone you've never met

**260 words · fact-check: NOT AUDITED**

**Why it's here.** The model for distant material. Every claim that could be an assertion carries an attribution verb instead:

- *"**He's said many times** that he was embarrassed to ask."*
- *"**Jensen has said that**, had they held it, the stake would be worth around $1 trillion today."*
- *"around $1 trillion"*, hedged.

**The one quotation is a public utterance.** *"We're thirty days from going out of business"* is a line he opens all-hands with; it has been repeated for decades in public. Compare the closed-room quotes elsewhere in this corpus, the Mulally confrontation, the Rand meeting, the Blakely bathroom demo, all of which drew verdicts. **Public quotes survive audit. Closed-room quotes don't.**

**The numbers have the right shape.** *"from 100 employees down to 40"*, *"about thirty days of cash left"*, *"six months instead of the standard eighteen to twenty-four"*, *"a million units in four months."* Awkward, specific, sourceable. Nothing rounds to a marketing figure.

**The close is a restatement, not a reframe:** *"The most valuable chip company in history began with the three sentences its founder was embarrassed to say out loud."* It works because it points back at the post's own opening structure, three things, humiliating in order, rather than reaching for an abstraction.

<summary><b>Full post</b></summary>

```

First: the technology we promised you doesn't work.

Second: cancel the contract, because we'd just waste the rest of your money.

Third: we still need the final $5 million, or we vaporize overnight.

That was Jensen Huang. The customer was Shoichiro Irimajiri, then president of Sega.

Jensen had laid off more than half of Nvidia that year, from 100 employees down to 40. About thirty days of cash left.

He's said many times that he was embarrassed to ask. Irimajiri took a few days, then agreed to all three. The $5 million, converted to equity, kept Nvidia alive.

"We're thirty days from going out of business."

He's used it for thirty-three years.

The most valuable chip company in history began with the three sentences its founder was embarrassed to say out loud.

## Post 3: The six words that convert a fact into a reading

**267 words · fact-check: NOT AUDITED**

**Why it's here.** One line does all the licensing work:

> *"Here's what I take from it:"*

Everything before it is a claim about Joanna Griffiths. Everything after it is the writer's reading, which cannot be wrong in the same way. **Six words, and the back half of the post becomes unfalsifiable in the good way.**

Seven posts in this corpus have zero first-person pronouns, and four of those contain an invented outcome block. **A writer-absent post has no sentence available for "here's what I take from it,"** so its readings have to be asserted as facts about the world. The pronoun isn't decoration; it's what makes a general reading legitimate.

**And the reading is a permission claim, which is what n=1 licenses.** *"The term sheet runs both ways. You're allowed to use it."* Not "founders who disqualify investors outperform" (a population claim one case can't support). Not "she proved that…" Just: this is available to you.

**The mechanism is stated:** *"How an investor behaves when something about you is inconvenient for them is the most honest data you'll ever get about who they are."* That sentence says why the story supports the claim.

```
In 2021, Joanna Griffiths raised $53 million while pregnant with twins. Then she started rejecting investors who questioned her pregnancy. Here's the story:

Griffiths is the founder of Knix, a Toronto underwear brand she'd been building since 2013. She went out to raise while heavily pregnant. And in some of those rooms, the investors couldn't help themselves.

She didn't argue or try to win them over.

## Post 187: ANTI-PATTERN, the pivot that means the story didn't bind

**Why it's here.** It is the clearest instance of the corpus's most important failure tell.

The claim is *"business is one of the best ways to design your own reality."* Run the substitution test: any story survives that claim. Nothing about Carnegie's management-by-letter supports it specifically.

**And the post knows.** Look at what happens after the anecdote ends:
