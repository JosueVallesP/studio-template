# Type: borrowed story

Someone else's story, carrying the person's claim. Method by Josué Valles, built from 31 vetted posts, 19 of them checked against primary sources. Seventeen of those nineteen came back with a factual defect, and none of their writers thought they were making anything up. That number is why this type has a verification gate, and why the gate is not optional.

## What it's for

A true story about a real, recognizable person, company, campaign or moment from history, told so it proves something the person believes. The story brings the attention; the person's claim is why the post exists. Good for reach, and for a point of view people remember because it came attached to a story.

## Where it sits in the library

- **Hook moves:** mostly 3 (someone notable did something odd). Also 9 (the line someone said) for the stated-belief opening, 2 (here's the actual document) when the payload is a quote or a letter, and 10 (news, plus a take) when the story is this week's.
- **Structures:** S3 (the story arc) for one decision or a chronicle, S4 (the argument) for a refuted premise, S2 (the explained list) or S5 (the breakdown) for an annotated case.
- **Inside moves:** M8 (the subject's own words), M4 (the data line), M2 (the turn), M9 (the meaning line).
- **Inventory slots:** BORROWED, plus the OPINION it carries. Without an opinion of the person's, the story is trivia and the post fails the swap test.

What this file adds to card 3: the binding tests, the ceiling on what one case can prove, the six paths, the outcome-block rule, and the verification gate.

## When it fits, and when it doesn't

It fits when the person has a claim of their own and a real story that shows it. It doesn't fit when:

- The person has a story and no claim. Ask what it made them think, or offer it as a `/scout` find for later.
- The claim needs a general law and the person has one case. Bring in data with a source (see "What one case can prove") or make the claim smaller.
- The person was there. That's `owned-story`, unless they only watched from close by; then it's the Adjacent Witness path below, which is the safest one in this type.

## Facts and sources

Every fact about the story (a name, a date, a figure, a quote, an outcome) is `borrowed: verify` in the source map, with its source named on the row: a link, a book with the page, an interview with the date. The person's claim and anything about their own life follow `CLAUDE.md` rule 1 like any post. A detail the writer can't source doesn't go in as a flat statement; the gate below says what happens to it. When the story came from the person (pasted, told in the session), ask where they read or heard it. When the writer finds it, `/scout` is the tool, and it files the find as an outside entry with its source.

## Step 1. Bind the story to the claim

Every post in this type tells someone else's story and then says something. The craft is in the joint, and two relations look the same on the page:

- **The story proves the claim.** Take the story away and the claim has no support.
- **The story comes before the claim.** The person already held the claim, and the story is a warm-up act.

Three tests. They're independent; pass one strongly and there's a post.

1. **The substitution test.** Swap in a story from a different field. If the claim survives unedited, the story only comes before it.
2. **The shared-noun test.** The claim's working noun already appears inside the story, doing work. The tightest example in the corpus: the claim is "we often create rules to avoid having conversations", and the story is a director asking for a rule instead of having a conversation. Both nouns are in the story, so nothing needs arguing.
3. **The mechanism test.** Say in one sentence how the story produces the claim. Putting two things side by side is not an argument.

**The failure tell for this step: the post starts talking about why stories are useful.** One corpus post tells a Carnegie anecdote and, the moment it ends, turns to "I'm obsessed with reading about history. Why?" and never comes back to Carnegie. That pivot is what a writer does when the story didn't bind. If the draft explains why history is instructive, the story isn't.

## Step 2. What one case can prove

A borrowed story is a sample of one. It supports three shapes of claim:

1. **Possibility:** this can be done. A 55-year-old founded TSMC, so starting at 55 is possible.
2. **Mechanism:** here is how it worked in one case. An employee handbook worked as a shield against a conversation.
3. **Permission:** you're allowed to. "The term sheet runs both ways."

It doesn't support a general law, a population statistic, a ranking or a counterfactual. **The overreach tell is the verb "proved."** "Conant proved that high performance starts with high connection" rests on one CEO at one company. "Conant bet that" is true.

If the claim needs to be general, bring in data, and own it as hard as the story. One corpus post does the construction right (a claim about founder age, backed with population figures) and still drew a major defect, because the figure was wrong and had no source.

## Step 3. Pick the path

| The material is | Path | Material test |
|---|---|---|
| One person, one decision, one organization in a named bad state | **Decision and dividend** | Can you state the decision as one sentence a person made, and name the before-state with a figure you can source? |
| A belief the reader holds, and a case that contradicts it | **Refuted premise** | Can you write the belief in the words someone would actually say, not your paraphrase? |
| A story worth telling in full, with more than one reading in it | **Annotated case** | Do you have two readings that aren't restatements of each other, each pointing at a different detail? |
| A life or an arc, six or more events in order | **Chronicle** | Does every event carry something that isn't a job title: an amount, an hour count, an object, a consequence? |
| A sentence someone said, which is the whole payload | **Quote delivery** | Do you have the exact wording and can you name where it was said? If either is missing, this path is closed. |
| The person was there, met them, or it's their own organization | **Adjacent witness** | Can the person say in one line how they came to know it? (Ask; never assume.) |

The harder calls:

- **Decision and dividend is the high-risk path.** Six corpus posts, five checked, four defective, and always in the same place: the outcome block (step 4).
- **Annotated case is the only path that earns length,** because the readings pay for it. Two distinct readings is the floor, not a target. The weakest post in the corpus has seven readings and four of them are the same sentence; the shape asked for seven, the material had one, and the writer made up the rest. Too many readings forces invented support.
- **Quote delivery has the worst record:** both instances drew major defects, both over the quote. It's the smallest construction and the most dangerous, because the payload is the thing most likely to be wrong.
- **Adjacent witness has no defects on record.** When the person was in the room, there's nothing to get wrong that they can't correct; the risk is a dull post, not a false one. If this path is open, take it.

## Step 4. Draft

### The outcome block

The most consequential structure in this type: a colon-announced list of two or three results, ordered from a change in behaviour to a financial one to a superlative, with no actor, date or source on any line, sitting between the last story beat and the closing line. Six corpus posts had one; all six were checked; five were defective ("invented outcomes: the three numbered results have no source").

The block isn't banned. The one that survived had a **scope limiter** right above it: "This was the cornerstone of a broader turnaround built on 'people first' principles." That sentence says the habit was one part of a larger programme. Without it, the post claims handwritten cards turned around a Fortune 500 company, which is the claim the check destroyed in the near-identical post that lacked it.

**The rule: an outcome block gets a scope limiter above it and a source on at least one line.** Without the limiter it's a causal claim the post can't support.

### Hedges are where the writer noticed a limit

Two corpus posts are the same post (a CEO takes over a failing company, one humble habit, three results, a contrast line). One is 151 words and drew a major defect; the other is 225 words and checked clean. The 74 words between them are almost all hedges: "roughly 10 notes every single day", an opinion given to nobody in particular instead of asserted, and the scope limiter. Checked-clean posts ran about 2.25 hedges per 100 words; defective ones under 0.7. The sample is small, but the reason isn't statistical: a hedge is the writer noticing the edge of what they know. A post with none never noticed one.

### The hook is the riskiest sentence

The hook is where cause gets asserted. "In 1989, David Ogilvy sold his agency for $864 million and bought a 30-bedroom French castle with the profits" holds three errors in nineteen words: the takeover was hostile and he opposed it, he bought the castle twenty-three years earlier, and it has 30 rooms, not bedrooms. What does the damage is "with the profits", a joint between two real facts that is a third claim nobody checked.

**Any hook joining two facts with "and", "so", "after" or "with the" is asserting a joint, and the joint is the part that's wrong.** Two openings that avoid it:

- **The stated belief,** in the words someone would actually say: "I'm too old to start a company." A paraphrase ("many people believe they've aged out") is a sentence nobody recognizes as their own.
- **Two facts with an unexplained gap:** "Figma's stock is down 86% from its peak. Their revenue growth just accelerated to 46%." Two checkable facts and the tension between them, with no cause asserted.

### Put the person in the post

Seven corpus posts have no first person at all, and four of those seven hold an invented outcome block. That's mechanical: a post with no writer in it has nowhere to say "here's what I take from it", so its readings get asserted as facts about the world. One sentence of the person's reading turns everything after it into their view instead of a claim about the subject. It's also check 17 in the judge stage.

### The close

Five of six decision-and-dividend posts in the corpus end on the same contrast: "Most leaders try to command respect. Conant earned it, one note at a time." Only one pays for itself ("Most companies teach culture in a PowerPoint. Herb taught it in a mop bucket"), because "mop bucket" is a concrete object from the story; the others swap in an abstraction (respect, loyalty, learning) for the story they just told. **The test: the close carries a noun from the story.** The "most leaders" half is a most-people claim (house rule H6), so it goes in only when it's the honest frame; a close that names the object and stops usually does more. A close that turns on "not X, but Y" fails H17 whatever it holds.

### Length

Median 267 words, and the spread splits by path. Decision and dividend runs 88 to 225 words, one decision and one outcome. Annotated case and chronicle run 300 to 515, because the readings or the events pay for it. **A decision-and-dividend draft past 300 words has nowhere to put the extra length except invented outcomes.**

### Numbers

The shape of a number says where it came from. "100 employees down to 40", "about thirty days of cash", "424 interviews" are awkward and specific because someone wrote them down. The commonest single defect in the corpus is invented precision: "the kitchen floor tilted at 7.5 degrees" (the tilted thing was something else), "80% empty white space" (nobody measured it), "a 50-cent eye patch" (the documented figure is $1.50). The pattern: the writer knows the gist and supplies the decimal for texture. The gist is usually right; the decimal is where the post becomes false.

## The verification gate

The writer runs it before handing the draft to the judge, and records it under the source map; the judge runs it again (check BS1 below).

**Sort every specific into three tiers.** Mark every proper noun, date, figure and quotation in the draft.

- **Tier 1: the source can be named.** A link, a document, a book and page. State it flat; the source goes on the row.
- **Tier 2: confident, but no source to point to.** Hedge it or attribute it ("he's said many times", "roughly", "reportedly"), and mark the row `source: not found`.
- **Tier 3: reconstructed.** Cut it, or write it as reconstruction.

A Tier 2 or Tier 3 item stated flat is how every defect in the corpus got published.

**The seven ways it goes wrong, most frequent first:**

1. **Invented precision.** Ask of every decimal: who measured this, and where is it written down?
2. **The hook joint.** Two facts and a word of cause between them. The word is a claim.
3. **Quote marks around a remembered sentence.** Seven verdicts turned on this, and the pattern is exact: quotes from public statements survive checking; quotes from closed rooms don't. A line someone repeats at every all-hands is safe. A private confrontation is not. If it's a paraphrase, take the quote marks off; that costs nothing.
4. **The invented emotional beat.** "Jobs relented, smiled, and hugged him": the hug is invention. These get added because the scene wanted a beat.
5. **Wrong actor.** Check who did the thing, who bought the thing, who gave the award. One corpus post has the wrong acquirer inside a quotation, so the subject is quoted making the writer's error.
6. **Stale figures.** Market caps, headcounts and titles go out of date. They were true when someone wrote them down, which is why they feel safe.
7. **Timeline drift.** Four of seven checked posts that opened with a year had the wrong year. The year is decoration, so nobody checks it.

**Two checks that catch most of the rest:**

- **Read the hook against the draft's own quotations.** One corpus post's hook says "two hours a day" and its own quoted passage says "within an hour."
- **Count the hedges.** None at all is a warning on its own.

**Two cautions:**

- **The model not knowing a story is not evidence it's false.** One corpus post was flagged as made up by an automated check and turned out entirely true; the event came after the model's training. Check the source, not the flag.
- **The pool of business anecdotes is small and copied.** The same dozen stories recur on LinkedIn with the same errors, because everyone copies the same retelling. Two posts in this corpus give the same wrong price for the same eye patch. Five places repeating a fact isn't confirmation when all five come from one blog post. Go to the primary source or hedge.

## Checks the judge adds

On top of the stage's checks, each a FAIL with the line quoted:

- **BS1. The gate.** Every proper noun, date, figure and quote about the story has a `borrowed: verify` row with a named source, or is hedged and marked `source: not found`. A flat statement with neither is a FAIL, graded as "Outside example unverified" in the gates table in `skills/role-editor`.
- **BS2. Binding.** The post passes at least one of the three binding tests, and doesn't turn to why stories matter.
- **BS3. The ceiling.** No "proved", law, ranking or statistic resting on the one case; imported data has its own source.
- **BS4. The outcome block.** If there is one: a scope limiter above it and a source on at least one line.
- **BS5. The hook joint.** A cause word between two facts in the hook is sourced, or the hook is rewritten.
- **BS6. Quotes.** Every quoted line is a public statement with its source; a remembered or private line is unquoted.
- **BS7. The close** carries a concrete noun from the story.
- **BS8. Length.** A decision-and-dividend post over 300 words says what the extra words carry.

## Things that look like rules and aren't

Emoji, arrow bullets, dividers, closing questions, the "Here's the story:" preamble, second-person density, line-break rhythm, mixed list numbering. They vary freely across strong posts in the corpus. The house rules still apply to all of them.
