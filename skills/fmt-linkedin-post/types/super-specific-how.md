# Type: super-specific how

A post that hands the reader a procedure they can actually run. Method by Josué Valles, built from 42 vetted posts. The whole type turns on one question the reader asks at every line: **could I actually do that?** The moment the answer is no, they leave. The most useful finding: **16 of the 42 aren't procedures at all.** Their steps shuffle freely; they're power listicles with step numbers, filed as how-tos because the writer used the word "steps".

## What it's for

Leads and saves: a process, workflow, routine, prompt chain or method the person runs, written so a reader can run it on Monday. It proves the person has a method, not a personality. It's also the type to use when someone has a method and doesn't know whether it's really a sequence: the gate says so.

## Where it sits in the library

- **Hook moves:** 1 (proof, then payoff), when a result earns the procedure; 16 (do this right now), when the procedure is a check that takes minutes; 2 (here's the actual document) for an annotated exhibit; 5 (then and now) for a before and after.
- **Structures:** S5 (the breakdown), numbered steps; S2 (the explained list) when the steps carry reasoning.
- **Inside moves:** M1 (the context block), short; M5 (the side-by-side) for before and after, and for the wrong wording next to the right one; M4 (the data line).
- **Close:** C3 (the small instruction), as a check the reader runs on their own output.
- **Inventory slots:** METHOD first, then DOCUMENT (the artifact, pasted), RESULT, EXPERIMENT.

What this file adds to card 16 and S5: the sequence gate, five paths, what makes a step runnable, where the detail goes, and the test for whether a procedure was run or researched.

## Facts and sources

The procedure is the person's: the steps they run, the wording they use, the numbers they've seen, the failures they've hit. All of it comes from the four sources in `CLAUDE.md` rule 1 and is cited in the source map. The markers of a procedure that was run (a rate they measured, an exception they had to add, a person's name) are facts; never supply one to make the post feel lived. A named tool or path the person didn't give is `borrowed: verify` and is checked as current.

## Step 1. The sequence gate

**Swap two steps. Does a sentence somewhere become false?** If yes, it's a procedure. If no, it's a `power-listicle`, a different type, not a weaker how-to.

Two corpus posts fail this against themselves. One lists three changes as "#1 one idea per email / #2 killer subject line / #3 clear and concise", then reprints them in its own closing block with #3 in slot 2, and nothing breaks.

**Every post in the corpus with six or more items shuffles freely.** The crossover is five. At five or fewer, a step is an operation with an output; at six or more, it's a list item whatever the numbers say. Eight planned steps usually means a listicle.

A sequence needs one of three things: **an artifact** (step N makes something step N+1 uses); **a threshold** (stage N+1 can't start until stage N hits a countable condition); or **an imposed order** (the world, the page or the calendar sets it).

## Step 2. Route

| The procedure is | Path | Material test |
|---|---|---|
| A chain where each step makes what the next one uses | **Operation chain** | Can you name the thing that exists after step 1 and didn't before? Does step 2 point back at it with "the" or "that"? |
| A routine they run on a schedule | **Standing protocol** | Can they name the schedule, and say what done looks like as a condition they can test? |
| Stages climbed, each gated by a threshold | **Escalation ladder** | Can they name the number or the visible condition that says stage N is finished? If the gate is a feeling, this path is closed. |
| A before and after, with the changes between | **Annotated exhibit** | Can they paste the artifact as it is? Pasted, not described. |
| One governing rule, run once on their own case | **Rule and instance** | Is there the instance, with names? A rule without an instance is a slogan. |

A ladder's stages are positions, not tasks: tell the reader where they are before telling them what to do, and give each stage its own remedy (the same remedy at every stage is a list of rules with levels drawn on). **The annotated exhibit has a hard stop:** if the artifact is confidential or assembled from several, the path is closed. There's no version of it that works from a paraphrase.

## Step 3. Draft

### What makes a step runnable

Five things; a step needs most of them, and the ones it lacks are where it fails.

1. **The reader is the subject of an instruction with an object.**
2. **The object already exists:** a document, a tool, a person, a calendar block.
3. **There's a trigger or a schedule:** when, or how often.
4. **There's a done-test.**
5. **Often, the exact wording.**

**The strongest predictor is the stopping condition. A step that can't be finished isn't a step.**

**Steps that work on an outside object are concrete, without exception. Steps that work on the reader's idea of themselves are vague, without exception.** A PDF, a database export, a search result, a G2 listing, a calendar invite, a support-ticket archive: concrete every time. "Define your why", "establish your ethos", "think of your unique solution": vague every time. If a step's object is the reader's own mind, give it a surface, an input and a done-test, or it won't survive Monday.

**The grammar tell.** One post's steps 1, 2, 3 and 5 open with the reader as the subject ("Gather raw material", "Go for a walk"). Step 4 opens "The idea shows up on its own." The reader is the object of that sentence. **A step whose subject isn't the reader isn't a step**; it's a promised outcome put in a numbered list.

**Where the number goes missing.** The commonest failure isn't that the number was never known; it's that it lived in the story and fell out of the instruction. One post's story says "staring out the window for 20 minutes"; four paragraphs later the step is "Stare out the window and let your mind wander." Before finishing, check whether the setup holds a quantity the steps don't.

**The fix for most vague steps: promote the example to the instruction.** One post's instruction is "give them good ideas about how to improve it", with no channel, format or volume. Eight lines later the writer gives the runnable version by accident, in the third person: "If someone emailed us right now with ideas for amazing YouTube shows that were relevant to Clay, and proved they could produce them, we'd jump to work together!" The executable version was in the post the whole time, in the illustration.

**The synonym pile.** When a step gives five synonyms for an abstraction instead of five instances of it, nobody can run it. "Who you champion. Your beliefs, principles, and most of all vision. The hill you'll die on." The same post elsewhere gives a menu a reader can scan: "New tech, new communication channels, political change, shifts in pop culture." A reader can look at "siloed workflows" and think "that's us"; nobody can find their hill.

### Detail, ranked by what it buys

1. **Exact wording.** The only kind of detail that removes the writing step instead of describing it, and writing is where procedures die: a reader can accept "send them a message" and never send one. Every high-scoring post in the corpus quotes language; every low one fails at the point where language was needed. The densest form is a **slotted script**: "I do (your service) for (the clients you serve). I post every (day of the week) at about (time)." It gives the wording and marks where the reader's own material goes; the slots are the reader's to fill, named in plain words. Nearly as strong is **the wrong version, quoted**, because it's what the reader would otherwise write: "Move it inside the same week. Don't write 'I'll come back to you with a new time.'"
2. **A worked example on the person's own case,** right after the rule, so the reader matches the pattern instead of interpreting.
3. **A threshold:** the point where the reader would otherwise need judgement they don't have. "Once saturated, move to every Head of Marketing in San Francisco. Only then move to New York or Austin."
4. **A named tool and the path inside it.** Removes the search, but leaves the reader at a blank field, which is why it ranks below wording.
5. **A bare number.** Makes a step finishable, not performable.
6. **A time box.** Last for how runnable it makes things, first for getting a start: it turns "do I want this?" into "do I have twenty minutes?" State it at the top and again at the close.

### Name the failure inside the step

About half the corpus names a failure, nearly always as a premise before step 1 ("most people get this wrong"), which buys attention and helps nobody run it. **Put the warning at the step where it breaks, and say what breaking feels like.** The corpus's best: "most people quit here because it's uncomfortable... you're holding a bunch of disconnected information and nothing clicks yet. That discomfort is the process working." It gives the step a quality bar, proves the writer did it (only someone who has knows the feeling), turns a bad feeling into a sign of progress, and allows partial completion.

Almost nothing in the corpus says what to do when the procedure doesn't work. The one clause that does ("If they accept, great. If they reject, no bother") pre-approves the likeliest outcome of the most exposed step, in six words. No post ends on a contingency ("if X, then Y"); that's an opening, not a convention.

### Where the proof goes

A result before the steps is always a number and works as a credential. A result after the steps is usually a state, and it's stronger, because a result stated after is credited to the steps. **A number up front earns the right to close on a feeling.** With no result, the substitutes, most credible first: a worked instance; a time budget instead of an outcome ("fix it in an afternoon"); third-party proof; a prediction; a moral. **No result and steps that shuffle is a stance dressed as steps,** the weakest posts in the corpus.

### Length and count

Median 284 words and four steps. Depth per step falls as count rises: three steps run 55 to 115 words each; eight to ten run 15 to 25. **The failure zone is six to eight steps at 15 to 25 words:** too many for depth, too few for a complete chain. One post beats it at nine steps and 472 words by making each step a copy-paste string (a prompt is dense without being wordy).

**Match words to what the step costs, not to its neighbours.** In one ten-step post, "3. Buy it." is two words for the most expensive and irreversible step, between a fifteen-word step about a database export and a twenty-seven-word one about a DM.

**There's no word floor; there's a floor of about one particular per instruction.** A 183-word post in the corpus reads as fully run; a 191-word one reads as invented, because it has no particulars. Break lines at the step boundary.

### The handoff

About half the corpus closes on a restated saying, which gives the reader nothing to do. The strongest close pairs **a bounded cost** with **a check the reader runs on themselves**: "Then compare those five topics with what you actually post about. If they match, you win. Takes ten minutes." Or: "Upload your updated profile PDF and ask, 'Would a stranger understand what I do in 5 seconds?'" A self-run check means the reader no longer needs the person's judgement to grade their own work (C3).

## Run or researched

The check that decides whether any of it is believed. Compiled procedures are smooth and complete. **Procedures that were run are lumpy, and have holes the writer points at.** The markers, strongest first, all of them facts from the person's sources:

- **Residue:** detail with no teaching purpose that wouldn't survive an outline. "About a third say yes. That's plenty." "Every invite needs a clear agenda, contact phone numbers, 20-minute default timing." Nobody designs phone numbers into a meeting spec; they're there because a call once failed.
- **Shaped by contact:** an exception no clean design would produce. "You have a hard cap of 30,000 LinkedIn connections. So, whitelist the accounts most valuable to your business."
- **The shape of the numbers:** "12 people when we first hit product-market fit", "about a third" read as measured; "guarantee your first 100 customers", "3x your conversion" read as asserted.
- **Where the attention points:** at the reader's likely failure at the step, not at the outcome.
- **The limit of what they know:** "I haven't done this, but if you asked each of our 35 remote team members..."

**At least one thing that costs the person:** a number worse than round, a name, an exception they were forced to add, a rate they measured, a stated limit. One carries more weight than a hundred more words of correct instruction. When the material holds none, ask: "Where does this usually go wrong when you run it?"

## Checks the judge adds

On top of the stage's checks, each a FAIL with the step quoted:

- **SS1. The reorder test,** on the finished draft. Steps drift toward independence while writing; if two swap freely, it's a listicle.
- **SS2. Count.** Six or more steps that don't shuffle need saying why.
- **Per step:**
  - **SS3. The photograph test:** could someone photograph the evidence it was done (a document, a calendar entry, a sentence in quote marks, a clock time)? A step that leaves only a state to be in is a principle.
  - **SS4. The subject test:** the reader is the subject.
  - **SS5. The stop test:** the reader knows when they're done.
  - **SS6. The composition test:** a step that leaves the reader a sentence to write writes it for them.
- **SS7. Particulars:** one per instruction, at least. No quantity in the setup missing from the steps. No step costing far more than its words.
- **SS8. The close** hands over a check, not a moral.
- **SS9. Run, not researched:** at least one costly marker, from the sources.

## Things that look like rules and aren't

Emoji, dividers, DM-me lead captures, P.S. lines, the closing saying (half the corpus does it, and it's the type's biggest gap), and how often the post says "you". A distinction drawn as "it's not X, it's Y" fails house rule H17 here as everywhere; this type has three better places for the same work: the wrong version quoted, the worked instance, and the failure named inside the step.
