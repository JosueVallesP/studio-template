# Exemplars

> Reference for the `owned-story` type (`../owned-story.md`). Method by Josué Valles. The posts quoted here are other people's, kept as they were published: learn the move, never copy the wording or the tics (dashes, stacked fragments, labelled takeaways). Every number, name and example in a post the studio writes comes from the person's sources or is a real case marked `borrowed: verify`; the figures in this file show the level of detail, never material to reuse.


Ten complete posts from the source corpus, grouped by path. Read the ones for your path before drafting.

They differ as much as possible on the surface while sharing a construction. That's deliberate, three posts making the same move in visibly different ways can't be copied, so you have to infer what they share, and what they share is the path.

Every one has a flaw noted, including the strong ones. Knowing where a good post is weak is more useful than a clean template.

Engagement figures are given for context, not as targets. Across the corpus, path does not predict reach.

# Located Moment


## Post 64: LOCATED MOMENT

*180 words · 436 reactions · 155 comments (36% of reactions) · 3 reposts · Text*

**Why it's here:** the highest comment-to-reaction ratio in the corpus (36%). It inverts the default order, the interpretation lands *before* the payoff. "Something interesting happens when you don't take shit from people" comes at 49%, and only then do we learn he apologised. That converts the next sentence from an event into a demonstration, so the reader arrives at the rule already convinced.

Also note the antagonist is never named, and "Did I make a mistake? Perhaps." is left unresolved, the admission is still open at the end, which is what generates argument in the comments.


> I once fired a client on the spot after he literally screamed “GO MAKE ME A NEW LOGO” over voice notes. 
> 
> I was still doing brand identity work in 2019.
> 
> I felt the chemistry with this client was already a bit off. 
> 
> When I presented a logo concept that looked like another company’s, he just lost it. 
> 
> Did I make a mistake? Perhaps. 
> 
> But no money is worth being yelled at. 
> 
> I ended the collaboration and offered a refund. 
> 
> Something interesting happens when you don’t take shit from people. 
> 
> He apologized, explained his behavior, and asked to finish the project together.
> 
> After adding some boundaries, we successfully finalized the project. 
> 
> I needed the money back then. 
> 
> But if there’s one thing I’ve learned over the years, it’s to never let people treat you like shit because you’re desperate, poor, or greedy. 
> 
> Since then, I follow a rule:
> 
> If I don’t want to have dinner with them after 5 minutes of talking to them, I’m not going to work with them. 
> 
> Save your talent and time for people who deserve it.

# Deliberate Practice


## Post 8: DELIBERATE PRACTICE, terminated

*218 words · 12,258 reactions · 824 comments (7% of reactions) · 130 reposts · Text + Visual*

**Why it's here:** the model of an honest bounded run. The question is stated at 9% and paid at 75%, then re-paid at 100%. The prior uncertainty is explicit and specific, "It was a three-day weekend. People were traveling." The reframe is a negation: "The experiment wasn't really about popsicles." And it closes *open*, escalating to a next run.

The hedge is the part to steal: "Based on one afternoon in our front yard, I think the answer is yes." n=1 stays n=1 and is still useful.

**Its one flaw, worth knowing:** the two-hour payoff is narrated entirely in categories, "Some stopped by for a few minutes. Others stayed much longer." No neighbour, no name, nothing anyone said. All the concrete capital is spent in the setup, so the paragraph that should be most vivid is the vaguest in the post.


> I had a simple question: if I invited my neighbors over for a popsicle, would anyone actually show up?
> 
> For those of you who know me, I tend to overthink things. This year I've been pushing myself to learn by doing instead of just thinking. I had no way to know whether anyone would come unless I extended an invitation, so I decided to do just that.
> 
> Last Tuesday, I walked around our neighborhood and left invitations on 80 neighbors' doors, inviting them to stop by for a popsicle and say hello. I wasn't sure what would happen. It was a three-day weekend. People were traveling. People already had plans. Maybe nobody would come.
> 
> Instead, neighbors flowed through our front yard for two straight hours. Some stopped by for a few minutes. Others stayed much longer. We met people we'd never met before despite living only a few houses apart. A few neighbors even brought flowers and cards, which was unexpected and kind.
> 
> The experiment wasn't really about popsicles. It showed me that people want to know their neighbors. More importantly, it answered a question that overthinking never could. Is there appetite for something bigger? A potluck. A block party. A neighborhood park event.
> 
> Based on one afternoon in our front yard, I think the answer is yes.


## Post 229: DELIBERATE PRACTICE, ongoing

*287 words · 138 reactions · 12 comments (9% of reactions) · 0 reposts · Text + Visual*

**Why it's here:** three consecutive mechanism sentences, which is what the ongoing branch lives or dies on. Golfers tee off at sunrise to be home by eleven; single players get paired with strangers; the round takes four hours. Without that paragraph there is no turn at all.

Also the specificity pattern: the practice is stated with total precision ("the most expensive public golf course I could afford", "every Saturday at 6 AM"), then the lesson goes deliberately empty, then it *re-specifies as hypothetical*, "If your ideal clients play chess… If they run marathons." Concrete grammar, empty referent, reader fills it.

And a three-beat restatement of the turn: negative form, positive form, sensory form.


> In 2012, I moved from Tokyo to LA with three kids and only $20,000 in my bank account.  I didn't know anyone, so I joined the most expensive public golf course I could afford and started showing up every Saturday at 6 AM.
> 
> Here's what I found out: 
> 
> Successful American business people (the kind of people I needed to meet) play golf from sunrise on Saturday mornings. They tee off early so they can be home by 10 or 11am for their families. 
> 
> Many people who play from that early come as a single player and are paired with 3 other strangers. You get to meet new people every week.
> 
> I'm not a great golfer, but that wasn't the point. 
> 
> The point was to put myself in a room (or on a fairway) with the right people, for enough time to have a real conversation. A genuine four-hour conversation about life, family, work, and whatever else comes up between holes.
> 
> Over six months, I built the core of my LA network on that golf course. 
> 
> Investors, advisors, potential clients, future partners (people who would never have taken a cold call from me but were happy to chat on the back nine).
> 
> The lesson isn't "go play golf." 
> 
> The lesson is this: stop trying to network through channels designed for networking. Conferences, LinkedIn messages, cold emails (those are saturated). 
> 
> Instead, find where your target community already gathers for something they enjoy, and join them there. 
> 
> If your ideal clients play chess, find where CEOs play chess. 
> 
> If they run marathons, train for one in their city.
> 
> You'll never build the best relationships in a meeting room. 
> 
> You'll build them in the spaces where people let their guard down.


## Post 35: DELIBERATE PRACTICE, on the line

*246 words · 13,604 reactions · 1,325 comments (10% of reactions) · 136 reposts · Text + Visual*

**Why it's here:** it sits exactly on the termination switch, so it shows you where the seam is. It reports like a terminated run, "It's officially been 1 month… I have been surprised by several things", but declares permanence like an ongoing practice: "I plan on never going back." The month is a *reporting window*, not a boundary.

It classifies as terminated because all four findings are revisions of stated priors, which is a run's signature. If you have a permanent habit and need a run, this is the move: keep the habit, bound the report.

It also puts the reader inside a *future* version of the writer's experiment, "Once you have a dumbphone, you'll frequently find yourself as the only person in the room not on their phone", which is the most efficient position available for a should-I-do-this-too post.


> It's officially been 1 month since I switched to a flip phone.
> 
> And I gotta be honest, I have been surprised by several things:
> 
> - Everyone is more severely addicted to their smartphones than I thought. Once you have a dumbphone, you'll frequently find yourself as the only person in the room not on their phone. It's not just teenagers, it's parents and adults of all ages. It's like everyone is stuck in a trance. 75+ year olds might be the only exception.
> 
> - All the objections I previously had for getting a dumbphone have turned out to be overblown and/or solvable. My iPhone addiction had fed my brain excuses to not do this earlier. If you really want to make the switch, you can.
> 
> - I've felt embarrassed to pull out my flip phone in public at times, for fear of being different or drawing too much attention to myself. But I have learned to just own up to it. Most people end up saying something like "Oh, I probably should do that too."
> 
> - I am using my brain more. Even though my flip phone has Waze, I find myself memorizing maps and roads. I'm more bored and get lost in my thoughts. I'm using paper and pen more. Increased desire for tangible things > digital things.
> 
> Overall, it has been a great experience and I plan on never going back.
> 
> Happy to answer any questions for those wanting to make the switch 🫡

## Post 39: FIELD REPORT

*512 words · 935 reactions · 169 comments (18% of reactions) · 41 reposts · Text + Visual*

**Why it's here:** the only strong Field Report in the corpus, so read it as the standard rather than as one of several.

The vantage is genuinely scarce (five days with frontier model teams), which is the thing the path runs on. At least one item could only come from being there: "The Obsidian community in SF is weirdly intense. Multiple founders showed me their vaults unprompted." And it closes by zooming from items to era.

Note the deliberately low ego, first-person density stays flat throughout at ~3%. The writer is a lens, not a character. That's why the path can carry fourteen unrelated claims without incoherence.

**Its flaw:** item 11 is filler and sits next to item 4, which is the best thing in the post. In a path where the list is the entire proof of noticing, one weak item is visible.


> I just got back from SF and I FEEL INSPIRED. I spent 5 days with frontier AI model teams and 3 billionaires. My takeaways:
>

> 3. Consumer AI is massively underbuilt. Every billboard in SF is either B2B inference infrastructure or vertical agent companies. The entire city is optimized for enterprise.

> 4. MCP came up in literally every conversation. The companies exposing their product as MCP endpoints are getting pulled into deals they never pitched for. The ones that aren't are becoming invisible to agents. This is the new SEO.

> 
> 7. The mood around open source shifted. A year ago it felt like open source was chasing the frontier models. Now founders are telling me Gemma and DeepSeek are good enough for 80% of what they need at a fraction of the cost.

## Post 402: REVERSAL

*255 words · 65 reactions · 21 comments (32% of reactions) · 2 reposts · Text + Visual*

**Why it's here:** the admission at maximum volume: "I was intolerant, arrogant, and on a bad day, cruel." 32% comment ratio, second-highest in the corpus.

The safety mechanism is precise and worth copying: the reader is never called an idiot, the writer is. "I just thought everyone on my team was an idiot. The reality? I was an awful manager." That's what lets a Reversal attack a belief the reader currently holds without triggering defence.

Note also the formal break at the turn, "The reality?" is a two-word question inside a run of declaratives.

**Its flaw:** it opens on a mission statement. Nothing is visible until 22% in, and its best line sits at 39%, well past the fold. Lowest reaction count in the corpus. Great body, wrong first line.
