# The Specificity Ladder

> Reference for the `proof-post` type (`../proof-post.md`). Method by Josué Valles. The posts quoted here are other people's, kept as they were published: learn the move, never copy the wording or the tics (dashes, stacked fragments, labelled takeaways). Every number, name and example in a post the studio writes comes from the person's sources or is a real case marked `borrowed: verify`; the figures in this file show the level of detail, never material to reuse.


Load while drafting any section of a proof post.

**One test runs through every section of this file:**

> Could this sentence describe almost anyone?

If yes, it's too generic, and generic doesn't just underperform, it actively fails. The reader can't see themselves in a description that fits everyone. They need to think *"holy crap, this is me"*, and that only happens at a level of detail most writers find uncomfortable.

---

## 1. The hook: summarise the transformation

**The rule: the before-state goes in the hook, not just the result.**

The commonest mistake is getting trigger-happy about the outcome and skipping where the client started.

| ✗ Result only | ✓ Before + result |
|---|---|
| "How I helped an Agency CEO sign 17 new clients in 90 days" | "How an Agency CEO, **recently burned by bad lead-gen agencies**, signed 17 new clients in 90 days" |
| "How I helped a solopreneur make $15k in 60 days" | "How a solopreneur **struggling with content ideas** signed $15k in new clients in 60 days without spending a cent on ads" |
| "How I helped an agency owner land 5 clients in 90 days" | "How an **overworked** agency owner, **drowning in proposals**, landed 5 high-ticket clients in 90 days using one email sequence" |
| "How I helped a designer sign $10k retainers" | "How a freelance designer **stuck charging $500 projects** broke through to $10k retainers in under three months" |
| "How I helped a SaaS founder generate $50k in 45 days" | "How a SaaS founder **who couldn't close leads from their content** generated $50k in 45 days without cold outreach" |

The hook gets longer. That's the trade, and it's worth it.

**Why the before-state is the non-negotiable element:** without it you're announcing a result to people who don't yet know it's for them. With it, you're saying *"I just helped someone in your exact situation get the thing you want."* That's competence and understanding in one line.

There's also a commercial consequence. The before-state is what pre-qualifies the enquiry. Attract people by result alone and you'll spend the DM conversation working out whether you can help them. Attract them by their *situation* and they arrive already matched to the offer that produced it.

### The six-component check

A fully-loaded transformation hook speaks to:

1. **One specific person**
2. **In one specific situation**
3. **With one specific pain**
4. **Who wants one specific outcome**
5. **In one specific timeframe**
6. **Without one specific obstacle** *(the "without…" clause)*

Every component you drop widens the funnel and loosens the filter. That's sometimes the right call, but make it deliberately, knowing the cost is qualification time later and, worse, signing clients your offer was never built for.

**Not a template.** Run it as a check against a drafted hook. Six clauses bolted together is not a hook, see the hook move cards in `../../library/hook-moves.md`.

## 2. The before and the obstacles

The hook gave a snapshot. This section goes deeper on the same ground.

**The generative question isn't "where were they before?" It's: what had they already tried that didn't work?**

That's the **sunken cost**, and it's what makes a before-state specific instead of abstract. Notice that every strong example below contains one.

| ✗ Generic | ✓ Specific |
|---|---|
| "They wanted to grow their business" | "They had spent **$65,857 on Facebook ads with zero return**" |
| "They struggled to get leads" | "They had been **ghosted by 9 potential clients in the last 50 days**" |
| "They felt stuck and didn't know what to do next" | "They were working **12-hour days** and still couldn't close enough deals to **pay their team of 6**" |
| "They struggled to find a good marketing agency" | "They had tried **two different marketing agencies**, neither delivered a single lead, and now they were hesitant to invest in marketing help again" |
| "They were busy and overwhelmed" | "They were working **14-hour days**, doing all the sales and admin themselves, and **missing time with their family**" |
| "They weren't getting results from social media" | "They had posted on LinkedIn **daily for six months** without a single enquiry, and were starting to think the platform didn't work for their industry" |
| "They were struggling to close deals" | "They spent **weeks writing proposals**, only for prospects to ghost them once they saw the price" |
| "They weren't connecting with their audience" | "They had **hired a full-time content writer** and were still missing the mark, because the messaging lacked clarity" |

**Why sunken cost works so well:** it carries the emotional weight, frustration, wasted money, damaged trust, *and* it disqualifies the wrong reader. Someone who hasn't tried and failed at this yet isn't ready to buy.

**Failure mode:** describing the before so unflatteringly the client would wince. Specific, not humiliating.

## 3. The steps

| ✗ Generic | ✓ Specific |
|---|---|
| "We reviewed their marketing" | "We created a new messaging strategy to clarify their value proposition and speak directly to their target audience" |
| "We helped them understand their audience" | "We ran an audience survey to identify their market's biggest challenges, then built an email campaign addressing those pain points" |
| "We improved their website" | "We redesigned their navigation, added a clear CTA on the homepage, and optimised the product pages for conversion" |
| "We helped them with ads" | "We implemented a retargeting strategy across Facebook and Google that captured warm leads and brought them back to complete purchases" |
| "We trained their sales team" | "We trained their sales team on a **4-step objection-handling framework**, which lifted close rate by 25% in two months" |
| "We worked on their email campaigns" | "We analysed their past campaigns, rewrote the subject lines, and ran A/B tests to optimise future sends" |

## 4. The results

Three distinct failure modes here, and most weak proof posts have all three.

**(a) Vague.**

| ✗ | ✓ |
|---|---|
| "They saw a big increase in revenue" | "They increased revenue by **$250,000 in 90 days**" |
| "Our strategy helped them close deals faster" | "We reduced their sales cycle **from 30 days to 10**" |
| "They saw more enquiries" | "They went from **2 enquiries a week to 10**, converting into 4 new clients in the first month" |
| "They had more time for client work" | "They saved **10 hours a week** by automating content scheduling" |
| "Their email campaigns performed better" | "Open rates went **from 15% to 35%**, driving an extra **$50,000 in sales** over three months" |

**A caution on the ✓ column above.** Specific does not mean *large*. The examples are big because the source cases were, but the governing rule is `../proof-post.md` (Open proof) **small beats big**: a precise modest result outperforms a spectacular one, because the reader is assessing likelihood-this-happens-to-me rather than impressiveness. *"They went from 200 impressions a post to 2,000"* is a strong result. Oversized numbers invite disbelief and read as bragging. Precision is the requirement; scale is not.

**(b) Emotional outcomes only.** *"They felt much more confident"* / *"they were thrilled with the process."*

Confidence and clarity are real, and they're **by-products** of a working process, they don't sell it. The reader wants to know what measurably changed. Emotional wins belong *alongside* a number, never instead of one.

**(c) Untied to the original pain.** This is the subtle one, and it's the most damaging.

> "We helped the client generate $100k in sales within three months."

Good result. But it floats. Why did they need help? Was the problem leads, a broken sales process, pricing? Without the tie-back, the reader can't see the journey, and the journey is the thing that makes them think *"I'm in that same situation, so if it worked for them it can work for me."*

**Close the loop you opened.** If the before-state was "$65,857 spent on ads with zero return," the result should be denominated in the same currency: what happened to the ad spend, or what replaced it.
