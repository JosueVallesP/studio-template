# Proof Assets: the six types, and how to get better ones

> Reference for the `proof-post` type (`../proof-post.md`). Method by Josué Valles. The posts quoted here are other people's, kept as they were published: learn the move, never copy the wording or the tics (dashes, stacked fragments, labelled takeaways). Every number, name and example in a post the studio writes comes from the person's sources or is a real case marked `borrowed: verify`; the figures in this file show the level of detail, never material to reuse.


Load when the writer says they have "a testimonial", or says they have no proof at all.

---

## The six types

Ranked roughly by how hard they are to get, not by how well they work.

### 1. Written testimonial
A LinkedIn recommendation, a review, an email. Strongest when it names a *result*, weakest when it praises *you*.

### 2. DM / message screenshot
A client message, screenshotted. Reads as unguarded and unsolicited, which is exactly why it works, nobody performs in a DM. Blur or crop the name if needed; the credibility survives anonymisation better than people expect.

**Always ask permission before screenshotting a private message.** Every time, no exceptions.

### 3. Video testimonial
Highest trust, hardest to get. See the elicitation questions below, an unbriefed video testimonial is almost always unusable.

### 4. Twitter / LinkedIn comment
The most overlooked source by a distance. Someone leaves a warm comment on your post, or replies to a thread, that's proof, already public, already attributable, and you don't have to ask for it. Screenshot it.

### 5. Case study
The full engagement written out. Most work, and the one most often wasted by being posted as a link instead of as the post itself.

### 6. Your own metrics and statistics
**The escape hatch when you have no client results at all.** Your own numbers, audience growth, revenue, output, an experiment you ran on yourself, are proof that you can do the thing. Not as strong as a client result, but real, publishable, and entirely yours.

If you're early and have nothing else, this is where you start. Do the thing, in public, and report what happened.

## How to get testimonials that are actually usable

Most testimonials are useless for a proof post, and it isn't the client's fault. Asked "would you mind writing a testimonial?", people produce warmth: *"X was great to work with, really knowledgeable, highly recommend."* That praises you and proves nothing.

**The fix is to ask questions instead of asking for a testimonial.** Send three or four, and let the answers become the testimonial:

1. **Where were you before we started?**, produces the *before*
2. **What was getting in the way?**, produces the *obstacles*
3. **What changed, specifically?**, produces the *result*
4. **What's different about your week now?**, produces the *lifestyle outcome*

That's the four-part structure of a proof post, asked as an interview. The client assembles the post for you without knowing it.

**For video:** send the questions in advance and ask them to answer one at a time. An unbriefed video testimonial is a person saying nice things into a webcam. A briefed one is a transformation narrated by the person it happened to.

**One more ask, if it fits:** request that they screen-record their own numbers while they talk. A dashboard on screen while the client explains what changed is the strongest single asset in this list, and almost nobody asks for it.

## Where else proof hides

Writers routinely say they have "no social proof" while sitting on all of this:

- Comments left on their posts
- A line in an email that was never meant as praise
- Public logos on their own website or deck
- Aggregate numbers, clients served, years in the field, people trained
- Their own results, from doing the thing to themselves
- A book, a talk, a podcast appearance
- Anything already published with permission

**Before accepting "I have nothing," walk this list.** Most people have five of these and have never counted any of them as proof.

## Formatting a proof post

These posts are longer than average and get skimmed harder, because the reader is scanning for whether they match. Formatting is doing real work here, not decoration.

- **Sort lists by length**, ascending or descending. It reads as deliberate and it pulls the eye down the page.
- **Group information in varied cluster sizes**, three, then two, then two, then one. Uniform grouping (3-3-3-3) reads as monotonous and, on LinkedIn specifically, as generated.
- **White space between every idea.** A paragraph over three lines on mobile is a wall.
- **Short sentences.** Full stops instead of commas where you can.
- **A colon at the end of the hook** signals there's more coming and earns the "see more" click.
- **The CTA gets its own block**, at the bottom, with space around it. Never bury a link mid-paragraph.

The house rules and `skills/craft-human` apply on top of these, and matter more on this type than on any other: sales-adjacent copy pulls toward machine cadence. Where a line here and a house rule disagree (stacked short sentences, a colon on the hook), the house rule wins.
