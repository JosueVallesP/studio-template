# Diagnosis: auditing a proof post, and when the post isn't the problem

> Reference for the `proof-post` type (`../proof-post.md`). Method by Josué Valles. The posts quoted here are other people's, kept as they were published: learn the move, never copy the wording or the tics (dashes, stacked fragments, labelled takeaways). Every number, name and example in a post the studio writes comes from the person's sources or is a real case marked `borrowed: verify`; the figures in this file show the level of detail, never material to reuse.


Load when reviewing an existing proof post, or when someone says their proof posts aren't producing enquiries.

# Part 1: The audit

Seven failure modes, in the order they usually appear. Each is a real defect with a specific fix, not a style preference.

### 1. The hook doesn't call anyone out
The most common and the most expensive.

> BAD: *"A 5 year old designed this project."*

Charming, gets attention, attracts nobody who can buy. The reader it needed, a business owner commissioning brand work, has no reason to stop.

> BAD: *"Here's what happens to the people who choose to work with me."*

"People" qualifies nobody and disqualifies nobody. It's too big to mean anything.

**Fix:** name the reader at situation-level. Not "people": *"director-level ecommerce brands"*, *"leadership coaches who are new to writing"*.

### 2. It's about you and your team
Five sentences in and the post is still listing what your agency was commissioned to do.

**Fix:** the client is the protagonist. You are the mechanism. If the first three sentences contain more "we" than "they", rewrite.

### 3. Information withheld behind a link
The post gestures at a case study and links out.

**The arithmetic:** if 100 people open the post, maybe 5 click the link. Write the case study *into* the post and 100 people read it. You have traded 95% of your readership for tidiness.

**Fix: give away the sauce.** Make it easy for *them*, not for you. If that means more work on your side, do the work, you're the one who wants something.

### 4. No transformation, just steps
> BAD: *"We had a call. I asked some questions. I wrote the posts. They loved them."*

That's a process, not a transformation. Nothing moved.

**Fix:** where were they, what was in the way, what's different now. If you can't answer all three, you don't have a proof post yet.

### 5. Feelings presented as results
A client says they're delighted. Nothing says anything happened.

> *"I could write a hundred posts, send them over, they're thrilled with the quality and the turnaround, and if they don't post a single one, they get nothing from it."*

**Fix:** emotional feedback is the *garnish*. It goes alongside a result, never instead of one. If the only available evidence is that someone was pleased, route to Cleared Proof and be honest about what it is.

### 6. The magic wand
> BAD: *"I gave her a magic wand to grant three things to change."*

What wand? Which three things? Vague mechanism is worse than no mechanism, because it advertises that there's something you're not saying.

**Fix:** name the actual move. The specifics are the proof.

### 7. No CTA at all
The post ends. Nobody is asked to do anything.

**Fix:** see `ctas.md`. Every proof post asks, within the channel's selling rule.

## The bar for a CTA

Read the closing lines and ask: **would the right reader feel stupid for not reaching out?**

> WEAK: *"DMs are open if you want to do this."*
> STRONG: *"DMs are open if you want a £19k pay rise in two weeks."*

Same offer. One states the mechanism, the other states the outcome. Only the second gives anyone a reason to move today.

# Part 2: When the post isn't the problem

Someone runs proof posts correctly for weeks, real results, quotes, a named client, a photo, and gets no enquiries. Before rewriting anything, check four things upstream. **Copy is the last place to look, not the first.**

### 1. Is the audience actually on this platform?
Some buyers don't live on LinkedIn. If yours are on X, in a trade association, in someone else's newsletter, or reachable only through search, then perfect copy on the wrong platform produces nothing. This is the possibility people skip because it invalidates the work.

### 2. Is the positioning right?
Are you talking about a problem this reader *has*, in the words they'd use? A well-written post about a problem nobody thinks they have converts at zero.

### 3. Is the problem big enough to act on?
Some problems are real and irritating and still not worth spending money on. If the pain doesn't clear the threshold where someone reaches for a budget, the post can be perfect and still produce nothing but agreement.

### 4. Is the profile carrying its weight?
The proof post's job is to send someone to the profile. If the headline, banner and featured section don't confirm what the post implied, the visit dies there. **A converting post with a vague profile behind it is a leak, not a failure of copy.**

**Say this plainly when it applies.** A writer who keeps rewriting hooks when the real problem is positioning or platform will work very hard and get nowhere, and will conclude that proof posts don't work, when they were never the variable.
