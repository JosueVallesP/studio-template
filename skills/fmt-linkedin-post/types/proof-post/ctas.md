## The reframe

A CTA is not a polite invitation at the end of a post. **It is a qualification instrument.**

Its job is to make exactly one kind of person feel addressed, and to make everyone else decide it isn't for them. A CTA that maximises the number of DMs is a badly designed CTA. A CTA that produces four DMs from four qualified people beats one that produces thirty from six.

The cost of an unqualified DM is not zero. It's a reply, a scheduling exchange, a call, and twenty minutes to establish what a well-built CTA would have established before they ever typed.

## The two moves

### 1. Name the criteria

State what someone must have or be for this to work. Plainly, as conditions.

> "If you're running a services business over £1M, you've got a leadership team of five to eight, and you've already tried an offsite that didn't stick , "

Three things happened there. The right reader ticked three boxes and felt seen. The wrong reader failed one and moved on without ever contacting you. And you established the shape of your client base to everyone else reading, which is how referrals get made.

**Criteria should be checkable, not aspirational.** "If you're serious about growth" excludes nobody, everyone thinks they're serious. "If you've got more than 10,000 followers" excludes precisely.

**Two to four criteria.** One doesn't filter. Five reads as a job advert.

### Laser targeting: the strong version

The more precisely you describe who you want, the more you can charge and the less time you waste. A worked example:

> *Here's who I'm looking for:*
> *- CEO/founder of a B2B service business (preferably an agency)*
> *- £3k to £10k per month offer*
> *- Making over £150k revenue per month*
> *- Currently posts on LinkedIn 3+ times per week*
> *- Believes in the power of writing online*

Five criteria, four of them checkable, one about belief. Everyone who fails one moves on without contacting you. Everyone who passes all five feels *seen*, and knows the service was built for exactly them.

That specificity is what lets you price at the top of your range. Criteria drawn from your best existing clients, not from who you wish would appear.

### 1b. Add urgency, not just scarcity

Scarcity says *there aren't many*. Urgency says *and not for long*. They are different levers and most CTAs carry only the first.

> "I'm looking to work with 3 clients, get in touch when you're ready."

Three spots is scarcity. "When you're ready" undoes it entirely: the reader thinks *I'll get to it*, and never does. Scarcity without a clock is a fact; scarcity with one is a reason to move today.

> ✓ "I'm opening 2 strategy spots this week. **DM me 'SAAS' before Friday.**"
> ✓ "Free workflow audit **for the next 24 hours**."
> ✓ "We start **10th April**, we move quick round here."

The complete CTA carries three things: **who it's for · how few there are · by when.** Drop any one and the other two weaken.

Same honesty rule as scarcity: if Friday passes, the offer closes. An urgency deadline that rolls forward teaches the reader that none of your deadlines are real.

### 2. Make the action tiny

The criteria do the filtering. The action should then be as close to frictionless as possible.

> "DM me the word COACH and I'll send details."

A single word in a DM outperforms a booking link, which outperforms a form. Every field is a drop-off. You have already qualified them, don't make them qualify themselves twice.

**Match the ask to funnel depth.** A reader eight posts deep can handle "book a call." A reader on their first can handle "comment 'guide'." Asking for a call from a cold reader converts nobody and makes the post read as desperate.

## Placement

**In the studio, the ask is the post's close** (C4, the open door, in `../../library/body-moves.md`), set apart in its own block at the end. The judge's check 14 fails a P.S. that is a pitch, so a P.S. ask goes in only when the person's own posts in `material/` end that way, and then it is the only ask. The reasoning below is why some writers use one.

**Why a P.S. can work.** It's visually distinct, it reads as an afterthought rather than a pitch, and it separates the value from the ask, which lets the body of the post stay clean.

> *P.S. Three spots on the autumn cohort. If you're a founder-CEO past Series A with a leadership team that's stopped escalating decisions to you and you'd like it to stay that way, DM me "COHORT".*

**One CTA per post.** Two asks halve both.

## Channel: match the privacy of the ask to the privacy of the problem

A CTA names an action *and* a place. Most writers only think about the action, and default to the comment section because it's better for reach.

**That default is wrong whenever the problem is one the reader wouldn't admit publicly.** A founder whose leadership team is failing, a CEO who has lost confidence in a hire, anyone whose enquiry would be visible to their own staff, board or investors, none of them will type "GUIDE" under a post their team can see. Ask them to and you have selected for the readers who *don't* have the problem badly enough to be discreet about it.

**Route these to a private channel: DM, or the email list.**

> ✓ "If this is the situation you're in, DM me, I won't ask you to talk about it in the comments."
> ✓ "The full breakdown goes to my email list on Thursday."

Naming the discretion explicitly is itself qualification: it tells the right reader you understand that the enquiry is sensitive, which is often the first evidence they have that you understand the problem.

**The trade-off is real, so make it deliberately.** Comment CTAs earn reach; DM and email CTAs earn qualified enquiries and cost distribution. The commercial question isn't which converts a higher percentage, it's whether the people you actually want can afford to be seen responding.

**The mixed case.** Where a post serves both, put the public micro-action in the body and the private one in the P.S., never two competing public asks.

## Scarcity

Scarcity works. It also has a failure mode that destroys the mechanism permanently, and most people trip it.

### The rules

**1. It must be real.** Genuine capacity limits: coach headcount, cohort size, a delivery calendar, an actual deadline. Not "three spots" chosen because three sounds urgent.

**2. You must close it.** This is the one everybody skips.

When the spots go, post that they've gone. *"That's the third one, cohort's closed."*

Skipping this is what kills it. A reader who sees "one spot left" in March and "one spot left" in June has learned that your scarcity is decorative, and they've also learned to discount everything else you claim. **Un-closed scarcity doesn't just stop working; it retroactively devalues every number you've ever published.**

Closing it does the opposite. It proves the constraint was real, and the next announcement carries genuine weight. The close post is also, conveniently, a second proof post for free.

**3. If you have no real scarcity, don't invent it.** Urgency can come from the reader's calendar instead of yours, a planning cycle, a fiscal year, a board meeting. *"If you want this fixed before Q1 planning, we'd need to start in the next fortnight"* is honest urgency and needs no invented spots.

### Scarcity most people overlook

Practitioner businesses often have real constraints they never mention because they seem mundane:

- **Delivery headcount.** N practitioners, each carrying M clients. That's an arithmetic ceiling and it's checkable
- **Calendar.** Anything requiring in-person time has a real diary limit
- **Cohort or programme structure.** Fixed start dates are constraints by design
- **Deliberate caps.** "I take four of these a year" is scarcity *and* positioning

If a real constraint exists, use it. It needs no theatre, stating the arithmetic is more persuasive than any urgency language.

---

## Failure modes

| Failure | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "Thoughts?" / "Agree?" | Engagement bait. Reads as such, and attracts commenters, not buyers |
| "DM me if you'd like to chat" | No criteria, no filter. Produces volume and no qualification |
| "Link in comments" with no reason to click | The reader has to guess what's there |
| Two CTAs | Splits intent, halves both |
| A CTA on every post | The audience stops reading past the value. One in five |
| Criteria nobody fails | Not criteria. "If you want to grow your business" filters nothing |
| Scarcity that never closes | Destroys the mechanism and the credibility of your other claims |
| Asking for a call from a cold reader | Mismatched to funnel depth |

## The reminder principle

The reason to run this weekly isn't only conversion. It's that **an audience which has never been told what you sell will refer you to nobody.**

Referrals need a sentence someone can repeat: *"she fixes leadership teams at scale-ups."* If you have never supplied that sentence, your most enthusiastic readers cannot pass you on, because they cannot describe what result you produce for whom.

The CTA is where that sentence gets supplied. Even the readers who never act on it are learning how to introduce you.
