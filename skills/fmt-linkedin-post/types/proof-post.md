# Type: proof post

The post that turns a result the person produced into an enquiry. Method by Josué Valles. Every other type builds an audience; this one converts it. The cost of not running it is specific: an audience that has read someone for a year and can't say what they sell, so it takes the free advice and refers them to nobody.

> "I have followed people for months on LinkedIn and I have no clue what they do. Because they don't show me. They only tell me."

## What it's for

Leads, and the sentence a reader can repeat when they introduce the person ("she fixes leadership teams at scale-ups"). It works on readers the rest of the feed has already moved from knowing the problem to knowing there's a fix; it converts nobody who arrives cold. How often to run it is the strategy's call (`canon/strategy/channels/linkedin.md` sets the mix and the selling rule). The method's own advice is one or two a week, about a fifth of output, in a fixed slot so it doesn't get forgotten.

## Where it sits in the library

- **Hook moves:** 1 (proof, then payoff), the main one; 5 (then and now) for a before and after; 2 (here's the actual document) or 9 (the line someone said) for a client message or testimonial; 18 (the announcement) when the post opens spots in an offer.
- **Structures:** S5 (the breakdown) for the steps; S3 (the story arc) for a client's story from start to finish; S2 (the explained list) for method proof.
- **Inside moves:** M1 (the context block) for the before-state, M4 (the data line), M5 (the side-by-side) for before and after, M8 (the subject's own words) for the client's line.
- **Close:** C4 (the open door), always. This is the one type where the post asks, within the channel's selling rule.
- **Inventory slots:** RESULT and METHOD first; LINE or DOCUMENT for a client's words; LAUNCH for capacity.

What this file adds to card 1 and S5: the mindset gate, the six kinds of proof and the honesty rules for each, the six steps, the targeted hook, and the CTA as a filter.

## Facts and sources

Every result, number, client descriptor, client quote, before-state, obstacle, step and capacity limit is a fact about the person's work, from the four sources in `CLAUDE.md` rule 1, cited in the source map. A feeling the client had comes from the client's own words in the material, never from the writer. Spots, deadlines and caps are real or they don't go in. When something is missing, ask; the questions under "The six steps" are the ones to ask.

## Stage 0. The mindset gate

Run it first, and out loud if the person hesitates. The blocker is almost never skill. It's one of six things: fear of seeming arrogant, fear of sounding salesy, thinking it's boring, forgetting, not knowing the shape, or confidentiality.

**The reframe: they aren't selling, they're reporting what happened.** The salesiness people fear comes from one avoidable move, making the post about themselves.

| Less of this | More of this |
|---|---|
| "No one does it better than us" | "Our client got X. Here's how" |
| "Our client couldn't have done this without us" | "This is how I helped my client get X" |
| "We're the best in the industry" | "My client was stuck on X, now they're Y. Here's what they did" |
| "Look at this amazing result" | (say what happened, in order, and stop) |

None of the right column is a claim; it's a report. The client is the protagonist and the person is the mechanism. The same reframe is what survives confidentiality: a report on what happened doesn't need a name, because the reader is checking whether the shape matches their own situation, not auditing a client list.

## Stage 1. What kind of proof they have

Ask what they have. Six kinds can carry the post, and they aren't interchangeable. **Ask the permission question early:** "Can we name them, and can we publish the number?" People assume yes and find out otherwise after the draft. For coaches, therapists, lawyers, advisors, ghostwriters and anyone who works with leadership teams, the answer is usually no.

| What they have | Kind | Section |
|---|---|---|
| A named client, a real number, and permission | **Open proof** | A |
| One real client and real numbers, no name allowed | **Anonymized proof** | A2 |
| Real results across many clients, none attributable | **Pattern proof** | B |
| A method with outcomes they can't publish | **Method proof** | C |
| Testimonials, logos, a book, credentials or their own metrics, already public | **Cleared proof** | D |
| No clients or results yet | **Projected proof** | E |

If they say they have none of these, walk the list in `proof-post/assets.md` ("Where else proof hides") before accepting it: comments on their posts, a line in an email, public logos, aggregate numbers, their own results. Most people have several and count none. If it's really true, projected proof is the honest construction; never stand one of the others up without the facts.

## The six steps

Every proof post runs this sequence; the three frameworks below weight it differently.

1. **Summarize the transformation** (the hook).
2. **Show what life was like when the client reached out.**
3. **Name the obstacles they faced.**
4. **List the steps the person took to get past them.**
5. **Show the result.**
6. **Ask** (the CTA).

**Step 4 is the one people skip, and it carries the post.** Steps 2 and 3 prove the person understood the problem and step 5 proves they solved it; without step 4 there's a before and an after with magic between, which reads as luck, and luck isn't something a reader can buy.

The questions that fill the steps, asked one at a time when the material doesn't hold the answer: "Where were they when they came to you?", "What had they already tried that didn't work?", "What did you do, in order?", "What changed, and by how much?", "What's different about their week now?" (The last two can come from the client; see the testimonial questions in `proof-post/assets.md`.)

Read `proof-post/specificity.md` while drafting: every step has a generic version that fails and a specific version that works, with worked pairs. The pairs show the level of detail; the numbers in them are illustrations, never material.

### Three frameworks

- **The journey** (a video testimonial, a long engagement with a clear arc): hook names the reader → show where it ends up, early → the client's journey → the change → life now → a plain ask.
- **The message** (a client message, short unguarded proof, a capacity announcement): hook names a desired outcome → what happened → the real limit on the service → who it's for and who it isn't → the ask, with the outcome and the deadline.
- **The problem** (a written testimonial, a one-off session, explaining a service): hook names the reader → what the client's problem was → the service as the answer → the change, in the testimonial's words → the ask.

## Part 1. The hook

The hook move is card 1 or 5; read the card. What this type adds is calibration: **the hook turns away as many readers as it attracts.**

**The before-state is never optional.** "How I helped an agency CEO sign 17 new clients in 90 days" announces a result to people who don't know it's for them. "How an agency CEO, recently burned by bad lead-gen agencies, signed 17 new clients in 90 days" tells the right reader it's their situation. The hook gets longer; take the trade. It also does commercial work, because the before-state qualifies the enquiry before the DM.

**The six-part check** for a drafted hook: one specific person, in one situation, with one pain, who wants one outcome, in one timeframe, without one obstacle. Each part dropped widens who responds and loosens the filter. Sometimes that's right, but decide it, because the cost arrives later as calls with people the offer was never built for. Run it as a check against a hook already written, never as a form to fill: six clauses bolted together isn't a hook, and fails the hook card's "how it fails" list.

## Part 2. The before and the obstacles

**The client's before is the reader's now.** The reader is checking whether the starting point resembles theirs.

Cover where they were, concretely; how it felt, in one line, from the client's own words; and what stood in the way. The question that makes a before-state specific is **what had they already tried that didn't work?** "They struggled to get leads" describes everyone; a named amount spent on ads with nothing back describes one person and every reader who has done the same. It carries the weight and it filters: someone who hasn't tried and failed yet isn't ready to buy.

The obstacles carry the post. The reader is asking "are these my obstacles, and did they get past them?" A before with no obstacles reads as an easy client and tells the reader nothing. A short list beats a paragraph.

**Failure mode:** a before so unflattering the client would wince to be recognized. Specific, never humiliating.

## Part 3. The steps

**It has to look like a system, not a scattergun.** The failure here is randomness: a loose list of things done, or a list of the service's features, makes the outcome look situational. Every engagement is tailored, but the reader needs to see an approach underneath. Number the steps, name the phases. This section answers "how does it work?", the first question in almost every enquiry, to everyone at once.

**Give away more than feels comfortable.** Readers who can run it themselves were never buying; readers who realize they'd rather not are the clients. Write the case into the post instead of linking out: of 100 people who open it, maybe 5 click a link.

## Part 4. The result

### A. Open proof

Named client or client type, real figures, permission. Three layers: the number; what changed for them, in their words; what's different about their week. Most writers stop at the first.

- **Exact, never round.** "174% in 60 days" reads as measured; "over 100%" reads as marketing.
- **Small beats big.** The reader isn't judging how impressive it is; they're judging how likely it is to happen to them. A 30% gain they believe beats a tenfold one they don't. Don't lead with the best result ever.
- **Three ways it fails:** vague ("thrilled with the results"); feelings only ("they felt more confident", which belongs beside a number, never instead of one); and untied to the pain named earlier. If the before was money spent on ads with nothing back, the result is in the same currency.

### A2. Anonymized proof

One real client, real numbers, no name: the commonest case and the most often botched. **Strip the identity, keep the descriptor.** "My client got 100,000 impressions" loses the post; "My agency CEO client got 100,000 impressions" keeps it, because the descriptor is what makes the right reader think "that's me." Anonymize until a stranger couldn't identify the client, and not one word further. Two cautions: descriptors stack into identity (sector, headcount, funding stage and timeframe can name someone in a small market), and "a stranger couldn't tell" is the person's judgement, not the client's, so they clear it with the client anyway. If what survives is too thin for anyone to recognize their situation, use B or C.

### B. Pattern proof

For results the person can't attribute to anyone. The proof is the pattern and how often they've seen it, stated as a pattern: "I've watched this happen in eleven of the last fifteen leadership teams I've worked with." The count, the kind of client and the pattern all come from the person. Say plainly that it's a pattern across clients; readers respect it and it protects everyone. Never write one client with the details filed off (they'll recognize themselves), and never assemble a client who doesn't exist by mixing details or changing the sector: that's an invented client (`CLAUDE.md` rule 1). If a scene is distinctive enough that the people in it would recognize it, it can't be published; say so.

### C. Method proof

When no outcome can be published, the proof is the method, shown in enough working detail that its quality is plain. A buyer who can't verify results is really judging whether the person has a method or a personality, and publishing the diagnostic, the sequence and the actual questions they ask proves what a testimonial can't. Shape: the problem the method exists for → the sequence → the thing it catches that other approaches miss → what it costs to run. Give away enough that a capable reader could try it.

### D. Cleared proof

Already-public material: published testimonials, named logos, a book, credentials, aggregate numbers, and the person's own metrics. Their own numbers are the way in when they have no client results: doing the thing in public and reporting what happened proves they can do it. **One at a time.** Five stacked testimonials read as a brochure; one quote with the person's account of what the work involved does more.

### E. Projected proof

For someone with nothing yet. Turn the frame and prove understanding by planning for the situation: "If I were a {person} who {life before} and wanted to {outcome}, here's exactly what I'd do." The person, the situation and the outcome are filled from the reader the person serves (the audience file, or what they said). Then run steps 2 to 4 as usual; step 5 becomes the outcome they'd aim for and why it's realistic, stated as a projection. **Never let it slide into A.** A projection written in the past tense is a made-up case study. Keep the conditional. The first person who runs the plan becomes open proof.

## The honesty gate

**No invented numbers, no inflated outcomes, no borrowed results, no invented scarcity.** It's mechanics as well as ethics: professional circles are small and talk, and an inflated claim survives until the first client who bought on it gets less, then travels as a refund, a review and a story. If the honest result is modest, say it plainly; modest and precise does better than impressive and vague.

## Part 5. The ask

Read `proof-post/ctas.md` before drafting the close. In short: the ask is a filter. The right reader should feel called out, and the wrong one should rule themselves out before they ever write. The bar: **would the right reader feel foolish not to reach out?** "DMs are open if you want this" fails; "DMs are open if you want a £19k pay rise in two weeks" passes, when the £19k is the person's real result.

- Name two to four criteria the reader can check ("over 10,000 followers"), never wishes ("serious about growth"). Take them from the person's best existing clients.
- Scarcity only when it's real (headcount, calendar, a cohort date, a cap the person sets), and the person closes it publicly when it fills. Urgency can come from the reader's calendar instead ("before Q1 planning"), which needs no invented spots.
- Make the action tiny, matched to how warm the reader is, and match its privacy to the problem's: a problem nobody admits in public gets a DM or email ask, not a comment.
- One ask per post, at the end (check 14).

## When the posts aren't working

If the person says their proof posts don't produce enquiries, or hands one over to fix, read `proof-post/diagnosis.md`. It holds the seven-point audit and the four checks for when the copy isn't the problem: the buyers aren't on LinkedIn, the positioning names a problem the reader doesn't feel, the problem isn't big enough to spend money on, or the profile doesn't confirm what the post implied. Say so plainly when one applies; rewriting hooks won't fix a platform problem.

## Checks the judge adds

On top of the stage's checks, each a FAIL with the line quoted:

- **PP1. Kind.** The post is the kind of proof the material holds, and the format plan's TYPE NOTES line records the permission: what can be named and published.
- **PP2. Before-state** is in the hook.
- **PP3. Steps.** Step 4 is there, numbered or phased, as specific actions.
- **PP4. Result** is exact, tied to the pain named earlier, and stated with its source. A feeling is the client's words, beside a number.
- **PP5. Protagonist.** In the first three sentences the client appears more than "we" or "I".
- **PP6. Honesty.** No invented client (including an assembled composite), no inflated or rounded result, no scarcity or deadline the material doesn't record. Graded as check 18.
- **PP7. Projected proof** stays conditional throughout.
- **PP8. The ask** names checkable criteria, is the only ask, and fits the channel's selling rule.
- **PP9. Anonymity.** An anonymized client keeps a descriptor and isn't identifiable; a recognizable scene is flagged to the person.
