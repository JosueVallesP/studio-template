# Type: simple listicle

A list whose items point at things outside the post (books, tools, people, resources, things worth buying, the contents of something the person made), so the reader leaves with something to go and get rather than something to believe. Method by Josué Valles, built from 9 posts read against the power listicles they sit beside. It's the smallest corpus behind any type, and two of the nine are lead capture rather than posts, so most of this file is a boundary and a gate; where nine posts can't support a rule, it says so.

## What it's for

A reading list, a tool stack, people worth following, a roundup, a recommendations post, a table of contents. What the reader is really paying attention to isn't the items; it's that someone with this access sorted them. **A power listicle spends the person's thinking. A simple listicle spends their access:** to a sample, a budget, a stack, a room, a contact list.

## Where it sits in the library

- **Hook moves:** 12 (the numbered promise) on the annotated shelf; 1 (proof, then payoff) or 11 (the finding) when the hook states the sample ("I asked 200+ HR leaders..."); 18 (the announcement) for a contents page.
- **Structures:** S2 (the explained list) for the annotated shelf; S1 (the bare list) for the bare inventory and the contents page.
- **Closes:** C2 (the question back), as an invitation to add to the list; C6 (no close); C4 (the open door) only on the contents page.
- **Inventory slots:** a list of named things the person uses, recommends or sorted, with the sample or the access behind it (PATTERN, DATA or their own experience).

## The seam, before anything else

**Can the item's headline be true or false?** A power listicle item holds a proposition, a claim that lives entirely inside the post ("Fight the curse of knowledge"). A simple listicle item holds a referent, a pointer to something the post can't contain ("*Made to Stick* by Chip and Dan Heath"). Same book, same commentary length; the item noun is the whole difference.

**The part that decides it is "outside the post", not "true or false".** A post listing nine words to delete has headlines that can't be true or false ("Fine"), and it's still a power listicle, because the post already holds the word, the reason and the replacement. Nothing is left to go and get.

## The material gate

**Does the material name three or more things that exist outside the post, each of which the reader could go and get, consult or contact for themselves?** Named, three or more, each retrievable by the reader. It passes all nine simple listicles and fails every power listicle, including the hardest: a post of seven diagnoses names nothing retrievable; nine words to delete are already in the post; ten rules where one says to read a book name only one thing; an analysis whose hook names four companies names things the reader can't obtain.

"For themselves" matters. Without it, the gate passes a founder listing his own exits ("Vitamin Water: sold to Coke for $4.1B"), which is evidence for a claim, the subject's possessions rather than the reader's options.

A post can contain a simple listicle without being one: a power listicle that ends on a ten-book appendix is still a power listicle.

## Facts and sources

The items are the person's: what they read, use, recommend, or found when they asked a sample. They come from the four sources in `CLAUDE.md` rule 1. **Never add a referent the person didn't supply.** If the material names two books and the post needs five, the answer is three more books from the person, asked for in one question, not three from the writer. Naming a source nobody supplied is the same failure as inventing a number. A fact about the referent the person didn't give (a price, an author's credential, what a book argues) is `borrowed: verify` with its source; a price is checked as current.

## The three paths

**The annotated shelf** (4 posts, 371 to 437 words). Every instance in the corpus is a book list, which is an observation about nine posts, not a rule. A hook that states the count or the sample, ending in a colon → `N. Title by Author` on its own line → one commentary paragraph → repeat → a close inviting additions. Eight items is the median; headline 5 to 13 words; commentary 18 to 45 words, never two paragraphs, never a sub-list.

**The bare inventory** (3 posts, 67 to 261 words). No numbers, a bullet and a noun, commentary absent or on the same line after a colon. A short hook with no count → the list, one item per line → stop, or one invitation to add. Counts of 12, 22 and 4, never announced. The purest instance is 67 words with no commentary and no close.

**The contents page** (2 posts, 127 to 163 words). The referent is one thing the person made, and the list is its table of contents: a short title as the hook → one line naming the thing → what's inside → a sign-off and the ask. **Say it when you route here.** Both instances have more comments than reactions, which is a comment-gate effect, not readership: this is lead capture dressed as a list. It works, and it's a different object from the other two. It's an announcement (card 18), it asks once (check 14), and it runs only where the channel's selling rule allows.

## Inside an item

**The referent comes first, nine posts out of nine.** No item in the corpus opens with commentary and lands the name at the end.

When commentary is there (five of nine), it does one job: why this thing, for whom.

> 7. Turn the Ship Around by L. David Marquet
>
> A submarine captain's approach to creating leaders at every level. Instead of giving orders, Marquet created a culture where everyone took ownership. Perfect for leaders who want to scale themselves.

**Parentheticals carry two things, never a timeframe:** price and brand ("(Breville: about $1,000)", "(Equinox, Lifetime)"), or what an initialism stands for ("(BLUF: bottom line up front)"). In a list with no commentary the parenthetical is the commentary, and it's where the person's exposure lives: strip the prices from a taste list and the post costs its author nothing.

## Order

Order carries weight in four of nine. **It starts to matter the moment an item's commentary refers to another item.** Four mechanisms from the corpus, each broken by moving the item: a "ties it all together" item at the end, which means nothing unless the others come first; a correction ("Not quite a book, but..."), which has nothing to correct at position 1; a "the classic that keeps coming up" at 1 and a "this one dominated the answers" at the end, with neutral entries between; items that grow in length from top to bottom, where the longest is the only reason the last line reads as a close. A bare inventory with no commentary can be shuffled, unless the writer gave it a shape; check before moving anything.

## The opening and the close

**The opening** is a colon-stem in nine of nine. The count is declared only on the annotated shelf. Two moves recur, both about where the list came from: the sourcing line ("I asked 200+ HR leaders about the one book that changed how they hire"), in two of nine and both among the three most reposted; and the conditional frame ("If you're a venture-backed founder," "If I were raising an early-stage round in 2026,"), in three of nine. **What it cost never opens a simple listicle** (zero of nine). The "I paid $900 for this book" opener lives on the power side.

**The close.** Five of nine invite additions, all worded differently: the gesture is shared, the sentence isn't, so don't reuse one. An invitation to add is evidence for this type, not proof of it; run the gate, not the close. One post has no close at all, reaches its longest item and stops, and is second for comments in its set. A simple listicle is allowed to just end.

## What it spends

- **Curation work, shown.** The two most reposted non-gated posts are the two that state a sample size, and one spends the finding rather than hoarding it: "Zero traditional HR textbooks made the list." A list with no shown work is a photo of a shelf.
- **Taste, exposed.** Named brands, cities, prices. The post that spends most here has the highest comment ratio in the set: people argue with taste.
- **A recommendation that can be wrong, with the seam left in.** Two writers miscategorized their own items and printed the correction inside the item ("Not a traditional HR book, but HR leaders love it").
- **A relationship, named.** Listing twenty-two real investors is a public claim to a relationship with each. That's the cost.

**One pattern to plan around (nine posts, three writers, nothing causal):** lists of things people keep (books, named investors) got 73, 38, 36 and 28 reposts; lists of taste (consumer goods, internal tools) got 3 and 3, though one of those pulled 748 engagements and 217 comments. People save a resource list and argue with a taste list. If the slot needs reach through resharing, the items have to be things people want to keep.

## Checks the judge adds

On top of the stage's checks, each a FAIL with the item quoted:

- **SL1. The gate.** Three or more named things the reader can go and get for themselves. Items that are claims mean `power-listicle`; two books and eight opinions is an op-ed with a reading list stapled on.
- **SL2. Supplied.** Every referent comes from the person's sources. A referent the writer added is invented (check 18). Prices and facts about the referent are `borrowed: verify`.
- **SL3. Referent first** in every item.
- **SL4. Something spent:** shown curation work, exposed taste, or a named relationship.
- **SL5. Order.** A moved item doesn't break a reference to another item.
- **SL6. Commentary** stays one paragraph; a second paragraph or a sub-list is a power listicle item trying to get out.
- **SL7. Counts.** No count declared on a bare inventory.
- **SL8. The contents page** is named as lead capture in the format plan's TYPE NOTES line and asks once.
