# Type: power listicle

A numbered post where each item is a real lesson with something behind it, and the list is the content. Method by Josué Valles, built from 71 vetted posts.

## What it's for

Saves and followers: a body of work (rules the person lives by, lessons in the order they learned them, a process in stages, a book distilled) handed over in a form people keep. It's also the type to use when someone has a pile of material and doesn't know whether it's a list at all: routing says which kind of list it is, and the count step says when the material can't pay for the number.

**The boundary with `simple-listicle`.** Each numbered item here holds a proposition: a claim or instruction that lives entirely inside the post, so the reader finishes it with something to believe or do. A simple listicle's item holds a pointer to something outside the post (a book, a tool, a person) that the reader goes and gets. The corpus has both treatments of the same book: one item says "Fight the curse of knowledge", the other says "*Made to Stick* by Chip and Dan Heath". **The test before anything else: can the item's headline be true or false?** If not, it's a simple listicle.

## Where it sits in the library

- **Hook moves:** 12 (the numbered promise), which is weak alone and always sits on another: 1 (proof, then payoff) when the person's experience pays for the list, 2 (here's the actual document) when a book, ad or memo does.
- **Structures:** S2 (the explained list) for an annotated rule set and a staged ladder; S1 (the bare list) for a bare inventory; M5 (the side-by-side) carried down a whole list for a contrast rack; S4 (the argument) with a short list as its spine.
- **Closes:** C6 (no close) when the last item is the widest; otherwise the interaction close below. C1 (the restated principle) almost never.
- **Inventory slots:** METHOD, PATTERN, OPINION, DOCUMENT (a book or source being distilled), RESULT for the cost line.

What this file adds to card 12 and S1, S2: the five paths, the rule that the material decides the count, the item headline, the order, and the item test.

## Facts and sources

An item that rests on the person's experience (their number, their scene, their artifact) cites it in the source map. A list distilled from a book, ad or talk is `borrowed: verify` item by item, with the source named (book and page, the ad and its year); quote its wording exactly or don't use quote marks. The person's commentary on each item is theirs or the writer's reasoning, marked as such. Never add an item the material doesn't hold to reach a number.

## Step 1. Route on how the items relate

| How the items relate | Path | Material test |
|---|---|---|
| Each is an independent claim that pays its own way | **Annotated rule set** | For every row, can you write a second sentence that isn't a restatement of the first? |
| Each is one line; the meaning comes from the premise and the volume | **Bare inventory** | Are there ten or more rows at the same level, and a premise more interesting than any one row? |
| The order is the content | **Staged ladder** | Swap any two rows: does a sentence in the notes become false? |
| Two sets held against each other | **Contrast rack** | Does every trait have its opposite in the other column? |
| The list is a pivot inside an argument | **List-spined argument** | Is there one claim they'd defend under pushback, plus two to four parts that can't be separated from it? |

The harder calls:

- **A ladder needs one of three things:** a procedure they ran in that order, stages with a direction, or an order the world imposes. Without one, it's an annotated rule set whose items happen to be numbered. (A procedure the reader runs is `super-specific-how`.)
- **A contrast rack needs strict symmetry.** Eight rows against three is an op-ed with a straw man.
- **A list-spined argument leaves the type when the prose outweighs the list.** Then it's `op-ed` with a numbered paragraph.
- **If nothing routes** (the material is a set of things, not claims, or three rows and no premise), say so and say what's missing.

## Step 2. Let the material set the count

Do this before drafting; it decides whether the post works.

**The list body sits at 200 to 500 words whatever the count.** Across the corpus: 10 items at 26 words, 7 at 67, 5 at 74, 13 at 39, 22 at 21, all in that band. Count and depth are one budget spent two ways. So the count depends on **who pays for the evidence**:

- **An outside source pays** (a book, a canon, a named guest): each item only needs a gloss, so ten items at 20 to 30 words works.
- **The person pays** (each item needs their own number, artifact or scene): the ceiling is five to seven.

Get this backwards and you get the corpus's commonest failure: ten items announced, six paid for, four padded. Median count is 7, floor is 3 (below that the list stops being the spine), ceiling about 10 with real commentary or about 25 one-liners. The danger zone is 13: high enough to owe commentary, low enough to think it can be paid for.

**Ten pulls filler toward it.** Every case of a padded last item in the corpus is in a post that announced a round number. Announce the number the material pays for, then keep to it.

## Step 3. Draft

### Shape by path

- **Annotated rule set.** A cost or authority line → the count with a category noun → colon → items → the last item carries extra weight → a close that works on the whole list. Inside an item: a headline claim of three to eight words → **the turn**, one sentence that makes the claim non-obvious → an optional so-what. 25 to 80 words.
- **Bare inventory.** A premise with tension → a line saying what it cost to know this → one-line items, 5 to 25 words, no second sentence → a last line that lands, or nothing. In a list with no commentary, a parenthetical is the commentary: "(Breville: about $1,000)" earns its line; "premium bedding" doesn't.
- **Staged ladder.** A result or credential showing the sequence ran → a line saying the parts are one whole → the stages → one beat outside the list that pays the ladder off → a close saying what the sequence buys. Inside an item: the stage's name → what it is → **what goes wrong if you stay here.** That third beat is what makes it a ladder. Give the last rung extra words; it's thin in every ladder in the corpus, because there's no next stage to fall short of.
- **Contrast rack.** A named split → block A, bare one-liners → block B, the mirror → the line saying which column the person is in. Their own column goes last.
- **List-spined argument.** A position stated flat → a scene or quote that gives it a body → what goes wrong, in prose → the list, two to four items → a close that restates the position. The sharpest form takes a quoted line of four to eight words and gives each word its own item.

### The item headline

Median six words, and this is where the post is won: a scanner reads every label and about a third of the bodies.

- **Put the payload in the label.** A label that's a category noun ("Branding." "Priorities.") delivers nothing at the one point the post is sure to reach the reader. A label that's already a claim pays up front.
- **Category plus action** is the device that transfers best: "Concrete: use 'Velcro'." A variant fills the second half with a diagnostic question: "Deadlines: real dates, or a case of tomorrowitis?"
- **A two-part label with a contrast** ("Be quick, but don't be in a hurry") is in 17% of headlines, mostly in the sharpest posts: the reader's eye finishes the line. It works only when the item's body says why. A label in the "Not X. Y." shape fails house rule H17.
- **Questions as headlines are almost absent** from the corpus. A question delays the value, which is the category noun's failure with a cost on top.
- Under three words is a filing tab; over twelve is a sentence the eye can't take in at once.

### Order

- **Position 1** is the prerequisite the others need, or the item most likely to be argued with. Never a narrow tactic. Its job is to win agreement cheaply so the reader commits to the form.
- **The last position** is the widest item, the one that reframes the others.
- **The best item ends up buried, and the fix is marking it, not moving it.** Lists sag in their fourth fifth, where the writer's attention drained, and that's also where the most specific item often sits, because it took work and got written late. The repost decision is made by one item: position 6 of 10 survives if it's longer, shorter or shaped differently from its neighbours, and disappears in a perfectly even list. One corpus post has its best item (the only one with clock times, the only clear instruction) at 6 of 10 among items that all run 34 to 50 words. Zero reposts.

### Depth

Two modes: a saying at 10 to 25 words, or a short essay at 40 to 70. Readers read a change in depth as a signal of where the value is, so make it deliberate; the working cases put the long item last. Items under 20 words get about 0.018 reposts per reaction; items of 30 or more get 0.048, nearly three times as many. Comments run the other way. For reshares, buy depth; for a comment thread, hold some back.

### The opener

**Write it last, from the strongest item.** The corpus has a controlled test: two posts with the same body (seven points from the same old ad). One opens "Found this 1980 ad about writing clearly. 45 years later, it's still the best writing advice I've ever seen." The other opens "In 1982, a paper company paid to teach America how to write. The editor of Reader's Digest wrote the ad. 44 years later, it's still the most useful thing I've read for the age of AI slop." **286 reactions and 36 reposts against 4,771 and 489.** Three facts and a present-day stake, in eighteen more words.

The opener can do four jobs, and the best do three inside 40 words: what it cost the person to know this, the count (so the reader can budget), borrowed authority when the material isn't theirs, and why now.

### The close

Optional. A quarter of the corpus has none and ends on the last item, including several of the strongest. Where there is one, the only kind that adds something the list couldn't is the **interaction close**, which tells the reader the items depend on each other: "Skip obsession and your conviction crumbles. Skip word of mouth and distribution fails."

## Step 4. The item test

**Cover the item's body and read only its title. Write the body yourself. If yours matches, the item is filler.** An item earns its number by carrying something that can't be derived from its headline, and there are three ways to carry it:

- **Mechanism:** why the claim is true. "Give people a $1M budget and they'll spend $1M. Give them one week and it takes one week."
- **Specimen:** a proper noun, a number, a price, an exact quote. "Buffett wrote 'far worse' instead of 'significantly worse.'"
- **Reversal:** the wrong way beside the right way, both quoted. "We offer high-performance solutions" beside "our battery saves you 47 minutes of charging a day."

The test is relative: a one-line item in a one-line list is fine; the same line in a list of paragraphs is a hole. Length doesn't detect filler; the weakest item in one corpus post is its longest, 93 words declining to answer ("I've seen everything work: daily, three times a week, weekly, monthly").

Three more tests:

- **Swap the noun.** "There's always more to learn but don't burn yourself out" survives being about pottery: filler.
- **Sibling collision.** Past fourteen items, the failure becomes duplication. One 13-item post has three duplicate pairs, and its last item repeats its second.
- **The hedge item** is always the weakest, because it's the only one nobody can act on.

### Earned or assembled

Whether the list reads as hard-won or generated is in the text:

- **Placeholder plurals.** "Whatever", "and all that stuff", "etc." inside an item mark where a real example should be.
- **Round numbers.** "$443k in 8 months" was looked up; "millions", "10x" and "the vast majority" weren't.
- **No named boundary.** "This is somewhat stylistic because a good 'really' can add emphasis. But use sparingly." That sentence gives up the clean rule and buys trust.
- **Suspicious evenness.** The two most even lists in the corpus (ten items each, all within a 16-word band) read most clearly as written to length. Real knowledge is lumpy.
- **The stranger test.** Read the labels aloud: could a capable outsider with a search engine have written them? One corpus post's eight items are all true and well put, and none needed the twenty years its opener claims.

An earned list holds at least one item you'd have to have been there to write, one number that wasn't rounded, and one sentence that costs the person something.

### Cutting

Score every item and cut the lowest until there are eight or fewer: headline-only items with a category-noun label; items a stranger could have written (even, especially, when true); two items with one mechanism (merge them); an item of a different kind from its neighbours (make it its own post); an item that sums up other items.

**Cut whole items, never the evidence inside one.** Two corpus posts carry the same seven-item book distillation. One trimmed inside items to fit the budget, and its item about being concrete lost its only concrete thing. **742 reactions against 160.** Cut from the middle, where attention gave out first. The cuts don't vanish: two can merge into survivors as the second beat they lacked, and the sharpest one cut for not fitting can become the opener.

## Checks the judge adds

On top of the stage's checks, each a FAIL with the item quoted:

- **PL1. The seam.** Every item headline can be true or false. If they're pointers, it's `simple-listicle`.
- **PL2. The item test.** Each item carries a mechanism, a specimen or a reversal. Name the items that don't.
- **PL3. The count** is the one announced, and matches who pays for the evidence (five to seven when the person pays).
- **PL4. Path.** A ladder's order matters (swap two: something becomes false); a rack is symmetrical; an argument's prose doesn't outweigh its list.
- **PL5. Headlines** carry the payload; no bare-noun labels, no question labels, nothing in the "Not X. Y." shape.
- **PL6. Earned.** At least one item only the person could have written, one unrounded number, one sentence that costs them.
- **PL7. Order.** Position 1 is a prerequisite or the most arguable item; the last is the widest.
- **PL8. Sources.** Distilled items are `borrowed: verify` with the source named.

## Things that look like rules and aren't

A "TAKEAWAY:" header (in five of six uses, what follows restates); all-caps labels (they make scanning harder); unicode bold, bullet glyphs, emoji, P.S. lines, dividers; having a close at all; second-person address inside items.
