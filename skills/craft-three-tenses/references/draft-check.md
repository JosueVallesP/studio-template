# Checking a draft

The person has written something and wants to know whether it would be stronger reaching into another tense. The job is to notice, point, and suggest a small reach, never to rewrite.

## Step 1: Sort the sentences

Go through the draft and tag what each part is doing:

- **Then:** past events, credentials, old projects, how they learned something, who shaped them.
- **Now:** how they work, what they recommend, what they're doing this week, current clients and projects.
- **Future:** plans, experiments, predictions, who they want to become or work with.
- **None:** general claims or advice with no time anchor ("Good writing is clear thinking").

Watch for drafts that are mostly **None**. Advice with no tense at all usually reads as generic, because there's no person in it. That's often the most useful thing to point out: "This reads like advice anyone could give. One sentence about when you learned it would make it yours."

## Step 2: Name the pattern

Tell the person in one or two sentences which tense the draft sits in and which lines put it there. Quote the lines briefly.

Common patterns and what they usually need:

| Draft sits in | Usually reads as | A reach that often helps |
|---|---|---|
| Only Then | A story with no point for today | One line on how it shapes the way they work now |
| Only Now | Useful but impersonal | One line on where they learned it, or why it matters to them |
| Only Future | A claim with nothing behind it | One line on what they've seen that makes them believe it |
| No tense | Generic advice | A specific moment from their own work |
| Then and Now | Authority, often strong already | Sometimes a last line on where it's heading |
| Now and Future | Connection, often strong already | Sometimes a line of history to make the plan believable |

These are tendencies, not rules. If the draft works as it is, say so plainly and stop. A short Now post with a sharp tip doesn't need a backstory.

## Step 3: Suggest the reach

If a reach would help, offer one or two sentences, no more. Build them only from:

- what's in the draft,
- what's in the tense bank, the idea bank, the canon and `material/`,
- what the person has said in this session.

Each suggested sentence says where its fact came from (the bridge, the file, or "said today"), the way a source map would. Match the draft's voice: sentence length, formality, how they address the reader. Show where in the draft the sentence would go.

If nothing on file has what you need, ask one question: "Where did you first learn this? The moment itself would make a strong second line." Then write the sentence from their answer.

## Step 4: Feed the bank

If the draft or the person's answer holds something new (a story, a plan, a person), add it to the tense bank as a bridge or a loose end, with the draft as its source. Drafts are one of the best sources of bank material, because people mention things in passing they'd never list in an interview.

## What not to do

- Don't rewrite the post or produce a "new version".
- Don't add a reach to every draft just to have something to say.
- Don't invent a past or a plan to fill the gap.
- Don't change their wording outside the sentence you're suggesting.
