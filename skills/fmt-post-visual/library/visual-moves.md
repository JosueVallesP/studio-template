# Visual moves

Method by Josué Valles, the studio's author.

Built from a research file of reference visuals (7 carousel sets, about 40 reference infographics, 6 field guides, about 36 frames of the studio's own) and from the visual-philosophy method.

A visual move is what a picture does to the viewer, independent of how it's drawn. A 2x2 as a hand engraving and a 2x2 as a pink gradient are the same move in two styles.

This library is a grammar, not a catalogue. The moves at the end are the common recipes, but any picture can be built from the same parts, and when a new picture works it goes to `studio-notes.md` as a candidate move for the studio's author (this file is the studio's, and an update replaces it). Four layers, chosen in order:

1. **Marks.** The vocabulary. What a picture is made of.
2. **Relations.** What an arrangement of marks claims. The honesty layer.
3. **Plays.** Operations you can apply to any mark or any move. Where the variety comes from.
4. **Moves.** Recipes: marks plus a relation plus the usual plays, named because they recur.

Then two layers that don't change the meaning: **format** (what container it ships in) and **style** (the skin, which lives in its own skill).

The rule that holds everywhere: the picture must be fully legible on its own, with a concrete headline and labels in familiar words, even if nobody reads the post. In a strong picture you could delete every label and still see the idea; the labels name what the viewer already understood.

---

# Layer 1: Marks

Twelve things. Everything in the file is made of these.

| Mark | What it is | What it usually stands for |
|---|---|---|
| Dot | A filled circle, small | One thing, a person, a point in time, a position |
| Line | A straight stroke | A boundary, an axis, a connection, a baseline |
| Bar | A filled rectangle, long and thin | A quantity (its length), one item in a count |
| Box | A rectangle with a stroke | A container, a step, a cell, a state |
| Circle | A ring with a stroke | A set, a scope, a group |
| Arrow | A line with a head | Causes, becomes, leads to, then (one meaning per picture) |
| Curve | A line that bends | A quantity changing, a path, a shape of experience |
| Field | Many copies of one mark | A quantity by mass, repetition, a population |
| Figure | A small human shape | The reader, a person, scale |
| Panel | A filled rectangle, large | A frame for one state in a comparison, a surface |
| Label | A word or two on or beside a mark | What the mark is (use as few as the picture needs) |
| Symbol | A borrowed shape the viewer already knows (a mirror, a door, a ladder, a calendar, a chat bubble) | What it means outside the picture |

A mark on its own means nothing. The meaning comes from how marks are arranged, which is the next layer.

---

# Layer 2: Relations

An arrangement is a claim. Readers bring these readings with them, so a layout makes the claim whether you meant it or not. Pick the relation the idea actually has, and check that the picture doesn't accidentally claim another.

| The idea says | Arrangement | What the viewer reads without being told |
|---|---|---|
| One is bigger or matters more | Scale: the same mark at different sizes | Bigger is more |
| Things sit between two ends | Spectrum: a line with two labeled ends, marks placed along it | Position is where it falls |
| Things happen in order | Timeline: left to right (or top to bottom) | Left came first |
| One thing produces another | Arrow or chain | This causes or becomes that |
| Two things trade off | Two axes, a balance, a seesaw | Gaining one costs the other |
| Things share ground | Overlap: circles that intersect | The overlap is both |
| A choice changes the outcome | Branch | A decision sends you down a different path |
| Some things rank above or hold up others | Hierarchy: stacked layers, a pyramid, a tree, nested rings | Top or center matters most, or rests on what's below |
| One thing changes as another changes | Curve on two axes | The shape of the line is the behavior |
| Something repeats or feeds itself | Loop | It keeps happening |
| Parts move together or fail together | Interlock: gears, puzzle pieces, shapes that fit into each other | Change one and the others change; take one away and the whole stops |
| Two things measured on the same criteria | Two-axis plot | Position compares them |
| A thing is made of parts | Anatomy: one object with parts pointed out | The parts relate the way they sit |
| Things are the same except for one difference | Side by side: two panels, same frame | The one difference is the point |

Conventions the viewer brings, which the picture has to honor:
- Up is better or more. Left is earlier. Center is core. Inside is part of. Dark is without, light is with (knowledge, clarity). Bigger is more important.
- An arrow means one thing per picture. Decide which (causes, then, becomes) and keep it.
- Once a direction means something, it means it everywhere in the picture.
- The order of the headline is the order on the page: "what is said, not who speaks" puts what is said on the left.

The certainty check: clean lines claim settled fact. If the idea is a model or a guess, the picture should look like one (a sketchier line, "a way of seeing" in the caption, no fake measurements). A bar of exact length claims a number nobody measured.

# Layer 3: Plays

Operations you apply to a mark, a relation or a whole move. This layer is open: new plays get added when a picture uses one that isn't here. Most strong pictures use one or two.

**P1. Vary one property.** Repeat a mark and change one thing about it: size, count, length, position, darkness, smoothness. The eye compares instead of reading. Circles that grow when you avoid a problem and shrink when you face it. Fifteen bars for "what you said" next to one for "what they remember". A smooth textbook curve next to a jagged real one. The rule: one property. Vary two and the point is lost.

**P2. Highlight one.** Among many, one is different: an accent color, a filled cell, a marker dot, a solid unit in a field of outlines. Says "this is the one that matters" or "this is where you are". Used in most of the studio's field guides (the gold dot).

**P3. Grey the rest.** The inverse of highlight: everything muted except the part that matters. Stronger than highlight when the picture is busy.

**P4. Blank one.** Remove a part so the viewer fills it in. A row of five with the fourth missing. A sequence with the last step empty. Makes the viewer do the work and remember it.

**P5. Put the reader in it.** A small figure or a "you are here" dot. Turns a diagram about the world into a diagram about them. Works on ladders, paths, spectrums, scenes.

**P6. Add time.** The same picture at two or three moments, in a row. Two states read as a switch; three or more read as growth. "Consistency creates competence" as a row of wobbly shapes slowly becoming a circle.

**P7. Count it.** Numbers on the marks: how many invited, how many said yes, how many kept going. Turns a shape into evidence. Only with real numbers.

**P8. Flip it.** The same picture with the claim reversed, side by side, or the "honest version" of a familiar layout. What people think X is / what X actually is.

**P9. Zoom.** A detail of the picture repeated larger, with a line connecting them. Shows that the small thing is where the idea lives.

**P10. Stack.** Two moves in one frame, each with its own subhead. A curve on top, a table below. This is the field guide.

**P11. Borrow a symbol.** Use a shape the viewer already knows and let its meaning carry over: a mirror for self-reflection, a door, a ladder, a bank, a calendar with one day marked. Avoid the worn ones (a lightbulb for an idea) unless twisted.

**P12. Build a metaphor.** When no symbol exists, make one from a property the idea actually has: a pile of rough blocks and a finished colonnade with a gap between them for "the work you've done and the body of work it could be". Build from the real property, not just a resemblance.

**P13. Draw the feeling.** When the idea is an experience (dread, momentum, a sting), put it on a line the way the hand-drawn "creative process" chart does. Boxes and arrows lose a feeling.

**P14. Let the structure argue.** Make the arrangement itself the claim, so no caption is needed: three org charts where one is a clean tree, one a mesh, one everything pointing at one person.

**P15. Subtract.** Make the version, then remove one element at a time until removing one more would break the idea. The last version before that is the picture.

# Layer 4: Moves

A move is a recipe: marks, a relation, and the plays that usually go with it. Each card has:

- **Job.** What the viewer understands in two seconds.
- **Shape of the idea it needs.** The gate. Pick the move from the idea's shape, not from taste.
- **Recipe.** Marks and relation.
- **Labels it needs.**
- **Variants.** Different drawings of the same move.
- **Plays that fit.**
- **How it fails.**
- **Pairs with.** Hook moves and body structures from the writing library.
- **Examples.** From the Figma file.

## V1. The 2x2

**Job.** "There are four kinds, and I can find mine."
**Shape it needs.** Two independent variables, each with two values, producing four cases worth naming. If one axis doesn't change the answer, it's a list.
**Recipe.** Two lines crossing (two-axis plot). Four labels. Often a dot.
**Labels.** Both axis ends in plain words. A name in every cell.
**Variants.** Square with crossing lines. Four separate boxes with a gap. A diagonal dividing good from bad. One cell shaded. Axes drawn as arrows (low to high).
**Plays that fit.** Highlight one (the cell to be in). Put the reader in it. Grey the rest. Blank one (the cell nobody wants to name).
**How it fails.** Axes that aren't independent. A cell with no real example. Naming the good cell so it's the only one anyone could want.
**Pairs with.** The reframe (13), the common advice is wrong (6). Body: the argument.
**Examples.** "Is your content authentic?", "Two ways to build a reputation", "Say / Do / Think / Believe".

## V2. The curve

**Job.** "It doesn't go the way you expect."
**Shape it needs.** One quantity over time or effort, where the shape of the change is the point: a plateau then a jump, a dip before the rise. A straight line is not a move.
**Recipe.** Two axes (lines). One curve. Often a second, dashed curve for what was expected. A dot at the moment that matters.
**Labels.** Both axes. The two lines named. The marker named.
**Variants.** One curve. Expected vs actual. Two curves diverging from one start. A curve with a shaded region. Smooth vs jagged (P1 on smoothness).
**Plays that fit.** Vary one property (smoothness, slope). Highlight one (the knee). Put the reader in it. Add time (three small curves in a row). Draw the feeling.
**How it fails.** Invented data drawn as measured. Two curves that differ in several ways. No marker, so nowhere to look.
**Pairs with.** Then and now (5), a bet on the future (17), the pattern I keep seeing (8).
**Examples.** "The Plateau of Latent Potential", "The Growth Paradox", "You have to be willing to be misunderstood", "Validation creates results", "10x different vs 10x better".

## V3. The ladder

**Job.** "There are levels, and here's the one I'm on."
**Shape it needs.** Ordered states where each is later or better than the last, and the viewer can locate themselves. Not a procedure (that's the sequence).
**Recipe.** Boxes rising left to right (hierarchy, timeline). Labels on each. Often a figure.
**Labels.** Every step, in observable words ("people quote your ideas back to you", not "authority").
**Variants.** Stairs. Stacked blocks. A pyramid. A vertical spectrum with marks. Rungs on a literal ladder (P11). Seed to plant, each stage drawn bigger than the last (P11 and P6 together, for growth that takes time).
**Plays that fit.** Put the reader in it. Highlight one. Count it (how many reach each step). Blank one.
**How it fails.** Steps that aren't ordered. Too many to locate yourself. Abstract step names.
**Pairs with.** Proof then payoff (1), the pattern I keep seeing (8). Body: the explained list.
**Examples.** "The reputation ladder", "Start with what people already believe about you", "The 4 levels of employee retention".

## V4. The overlap

**Job.** "The thing you want is where two things meet."
**Shape it needs.** Two or three sets, each fine alone, and a named thing that exists only in the intersection.
**Recipe.** Two or three circles intersecting (overlap). Labels in each. A dot or a label in the overlap.
**Labels.** Each circle. The overlap, concretely.
**Variants.** Two circles. Three circles. Four circles around one center, when the thing in the middle really needs all four (rare; check each pair overlap means something). Four small Venns in a grid, each showing a contrast. One circle inside another (that's hierarchy, check the claim). Overlap shaded, rest outlined.
**Plays that fit.** Highlight one (the overlap). Grey the rest. Flip it (what people think the overlap is vs what it is). Stack (four Venns).
**How it fails.** The overlap is the only label, so the circles are decoration. Three circles for a two-set idea. Using it for a comparison.
**Pairs with.** The reframe (13), the pattern I keep seeing (8). Body: the argument.
**Examples.** "Why some experts are hard to replace", "Your story has three tenses", "The Genius of Contradiction", "The Co-Founder Venn Diagram".

## V5. The side by side

**Job.** "Look at the difference."
**Shape it needs.** Two things the same in every way but one.
**Recipe.** Two panels, same frame, same scale (side by side relation). The same marks inside, with one difference (P1).
**Labels.** One per panel.
**Variants.** Left/right. Top/bottom. Before/after with an arrow between. Two versions of the same chart. Two chat threads with the emphasis swapped. The honest translation (a jargon line and its plain version). Versus: two panels with the same rows and a "vs" badge between them.
**Plays that fit.** Vary one property (this move is P1 at panel scale). Flip it. Count it. Grey one side.
**How it fails.** The sides differ in several ways. The bad side is a straw man. More than two panels.
**Pairs with.** The common advice is wrong (6), the honest version (14), the reframe (13). Body: the argument; the side-by-side inside move (M5).
**Examples.** "What You Said / What They Remember", "What people think HR is about", "Feelings before and after you talk about them", "Avoiding it / Facing it".

## V6. The anatomy

**Job.** "Here are the parts, and what each one is for."
**Shape it needs.** One whole made of named parts that relate in space (a tree, a building, a body, a machine). The relationships carry the meaning.
**Recipe.** One object (often a symbol or a built metaphor). Lines from parts to labels (anatomy relation).
**Labels.** A name and a one-line meaning on every part.
**Variants.** A tree. A building in layers. A cross-section. A machine with the parts exploded. A map with pins. A head in horizontal sections (what someone thinks about, top of mind to back of mind). Puzzle pieces that only make a whole together (then it's close to the gears, V21; the difference is fit vs motion).
**Plays that fit.** Borrow a symbol. Build a metaphor. Highlight one (the part that's usually missing). Zoom. Put the reader in it (a figure for scale).
**How it fails.** Parts that don't relate. A metaphor that misleads at the second step. Labels so abstract the lines point at nothing.
**Pairs with.** The reframe (13), the pattern I keep seeing (8). Body: the explained list, the breakdown.
**Examples.** "Your work is a tree", "Your personal brand has three layers", "Data, sorted, arranged, presented, explained with a story".

## V7. The sequence

**Job.** "Do this, then this, then this."
**Shape it needs.** Three to seven ordered actions where the order matters.
**Recipe.** Boxes or dots joined by arrows, left to right (timeline, chain). A number and a verb on each.
**Labels.** Number plus short verb phrase per step.
**Variants.** Boxes with arrows. A numbered timeline. A cone narrowing to one point and widening again (many inputs, one concept, many outputs). A loop when the last step feeds the first (then it's V18). A staircase of steps (then it's close to the ladder; the difference is actions vs states). A timeline with pins at alternating heights. Over and under: one main thread with events branching off above and below it. Chevrons side by side, each one pointing into the next.
**Plays that fit.** Highlight one (the step people skip). Blank one. Count it. Add time.
**How it fails.** Steps that are categories, not actions. Arrows that don't mean "then". Twelve steps.
**Pairs with.** Proof then payoff (1), do this right now (16). Body: the explained list, the breakdown.
**Examples.** "Beliefs → Decisions → Business", "Value → Credibility → Opportunity", "Rebuild one offer in six steps", "Follow this 5-step process".

## V8. The rings

**Job.** "It goes in layers, from the center out."
**Shape it needs.** Concentric importance or distance: a core and what surrounds it, nearer and farther.
**Recipe.** Nested circles or arcs (hierarchy). Each ring labeled. Sometimes a table under it mapping cases to rings.
**Labels.** Every ring. A caption saying which direction matters.
**Variants.** Full rings. Half rings (arcs). Rings with a wedge cut to show a cross-section. A target. A target with arrows in it (hit, close, missed), when the point is how efforts land against one goal. Nested triangles (a core inside two layers).
**Plays that fit.** Highlight one ring. Put the reader in it. Stack (rings plus a table). Count it.
**How it fails.** Rings with no order. Too many rings. A center that's "you" with nothing said about it.
**Pairs with.** The reframe (13). Body: the argument, the explained list.
**Examples.** "The circles of influence", "How to write a mic drop strategic narrative", "How change is created".

## V9. The scale

**Job.** "Same thing, three sizes. Pick the big one."
**Shape it needs.** One kind of thing at increasing magnitudes, where the magnitude is the lesson.
**Recipe.** The same mark two to four times, growing left to right (scale relation; P1 on size).
**Labels.** One per size, in the same grammar.
**Variants.** Rings growing. Dots growing. Bars lengthening. Boxes nesting (small inside large). A mountain with a chip taken out of it (the tiny version of the big thing).
**Plays that fit.** Vary one property (this move is P1 on size). Highlight one. Count it. Add time (the thing growing).
**How it fails.** The sizes aren't the same thing. Growth that's decoration.
**Pairs with.** The reframe (13), the common advice is wrong (6). Body: the argument.
**Examples.** "Three things clients pay for", "Avoiding it / Facing it", "Network effects".

## V10. The field

**Job.** "This is what a lot of the same thing looks like."
**Shape it needs.** A claim about quantity or repetition where seeing the mass makes the point. One unit in the field can be the exception.
**Recipe.** A field (many copies of one mark) filling the frame. Often one unit different (P2).
**Labels.** One line. The field does the rest.
**Variants.** A wall of one word. A grid of dots with one filled. A crowd of figures with one standing. A grid of loops that all fail except one.
**Plays that fit.** Highlight one. Blank one. Count it (the number of units is the number). Grey the rest.
**How it fails.** No point of difference and no number. Text too small to read as text and too regular to read as texture.
**Pairs with.** The call-out (7), the pattern I keep seeing (8). Body: the body is the hook.
**Examples.** "When most specialize, generalists stand out", "It doesn't work" (a grid of loops, one ending in "it worked").

## V11. The path

**Job.** "This is the route, and here's where people get lost."
**Shape it needs.** A journey with named places, including the wrong turns. The shape of the path is the content.
**Recipe.** One curve that winds (loop, timeline). Dots at stops with labels. Often a figure.
**Labels.** Every stop. Start and end.
**Variants.** A winding road. A squiggle with peaks and troughs (the feeling drawn, P13). A maze. A loop that returns to the start. A line with a detour drawn dashed. Footprints along a dashed route, numbered.
**Plays that fit.** Draw the feeling. Put the reader in it. Highlight one (the trap). Add time.
**How it fails.** A path with no detours (that's a sequence). Stops that are moods, not places.
**Pairs with.** The admission (15), put the reader in the room (4). Body: the story arc.
**Examples.** "The Boredom Trap", "A creative process", "The valley".

## V12. The table

**Job.** "Here's the grid, find your row."
**Shape it needs.** Two dimensions where every cell has a real entry. If a column would be half empty, cut it.
**Recipe.** Lines forming rows and columns. Labels in every cell. One column or row highlighted when there's a "this one".
**Labels.** Row and column headers in plain words. Specific entries.
**Variants.** Plain grid. Header row shaded. One column shaded (the recommended one). Two columns only (a comparison table). A checklist (one column of boxes).
**Plays that fit.** Highlight one. Grey the rest. Stack (under a diagram). Flip it (two columns: what they say, what it means).
**How it fails.** Filler cells. More than five columns. A list dressed as a table.
**Pairs with.** Proof then payoff (1), here's the actual document (2), do this right now (16). Body: the explained list.
**Examples.** "Run it for relationships", "What to post from each tense", "Should you delegate it to AI?", the pick/flip/mix table.

## V13. The distribution

**Job.** "The action is in the middle, not at the ends."
**Shape it needs.** A population spread along one axis where the interesting part is a region.
**Recipe.** A curve (bell or hump) over one axis. The region shaded or marked. Labels at the ends and on the region.
**Labels.** Axis ends. The region. One caption.
**Variants.** A bell curve. A skewed hump. A histogram of bars. A spectrum with a cluster of dots in the middle.
**Plays that fit.** Highlight one (the region). Grey the rest. Put the reader in it. Count it.
**How it fails.** A fake distribution. The marked region is the whole curve.
**Pairs with.** The finding (11), the pattern I keep seeing (8). Body: the argument.
**Examples.** "Who opens doors", "Yerkes-Dodson Law".

## V14. The branching

**Job.** "One thing leads to many, or many lead to one."
**Shape it needs.** A tree or a network with a direction: one node that splits, or many that converge.
**Recipe.** Dots joined by lines, flowing one way (branch). Numbers on the nodes when attrition or growth is the point.
**Labels.** Node names. Counts when the point is a count.
**Variants.** A funnel (many to one), drawn as stacked bands with a count beside each. A fan (one to many). A prism: one ray in, several out. Many arrows into one circle. A hub with spokes out to what it produces. A signpost at a fork, one sign per option, each road ending in where it leads. A tree with greyed dead branches. A network with one node larger. A decision tree with yes/no.
**Plays that fit.** Count it. Grey the rest (the branches that died). Highlight one. Vary one property (node size = count).
**How it fails.** No direction. Too many nodes.
**Pairs with.** The finding (11), proof then payoff (1). Body: the breakdown.
**Examples.** "The numbers that matter", "The past / the future" (a tree, grey to green), "How to lead a category".

## V15. The scene

**Job.** "Feel the idea before you read it."
**Shape it needs.** A single idea with a physical metaphor that doesn't need labels. One claim in one line.
**Recipe.** A symbol or a built metaphor (P11, P12), usually with a figure for scale. One line of text. No leader lines.
**Labels.** One, at most two.
**Variants.** A figure before an object (an obelisk, an arch, a mirror). A landscape with one feature (a gap, a cliff, a door). An object alone (an hourglass, a mountain with a chip missing). A room.
**Plays that fit.** Borrow a symbol. Build a metaphor. Put the reader in it. Subtract. Let the structure argue.
**How it fails.** A metaphor that illustrates the headline literally and adds nothing. A scene so busy it reads as decoration. Labels added to rescue a weak metaphor.
**Pairs with.** The reframe (13), the call-out (7), the body is the hook. The carousel opener.
**Examples.** Rich Webster's plates, "Creative people need time to sit around and do nothing", "B2B is a hallucination", "The obelisk", "The gap".

## V16. The word

**Job.** "Read this, and nothing else."
**Shape it needs.** A claim strong enough to carry a frame alone.
**Recipe.** Type as the image. One to eight words. A mark.
**Labels.** None.
**Variants.** Caps filling the frame. One sentence with half in grey (P3 on words). A word repeated into a field (P1, then it's V10). A word with one letter different.
**Plays that fit.** Grey the rest. Highlight one (one word in accent). Flip it (the word and its opposite).
**How it fails.** Too many words. A weak line set big. Type that fights the words.
**Pairs with.** The call-out (7), the common advice is wrong (6), a bet on the future (17). Body: the body is the hook; carousel middles.
**Examples.** foundr's orange cards, "The big LinkedIn carousel shift", Joe Mutuku's grey cards.

## V17. The list card

**Job.** "Save this."
**Shape it needs.** A real list, usually borrowed, where each item is one line.
**Recipe.** A headline, an optional small plate, numbered labels in one or two columns.
**Labels.** The source when borrowed.
**Variants.** Two columns. One column with numbers. A grid of four columns (categories). Two lists side by side (then it's V5).
**Plays that fit.** Highlight one. Blank one. Stack (with a plate).
**How it fails.** Items too long to scan. Should have been a table or a sequence. No source on a borrowed list.
**Pairs with.** Here's the actual document (2), the numbered promise (12), proof then payoff (1). Body: the bare list, the explained list.
**Examples.** "How to write like David Ogilvy", "The four enemies that power great positioning", "Talent acquisition vs recruitment".

## V18. The cycle

**Job.** "It comes back around, and each stage sets up the next."
**Shape it needs.** Three to six stages where the last one feeds the first and the order matters. If nothing feeds back, it's the sequence (V7).
**Recipe.** Arrow bands in a ring (loop relation), a number on each, labels outside the ring. The center names what keeps repeating.
**Labels.** Every stage as something that happens, in the same grammar. One line in the center.
**Variants.** Four bands. Three or six. A plain circle with arrowheads on it. Bands that lock into each other like puzzle pieces. Recycling arrows (one turned back on itself).
**Plays that fit.** Highlight one (the stage where people drop out). Put the reader in it. Blank one. Count it (how long one turn takes, with real numbers).
**How it fails.** A cycle with no real return, drawn round because round looks good. Stages that are categories, not things that happen. Arrows that don't mean "leads to".
**Pairs with.** The pattern I keep seeing (8), proof then payoff (1). Body: the breakdown.
**Examples.** The cycle diagrams in the Visual elements kit (four bands, recycling arrows).

## V19. The loop

**Job.** "It goes in, goes round, and comes out changed."
**Shape it needs.** An input, a process that repeats, and an output that differs because of the rounds. With no way in and no way out, it's the cycle (V18).
**Recipe.** A box, an arrow in, a ring with one arrowhead, an arrow out, a box (loop and chain together).
**Labels.** What goes in. What happens each round. What comes out.
**Variants.** In and out on the same side. A spiral instead of a ring, when each round is bigger than the last. Several small loops in a row.
**Plays that fit.** Count it (the number of rounds). Add time (the output after one round, three, ten). Highlight one (the round where it changed).
**How it fails.** The output is the input with a new name. The loop is decoration on what is really one step.
**Pairs with.** Then and now (5), proof then payoff (1). Body: the story arc, the breakdown.
**Examples.** The in-and-out loop in the Visual elements kit.

## V20. The wheel

**Job.** "One thing in the middle, equal parts around it."
**Shape it needs.** A core that every part serves or comes from, and parts that are equal in rank. If the parts rank, use the ladder (V3) or the rings (V8).
**Recipe.** A circle in the center with a heavy line, equal segments around it, a number on each segment, labels outside.
**Labels.** The core in the center (a name and one line). Every part outside, in the same grammar.
**Variants.** Three to eight segments. Petals instead of segments. The core as a symbol. Labels inside the segments when they're short.
**Plays that fit.** Highlight one. Blank one (the part people skip). Grey the rest.
**How it fails.** Parts that aren't equal drawn as if they were. A core that's only a title and connects to nothing.
**Pairs with.** The reframe (13). Body: the explained list.
**Examples.** The segmented wheels in the Visual elements kit.

## V21. The gears

**Job.** "These parts move each other. Stop one and they all stop."
**Shape it needs.** Two to four parts where a change in one forces a change in the others: a dependency, not an order. If one happens after the other, it's the sequence (V7).
**Recipe.** Interlocking gears (interlock relation, P11), the one that drives the others drawn biggest, labels on the hubs or beside the gears.
**Labels.** Each part, and what it moves.
**Variants.** Two gears. A chain of three or four. One gear with a tooth missing (where it breaks). Puzzle pieces, when the point is fit rather than motion (V6).
**Plays that fit.** Highlight one (the driver). Blank one (a missing gear). Vary one property (gear size = how much it drives).
**How it fails.** Parts that don't depend on each other, drawn as gears because gears look like "a system". A list dressed as gears. Gears are a worn symbol unless the dependency is the point.
**Pairs with.** The reframe (13), the pattern I keep seeing (8). Body: the argument.
**Examples.** The gear diagrams in the Visual elements kit.

## V22. The foundation

**Job.** "This holds that up."
**Shape it needs.** One result that rests on a few supports, which rest on a base, where taking a support away brings the result down. If the parts grow out of each other, use the tree (V6).
**Recipe.** A roof, pillars, a base (hierarchy read as "rests on"), a label in each.
**Labels.** The roof (what's held up). Each pillar. The base (what it all rests on).
**Variants.** Two to five pillars. Only a roof and a base (two layers). One pillar cracked or missing.
**Plays that fit.** Blank one (the missing pillar). Highlight one. Put the reader in it (a figure on the steps).
**How it fails.** Pillars that don't actually hold up the roof. A roof label so vague that any pillars would fit under it.
**Pairs with.** The reframe (13). Body: the explained list, the argument.
**Examples.** The temple diagrams in the Visual elements kit.

## Making a move that isn't here

When the idea's shape matches none of the 22, don't force one. Run visual-philosophy: state the idea in one sentence, find its logic (a contrast, a transformation, a size difference, a relation that always holds), pick the relation from Layer 2, pick a play from Layer 3, draw it from the marks in Layer 1, then run the tests below. If it works, write the full card, in the format above, to `studio-notes.md` as a candidate move for the studio's author. Never add it to this file yourself.

## Tests for any picture

1. **Delete the labels.** With every word removed except the headline, can you still see the relation? If only rectangles are left, the picture is a diagram about the idea, not a picture of it.
2. **Stand alone.** Does it make sense to someone who never reads the post?
3. **Stranger.** Could someone who hasn't heard the explanation say the idea back?
4. **Wall.** Pinned up with no caption, would it bring the idea back a month later?
5. **Certainty.** Does any line, size or count claim a measurement nobody made?
6. **One meaning.** Does every arrow, direction and size mean one thing, the same thing everywhere?

# Formats

**F1. Single image.** One move, one frame. LinkedIn: 1080x1350 or 1500x1800 (portrait fills the most feed), 1500x1500 square. Substack: 1456x819 inside a post, 1456x1048 for the featured image (grid views crop it to 3:2). Exact sizes and crop notes live on the placements pages of the visual kit.

**F2. The carousel.** One idea per frame, every frame the same size, exported as one PDF (the kit's Carousel page has the six-slide set in 4:5 and square). The opener is slide 1: the headline and a one-sentence promise of what swiping gives the reader; slide 2 shows beside it in the feed, so it has to hold up as the second half of the opener. The ones that work alternate a scene or word frame with a text frame. Middle frames are V16 or short text with one small diagram. The last frame restates the claim or makes one ask. 4:5 vertical, big type, small diagrams.

**F3. The field guide.** One frame, two or three sections, each with its own subhead and its own move (P10). The studio's six reference field guides are this. The format that rewards the grammar most: two moves whose shapes match two parts of the idea, stacked, with a table at the bottom when the idea needs rows.

**F4. The plate.** A scene (V15) in a framed panel, title above, two or three short paragraphs below. The text-led variant puts the text first and the plate small.

# The visual kit in Figma

**What it is for.** The kit is what pictures are built with, not a set of forms. A picture starts from the idea (visual philosophy, then the layers above). The kit supplies the parts, the proven moves as worked examples, and the look.

**The master.** "Content Studio · Template Library (master)" (key in `studio.manifest.yaml` as `figma_master`). Every person gets a duplicate through `setup-figma`; a change to the kit is a change to the master.

**Pages.** Start here (a one-page guide for the person). Kit: the marks from Layer 1 as components (Mark/Dot, Mark/Line, Mark/Bar, Mark/Box, Mark/Circle, Mark/Arrow, Mark/Curve, Mark/Field, Mark/Figure, Mark/Panel, Mark/Label, Mark/Symbol, Mark/Marker), each with its meaning in the component description, and starter frames at 4:5 (1080x1350), square (1080x1080) and field guide (1500x1800), each a Headline, a Diagram area and a Caption. Moves (examples): V1 to V22 drawn once at 1080x1350, plus nineteen variants (other drawings of the same moves) in the last rows, each with a note on the job, the shape it needs and the plays. Plays: one 2x2 drawn five ways (highlight one, put the reader in it, grey the rest, blank one, add time). Carousel: a six-slide set (cover, point, point, diagram, statement, end) in 4:5 and square. Field guide and plate. LinkedIn placements and Substack placements: every place the look shows up beyond posts (profile photo and banner, infographics, newsletter cover and logo, link preview, company page; Substack logo, wordmark, welcome cover, featured image, email header, in-post image), each at the right size with the platform's crop notes and a Guides layer to hide before export. Brand: swatches, type specimens, logo slot.

**V18 to V22 and the variants (Oct 2026).** On the Moves page of the master, rows four to eight, built like V1 to V17 (Headline, Diagram 920x820, Caption, a note under each; Studio text styles; every fill and stroke bound to the Style variables; every label named by part, such as Stage 1, Part 3 label, Where A leads). They were picked from a third-party infographic kit ("Visual elements"): 24 structures out of 301 diagrams, the rest being list layouts in different containers. A faded fill uses layer opacity, because a paint bound to a variable ignores its own opacity.

**Variables.** Collection "Style", two modes. Neutral: `color/paper`, `color/ink`, `color/line`, `color/muted`, `color/accent`, `color/accent-2`, `color/panel`, `stroke/hairline`, `stroke/heavy`. Brand: the same names with the person's values, written by `setup-figma` from the design system or the intake brand. Every fill and stroke in the kit is bound to these.

**Text styles.** Studio/Display, Studio/Headline, Studio/Subhead, Studio/Body, Studio/Label, Studio/Small label, Studio/Caption. A brand swaps their font families; sizes stay.

**Layer names.** Every layer a skill fills is named for what it holds (Headline, Caption, Cell 1, Axis X, Step 3 label, Marker, Region label, Footer). In a built picture, rename each mark for what it stands for ("Clients who stayed", not "Dot").

**To restyle:** put values in the Brand mode and fonts in the text styles (`skills/setup-figma/references/apply-brand.js` does both), then set the frame or page to the Brand mode. The marks, the relation and the labels stay.

# Notes

**The gate is the shape of the idea.** Pick the move by asking what shape the idea has. An idea with no shape yet isn't ready for a visual.

**Most of the file is six moves** (curve, overlap, side by side, 2x2, sequence, scene). The studio's own frames are strongest where the references are thinnest: the ladder, the rings, the table.

**Most variety comes from plays, not moves.** The same 2x2 with "highlight one", with "put the reader in it", with "blank one" and with "add time" is four different pictures. When a picture feels stale, change the play before changing the move.

**The stage that uses this library** is `fmt-post-visual/SKILL.md`: given the post's hook move and the idea's shape, it picks the relation, the move (or builds one), the plays and the format; places the marks from the kit; hands off to the style skill; and runs the six tests.
