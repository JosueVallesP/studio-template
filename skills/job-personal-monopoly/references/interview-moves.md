# Interview moves

Read this before stage 1. It covers how to ask, how to handle the answers you get, and what a good exchange looks like. It sits on top of `skills/craft-interview` (the studio's moves, including move 9, the far-field anchor) and the pushback table in `skills/setup-brand-tree/SKILL.md`; where they say the same thing, they agree. `/building-your-message` uses this file too.

## The basic loop

For every question:

1. Ask one thing.
2. React to the answer before asking anything else. One line is enough: "That's a much bigger claim than you made a minute ago", or "That's the second time you've mentioned pricing."
3. Decide: dig, or move on.

Dig when the answer holds something specific and unfinished. Move on when the answer is complete or a second attempt has already failed.

## Probes that work

- **The number probe.** "How much, by when, compared to what?" turns a story into evidence. Drop it if they clearly never tracked the number, and don't make them feel bad for not having data. When a number comes with a hedge, say it back as a plain sentence (`craft-interview`, move 3).
- **The contrast probe.** "What would have happened if they hadn't worked with you?" This is the fastest route to the real value. People describe their work as tasks and their value as outcomes, and the gap between the two lives in this question.
- **The nobody-else probe.** "Why did they come to you and not to someone cheaper?" If they don't know, the positioning problem is now visible to both of you.
- **The second-time probe.** Ask for another example. The first one is usually rehearsed, the second is more honest, and the third is where the pattern starts to show.
- **The heat probe.** With the anger question: "What did that cost the people it happened to?" It turns a mild complaint into a position worth holding.
- **The stranger probe.** "If I said that at a dinner party, what would the person next to me ask?" Good for testing whether a phrase means anything.

## Handling what comes back

- **Vague answer** ("I help businesses grow"). Don't argue with it. Ask for a case: "Tell me about one business. What did it look like when you started?"
- **Category answer** ("I provide strategic value"). Ask what they did on a Tuesday. Concrete beats abstract every time.
- **Modest answer.** Some people flatten their best work into nothing. Watch for stories they tell fast and then move past, and go back to them: "Wait, go back. You rebuilt their whole onboarding in two weeks?"
- **Grandiose answer** ("I transform organisations"). Ask for the specific organisation and what changed. Write down what they can support and no more. The document has to survive a sceptical buyer.
- **A wish** ("I want to be known for AI strategy"). Note it, then ask what they've already done in that direction. If the answer is nothing, the document says the position is something they're working toward, and the first decision is getting proof.
- **"You decide."** Some people ask the studio to pick for them. Give them a draft and ask them to correct it. They'll happily correct what they can't generate. The final document is never the studio's invention with their name on it.
- **No idea.** They freeze. This is common enough to have its own section, and it isn't a failure. People who have never said their value out loud are often the ones who need this most.

## When they have no idea

Some people can't answer "what value do you provide", not because they have none but because they've never had to say it, or because they're inside it and can't see it. Familiarity makes your own value invisible: to you it's obvious, so it can't be worth anything.

Run this ladder in order. Each rung is cheaper for them than the one after it.

**Rung 1: Guess from their own material.** Build two or three specific guesses from what they've said, even in stage 0, and from `material/`. "You mentioned clients come back after their first project. From what you've told me, I'd guess it's one of these: the pricing work, the way you run stakeholder meetings, or that you've sat on both sides of the table. Which is closest?" Correcting a wrong guess is far easier than generating from nothing, and a wrong guess often provokes the right answer: "No, it's not the pricing, it's more that I..."

**Rung 2: Teach with examples, then ask again.** Open the curtain partway. Pick three or four value types that could fit them and describe each through a named example, never a label:

> Value comes in more shapes than people think. Some people provide knowledge others lack: Naval Ravikant isn't the best investor alive, but he explains investing and philosophy in a way nobody else does, and that explanation is the product. Some save others time: Wirecutter doesn't make anything, it reads two hundred reviews so you read one. Some connect: Reid Hoffman's value was never only his own companies, it was knowing which founder should meet which investor. Some stop others making expensive mistakes: Warren Buffett's letters are known less for stock picks than for warnings. Which of those sounds most like what people get from you?

A person shown labels picks the impressive one. A person shown Wirecutter says "oh, that one, I'm the person who reads the two hundred reviews", and that recognition is real. Pull the examples from `frameworks.md` and pick the ones nearest their world.

**Rung 3: Go behavioral.** If they still can't say it, stop asking them to. Ask about things that happened, because behavior doesn't need self-knowledge:

- What was the last thing someone messaged you to ask for help with?
- When you left your last job or client, what broke after you were gone?
- What do colleagues hand you because "you're better at this"?
- What do people apologise for asking you about, because they know it's an imposition, and ask anyway?
- At your last job, what did you do that wasn't in the job description?

The last-message question is the workhorse: everyone can check their inbox, and three real requests tell you more than an hour of asking someone what their value is.

**Rung 4: Homework, and mark it thin.** If all three rungs produce nothing, say so. "We haven't found it yet, and I'd rather not invent it. This week, message three people you've worked with and ask them one question: what did you get from working with me that you didn't expect? Their answers are stage 1. Bring them back." Then either continue with the later stages, marking value as the open question, or pause. A document built on rung 4 says plainly that finding proof is decision one. When they bring the answers, they go to `inbox/` or the session, and their clients' words go to `canon/strategy/audience.md` as quotes with their source.

The same ladder works in stage 2 when someone insists they have nothing unique. There, rung 2 uses the uniqueness examples in `frameworks.md` (the accountant who ran a kitchen, the person who read four years of support tickets), and the behavioral questions become: what do people ask "how do you know that?" about, what did your strangest job teach you that this one uses, and what have you seen a lot of that your competitors have seen none of.

## Worked exchange

An illustration of the length and the rhythm, not a real case. Short questions, real reactions, no lectures.

> **Studio:** Think about the last year. Tell me about one person who can do something now because you helped them.
>
> **Them:** I helped a client fix their onboarding.
>
> **Studio:** What was breaking before you got there?
>
> **Them:** People signed up and never came back. Something like 70% never got to the point where the product does anything useful.
>
> **Studio:** And after?
>
> **Them:** About half get there now. Took maybe six weeks.
>
> **Studio:** Why did they come to you for that, and not to a product designer?
>
> **Them:** Because I'd run support for four years before this. I'd read the tickets. I knew where people got stuck; I didn't have to guess.
>
> **Studio:** How many people who design onboarding have read four years of support tickets?
>
> **Them:** Almost none. Designers ask users what they want. Support hears what broke.

The last exchange is the whole positioning. It arrived because the interviewer asked one more question instead of moving to the next topic.

## Pacing

- Stage 0: the orientation, then two questions
- Stages 1 and 2: five to seven exchanges each
- Stage 3: five or six
- Stage 4: three or four
- Stage 5: one or two

Every stage the canon already answers shrinks to a read-back. If someone gives short answers and clearly wants speed, compress. If someone is giving you rich stories, stay longer. The exit tests decide when to move, not the count.

Between stages, one line of progress and one line of what you heard: "That's stage 1 done. What I heard: you sell certainty about where users get stuck, and you get it from support data nobody else reads."

## Tone

Curious, direct, warm. You're interested in this person's work, and you won't let them undersell it or oversell it. Think of a podcast host who has done the reading, not a consultant running a discovery call with a scorecard. Don't congratulate answers: constant praise makes the real thing worthless and makes people suspect you're being nice rather than useful. When they give you the detail only they could know, say that this is the detail that makes it theirs.
