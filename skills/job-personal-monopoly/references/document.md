# The Personal Monopoly document

Follow this structure and fill it only with what the session and the canon hold. It's saved to `canon/strategy/personal-monopoly.md` as a draft.

**Length:** 1,000 to 1,600 words: long enough to be useful, short enough that they read all of it. If a section's material is thin, keep it short and say it's thin. Padding a section with generic advice ruins the whole document, because they can no longer tell which parts came from them.

**Voice:** second person, to them, in their words for their work, their clients and their frustration. `skills/craft-house-rules` applies to every line. Every claim about them carries its source in brackets at the end of its paragraph: a canon ID, `material/<file>`, or `said today`. The public lines (the "what to say" decision) come from the judged piece in `pieces/<date>-positioning-lines/`, not from a single draft.

---

## Template

```markdown
---
doc: personal-monopoly
status: draft
source: /finding-your-personal-monopoly, <date>
---
# Your Personal Monopoly
*<date>*

## Your positioning thesis

> <the one sentence agreed in stage 5>

Everything below is the evidence behind that sentence and what to do about it. (Your brand tree thesis, if you have one, is a different sentence: the claim your body of work proves.)

---

## 1. The value you provide

<Two or three value types, named in plain words, not as categories. Under each, the evidence they gave, with its source. One short paragraph each.>

**What this means:** <One or two sentences: the thing they sell that they may not have realised they were selling.>

## 2. Your personal monopoly

<The crossing, in the "you're one of very few people who A and B, which means C" shape. Then what holds it up: the path, the view that cuts against the standard advice, the access, the anger.>

**Your position on <the thing that made them angry>:** <Their view, written as a claim they'd defend in public, with what they saw that made them believe it. This is the most quotable part of the document and often becomes their whole message. It's filed as a draft POV.>

## 3. Who this is for

<The specificity sentence.>

**You'll recognise them by:** <Three to five signs visible from outside: company size, what they just did, what they keep saying, what they've already tried.>

**Not for:** <The near-misses: people who look like the segment but aren't, and why. Often more useful than the segment itself.>

**Market reality:** <What they said about size, ability to pay, awareness and urgency. If a problem showed up here, name it plainly.>

## 4. What you refuse

<Three refusals, each as a line with its reason: "I don't take X, because Y.">

**What this buys you:** <What the refusals let them get better at.>

---

## Decisions

<Six to eight, each in this shape:>

### <n>. <The decision, as a sentence they could say out loud>

**Why:** <What they said that this comes from, named, with its source.>
**First move:** <One action, small enough to do this week.>
**You'll know it worked when:** <A signal someone could observe, not a feeling.>

---

## The next 30 days

<Three to five items in order, concrete. The first one takes under an hour.>

## What's still thin

<An honest list of what the session couldn't support: gaps in evidence, decisions they put off, claims that need proof before they can be made in public. If nothing is thin, say the document is well supported and name what would strengthen it.>

## What went into your files

<One line per canon file touched, with the IDs, all as drafts: "Three results to your proof bank (R4 to R6), one opinion to your POVs (P7), the segment to your brand strategy (G2)." Then: the interviews confirm these, so you won't be asked them again.>

## How to use this

<Three or four lines. The positioning thesis is a filter, not a slogan: run new work, new content and new enquiries through it. Revisit it in 90 days, or when the business changes.>
```

## Which decisions to include

Pick from these, based on what the session found. Never all of them: six to eight is where people act, and twelve is a document they file and forget.

- **Price.** What the position supports. Give the reasoning, not a number from nowhere: what they charge now (`vehicle.md` or said today), what the refusals justify, what the segment can carry, and what happens to demand when they stop being interchangeable. If they gave numbers, use their numbers. If they didn't, say what to test and how. Never promise a result.
- **Client filter.** Which enquiries to turn down from now on, and how. If they named a current client who fails the filter, address it directly and gently: leaving a client is a real decision with real consequences, and it needs a plan, not a slogan.
- **Offer shape.** Whether what they sell matches the value they provide. Someone selling hours who provides judgment is mispriced by construction. Name the mismatch if you see one. A change to an offer is a draft in `vehicle.md`, never a rewrite of it.
- **Proof to build.** The one thing that would make the claim undeniable to the segment: a teardown, a study of ten companies, a documented result, a number nobody else has. One, not a content plan.
- **What to say.** The headline, the one-liner, the opening line of the bio, as drafted words they can paste today. These come from the judged piece (`skills/craft-sales-copy`, copy kind `positioning line`), and go in the document as chosen.
- **Content filter.** One question they run every idea through: "does this prove I'm the only person who could say it?", in the specific version for them, using their monopoly. If a strategy exists, the filter sits beside its content plan, not instead of it.
- **What to stop.** The service line, the platform, the audience or the habit that no longer fits. Every clarity document should cost them something; one that costs nothing was polite instead of useful.
- **Where the risk is.** The weak point: concentration in one client, a claim resting on one case, a segment that's shrinking. Say it once, plainly, with the move that reduces it.

## Rules for the decisions

- **Trace every one.** If you can't point at the answer it came from, cut it. Generic advice is what they can get anywhere, and it's the fastest way to make the whole document read like filler.
- **Make the first move small.** "Change your LinkedIn headline to this exact sentence" beats "reposition your brand".
- **Make the signal observable.** "Three enquiries arrive already knowing you do the support-data work" beats "you feel more confident".
- **Order them when they depend on each other,** and say so.
- **Stay honest about money.** Lay out how to think about price; never promise revenue or pretend to know their market better than they do.
