# Frameworks behind the interview

Sorting tools for the interviewer. By default they stay behind the curtain: listen, sort silently, then name what you heard in the person's own words. Each entry carries named examples, because when someone is stuck ("When they have no idea" in `interview-moves.md`), you teach with two or three of them and ask which sounds closest. Examples teach; labels get performed.

The named examples are real public figures and companies, used to show a shape. Before any of them goes into a piece the person publishes, it's a `borrowed: verify` line with its source (`CLAUDE.md` rule 1).

## 1. The ten types of value

Most people provide two or three of these well. Trying to provide all ten is the same as providing none. These are the same ten value types the strategy's character sheet uses (`docs/strategy-method.md`), so what stage 1 finds goes straight to that line.

- **Intellectual.** Knowledge others lack, frameworks, views that cut against the standard advice, spotting a pattern early. *Naval Ravikant explains investing and philosophy in a way nobody else does, and the explanation is the product. Morgan Housel makes financial ideas simple without making them dumb. Nassim Taleb built a career on saying the risk models are wrong.*
- **Network.** Introductions, putting two people together who should already know each other, moving ideas between groups that don't talk. *Reid Hoffman's value was knowing which founder should meet which investor. Kevin Kelly connects artists, technologists and philosophers who'd never find each other.*
- **Opportunity.** Deals, partnerships, referrals, jobs, board seats: value that arrives as a door opening. *Gary Vaynerchuk's public presence brings in the client pipeline for his agency. Technical bloggers get hired by companies that read their work, without ever applying.*
- **Efficiency.** Time saved, filtering so others don't have to search, faster decisions, a better process. *Wirecutter reads two hundred reviews so you read one. Morning Brew condenses a day of business news into five minutes. David Allen sold a system, not information.*
- **Inspiration.** A picture of what could happen; helping people believe they can do the thing. *Carol Dweck's growth mindset work changed how millions of people talk to themselves about ability.*
- **Entertainment.** Humour, wit, story: making a hard topic enjoyable enough that people stay. *John Oliver gets people to watch twenty minutes on municipal bonds. Malcolm Gladwell wraps research in stories people retell at dinner.*
- **Safety.** Helping people avoid expensive mistakes; warning about risk before it lands. *Warren Buffett's letters are known less for stock picks than for warnings. Brian Krebs tells companies about the breach before it reaches them.*
- **Status and identity.** Being inside something selective; helping people see themselves the way they want to be seen. *A Harvard alumni network or the TED speaker community is worth more as membership than as content.*
- **Learning.** Teaching a skill step by step; handing over a mental model that changes how someone thinks. *Khan Academy walks you through the maths. Shane Parrish sells the mental models themselves.*
- **Practical.** Tools, templates, systems, things someone can pick up and use today. *Tiago Forte's PARA system; a Notion template that saves a team its first week of setup.*

**How to sort:** listen for the verb in their story. "I showed them" points at learning or intellectual. "I introduced them" points at network. "I stopped them from" points at safety. "They stopped doing it by hand" points at efficiency.

**What thank-you messages tend to point at:**

- "You made this so clear": efficiency
- "I never would have thought of that": intellectual
- "You put me in front of exactly the right person": network
- "I finally felt like I could do it": inspiration
- "You saved us from a very expensive mistake": safety

**Which two or three to keep:** what do they create without effort, what does their market need, what can only they provide, what builds on itself over time, and what spreads through other people?

## 2. The four types of uniqueness

- **Capability.** A rare mix of skills. AI plus healthcare plus regulation: plenty of people have one, few have two, almost nobody has three. Restaurant operations plus accounting. Each part is common; the stack isn't. *Tim Ferriss isn't the world's best at optimisation, interviewing or self-experiment; he's the best-known crossing of all three.*
- **Perspective.** They see the field differently: a contrarian view backed by experience, a bridge between two groups that misunderstand each other (engineers and executives), a systems read where others see isolated problems. *Cal Newport built a position on one contrarian claim: that knowledge workers should quit social media.*
- **Access.** They know things others can't easily learn: two hundred pitch meetings seen from both sides of the table, a private group of thirty operators, numbers from inside an industry that doesn't publish them, four years of support tickets nobody else read.
- **Identity.** Their path, their principles, the positions they take in public when others stay quiet: the person willing to criticise their industry's standard practice under their own name.

Most people have material in three of these four and have never noticed. Your job is to name it. When someone insists they have nothing unique, the block is almost always the word "unique": they hear "best in the world" and correctly conclude they aren't. Reset the bar out loud: unique means different, not best, and it usually comes from a combination, not a talent.

Perspective is where the person's POVs live (`canon/person/povs.md`), and identity is where their values show (`canon/person/values.md`). When those are confirmed, start from them.

## 3. The personal monopoly

Being the best at one thing is a fight against everyone. Being good at two or three things that rarely appear together is a fight against almost nobody.

The shape: **<thing A> plus <thing B> means you can <do something for someone that neither A nor B alone can do>.**

The medical student who also follows venture capital doesn't have to choose. Turning a hundred-page medical paper into something a non-scientist finds gripping serves every researcher who needs their work read and funded. That's a different market from medicine and a different market from venture, and almost nobody else is standing in it.

The accountant who used to cook doesn't compete with accountants. Most accountants understand numbers and not kitchens; most chefs understand kitchens and hate the numbers. One person understands both, and restaurant owners who are busy but not profitable will pay for that.

**Test it.** Could a stranger repeat it back after hearing it once? Could they say who it's for? If not, it isn't sharp enough yet.

**The anger question** is the fastest way in. Anger at a common practice usually means they can already see a better way. Ask what that practice cost the people it happened to. The answer is often the whole positioning, and it's usually a POV.

## 4. The five layers of segmentation

Layer these to sharpen a segment. Two or three are usually enough.

1. **Role and stage.** Job title, company size, funding stage, industry, place.
2. **Values and outlook.** What they believe, how they learn, how much risk they take.
3. **How they buy.** Who decides, who blocks, how they like to be talked to.
4. **What's happening now.** The problem in front of them, the growth stage, the constraint they're under.
5. **What they want to become.** Where they're trying to get to and what they want to be known for.

The specificity sentence, filled with detail from at least three layers:

> "I help ______ who are struggling with ______ because they are ______ and want to ______."

- Weak: "I help entrepreneurs grow."
- Strong: "I help solo consultants billing 100k to 300k who are struggling to grow past themselves, because they're excellent at the work and have never written down how they do it, and they want a business that doesn't need sixty-hour weeks."

**Check how decisions get made.** In a company of ten, the founder decides this week. In a company of a thousand, six people decide over six months. That changes what proof they need, how long it takes to get paid and what the person should write. Ask how big the buyer's company usually is, and say plainly what that means for their sales cycle.

The segment's own words for their problem belong in `brand-strategy.md` (`their_words`) and `audience.md`. They have to be the segment's words, not the person's: ask "What's something a client said to you before you worked together?"

## 5. The five types of trade-off

Refusals fall into five kinds. Take one or two from different kinds rather than three from the same one.

1. **Content.** Topics, formats and platforms they won't use.
2. **Audience.** Segments and places they won't serve.
3. **Work.** Project types, revenue models and clients they won't take.
4. **Positioning.** Expertise they won't claim and fights they won't pick.
5. **Resources.** Skills they won't build and time they won't spend.

Each refusal needs a reason that points back at the segment or the value:

- "I don't work with companies under fifty people." Fine, but incomplete.
- "I don't work with companies under fifty people, because under fifty the problem is finding customers, and over fifty it becomes keeping the culture while hiring fast, and the second one is what I'm good at." That's a strategy.

Content and positioning refusals belong on the stop list; work refusals the person already keeps are behaviors of type `refusal`.

## 6. What to measure afterwards

Positioning changes the slow signals before it changes the visible numbers. Tell the person what to watch so they don't judge the work by the wrong scoreboard.

**Weak signals:** followers, likes, comments, profile views.

**Real signals:**

- Enquiries that arrive already knowing what the person does
- Referrals from people they've never worked with
- Being named in rooms they weren't in
- Other people using their words and their framework
- Fewer proposals sent per client won
- Less pressure on price

When a strategy exists, these sit beside its signals in `canon/strategy/content-plan.md`; don't set a second scoreboard.
