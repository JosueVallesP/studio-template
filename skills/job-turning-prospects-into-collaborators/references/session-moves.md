# Session moves for the dual harvest

Read this before stage 1. The first stages interview and decide, and the later ones mostly draft, so the loop shifts halfway from ask-and-react to offer-and-adjust.

## The basic loop

Stages 0 to 2: one question, wait, react, decide whether to dig or move on. Stages 3 to 5: draft, offer, adjust. When drafting, produce the material first and let the person correct it, because correcting is cheaper than creating, and their corrections are what keep the voice theirs.

## Probes that work here

**The specific-line probe.** For every invitation: "What's the one thing about them you actually noticed?" The answer has to be particular enough that it couldn't describe their competitor. "Their content is great" fails. "They wrote that thread about firing their biggest client and I've thought about it twice since" passes, once the thread is found and linked.

**The worth-it probe.** "If this person never becomes a client, is this exchange still worth twenty minutes of their time?" If the honest answer is no, the series design is the problem, not the invitation.

**The swap test** (`skills/craft-sales-copy`, "The craft"). When it fails, the fix is always more of what only this person did.

**The say-it-aloud probe.** Have them read the invitation as if leaving it as a voice note. Anywhere they stumble or wince is a line that isn't theirs yet.

**The truth probe** (`skills/craft-sales-copy`, truth test 2), asked of every claim in every draft. Launch language until guests exist, real names only after real yeses.

## When they have no idea

**Stuck choosing the five.** Rung 1: a shortlist from the relationships list in their strategy, warmest first, with reasons. Rung 2: questions about what happened: who replied to something of yours in the last three months, who did you have one good exchange with and never follow up, which past client moved somewhere new? The warm five are usually hiding in their own history.

**Stuck on the series subject.** Rung 1: draft two or three candidate series from their thesis, their branches and their five, and let them pick. Rung 2: the overlap question: "What do your five all have stories about that you really want to hear?" Rung 3: the selfish version, which often unlocks it: "What do you wish you could ask these people if there were no stakes at all?" The best series is usually the person's real curiosity wearing a format.

**Stuck on the questions.** Draft a full set of six and ask what's wrong with it. Include one question that only the person would think to ask, from a POV or a branch of theirs, and point at it, because that question is what makes the series theirs.

**Stuck drafting invitations.** Show the contrast pair: a templated version ("Hi NAME, big fan of your work, I run a series featuring industry leaders and would love to include you") beside a real one built from their specific line. The bad one teaches the register faster than any rule.

**Frozen at sending.** This is the most common stall, and it happens after the session, so get ahead of it inside the session: the first send is the warmest name, the message is an offer rather than an ask, and the worst realistic outcome is silence, which the plan already treats as information. Make the first send small and scheduled: "Tuesday morning, the one to <warmest name>, before you open anything else."

## Handling what comes back

**"I've got nothing they'd want."** Turn it around: the series gives them a platform, readers, and their thinking organised into a piece they can share, and busy people say yes to that often, because their opinion is the one thing they still give away freely.

**"This feels manipulative."** Take it seriously instead of reassuring past it, because the concern is the person's ethics working correctly. The honest answer: it's manipulative if the collaboration is a pretext, and it isn't if the collaboration is real, which is exactly what the worth-it probe, the truth constraint and the no-selling line hold in place. Invite them to set the boundary tighter than the skill does if they want, and write their version into the plan.

**The plan keeps growing.** The series grows a landing page, a logo and a newsletter before the first invitation goes out. Bring it back: five invitations and one published piece are the whole of round one, and the rest can be round two's problem if round one earns it.

**A pitch creeping into a draft.** Cut it as soon as you see it and say the line once: the collaboration proves and connects, and the selling happens later, in a conversation the collaboration earns.

**All five aspirational.** A round one weighted toward big names produces silence and a dead series. Rebalance to warm first and park the big names for round two, when the published pieces make the invitation credible.

## Pacing

- Stage 0: orientation, inputs, the goal
- Stage 1: 3 to 5 exchanges
- Stage 2: 4 to 6 exchanges
- Stage 3: the long stage, 5 to 8 exchanges, one invitation at a time, then the judge
- Stage 4: 3 to 4 exchanges
- Stage 5: 2 to 3 exchanges

Between stages, one line on what's done and one on what's next: "That's stage 2 done. The series is <name>, six written questions, and next we draft the five invitations, warmest first."

## Tone

Curious, direct, warm, full sentences, no copywriting devices. This session writes into other people's inboxes, so the standing posture is respect for the guests: their time is the scarce thing, the invitations are offers, and anything that treats a guest as a means to an end gets caught and cut in the draft, not discovered by the guest.
