## 1. Traditional and reverse, side by side

The usual approach treats outreach and content as separate jobs: publish steadily and wait for people to come in, and separately, send cold pitches. The dual harvest joins them. When someone asks why it works, these are the differences worth saying plainly:

| Traditional | Reverse |
| --- | --- |
| Builds content and hopes an audience forms | Builds the list of people first and makes things for them |
| Teaches from the mountaintop | Works it out alongside them, from the trenches |
| Publishes for reach | Makes things that prove what the person knows |
| Treats a cold message as a pitch | Treats outreach as an invitation |
| Treats the audience as spectators | Treats the network as participants |
| Converts slowly, because trust builds slowly at a distance | Builds the relationship from the first exchange, because making something together is how strangers become colleagues |

## 2. Why invitations get answered when pitches don't

Five forces stack inside a collaborative invitation. Naming them helps the person write better ones.

**Social proof.** Being featured beside respected peers carries credibility at once, which is why past guests get named in invitations as soon as it's true, and not before.

**Reciprocity.** The person gives a platform before asking for anything, and people respond to being given to.

**Curiosity.** Guests want to see what the others said and how their own answers compare.

**Recognition.** Nearly everyone enjoys being asked about the thing they actually know. A good set of questions is a form of respect.

**Network effects.** Guests share the piece they appear in, so every published interview reaches the guest's network, which is full of people like the guest.

The baseline it's measured against: a plain pitch to a senior person rarely gets a reply. A well-made invitation does far better, because it asks for the one thing busy people who know their field still give away, which is their opinion.

## 3. The scenarios

Teach with whichever is nearest the person's world. These are shapes of a plan, not real cases; never present them to the person or in a piece as things that happened.

**Senior leaders.** The goal is relationships with CEOs of large companies. The pitch version says "I help companies like yours grow revenue" and gets ignored. The invitation version launches a series on how senior leaders make strategic decisions under pressure, refers to a specific public decision the CEO made, and asks for a short conversation for the series. The specific reference does the work, because it proves the sender pays attention.

**Technical leaders.** The goal is CTOs at fast-growing startups. The series documents how companies handle technical scaling, and the invitation refers to a real event: "I saw you went from 10 to 100 engineers in 18 months, and I'd like to feature how you handled it." The interview brings up the guest's current challenges, and what the person learned from the other guests becomes the bridge to a longer conversation.

**Hiring.** The goal is hiring a senior person without posting a job. The series interviews excellent practitioners about how they work, the quality of the guests attracts readers of the same quality, and some guests become candidates. The lesson in this one: the dual harvest works for any goal that runs through relationships, not only sales.

**The founder series, the fullest example.** This is a case from the workshop where this method was first taught: a written micro-interview series with venture-backed founders. Connect, invite, five or six questions turned into posts, and a closing question asking whether they'd be open to a video version; the workshop reported that most said yes. That turned a written exchange into a scheduled live conversation. What to point at is the mechanics, not the rate: the light format made the first yes easy, the published pieces made each next invitation warmer, and the step up continued something real instead of pivoting to an agenda. The person's own numbers, from their tracker, are the only rates worth quoting.

## 4. The invitation shape

Four parts, in order, roughly 60 to 110 words:

1. **The specific line.** One true, particular observation about this person's work: a decision, a launch, a position they took, with its source. Not flattery and not general praise, because "big fan of your work" is what every template says.
2. **The invitation.** The series named and described in one sentence, and why this person in particular belongs in it.
3. **The honest ask.** The format and the real time cost: "six written questions, maybe twenty minutes whenever suits you."
4. **What they get.** The finished piece, the company they appear in (named only when true), and their answers in front of readers who matter to them.

The register test: when teaching, put a templated version beside the real one, because the contrast teaches faster than any rule. The real one sounds like it could only have been written today, to this person, by someone who reads their work.

The truth constraint is truth test 2 in `skills/craft-sales-copy`. For a series it means launch language until guests exist, and guests named only after they've taken part.

## 5. The transition and its ethics

The transition is the point of the whole system and also where it can rot, so both halves get said together.

**The mechanics.** The hardest step in any business relationship is getting a live conversation with someone who doesn't know you, and the collaboration removes that step. Inside the format, a closing question offers the natural step up, like the video version of a written interview. After the collaboration, the follow-up is anchored to something the guest actually said: "you mentioned the pricing wall you hit at the enterprise tier, and I had two thoughts that might be useful, want me to share them over a call?" The anchor is the difference between continuing a conversation and pivoting to one.

**The ethics.** The collaboration is worth the guest's time on its own terms, with nothing further needed to justify it. No selling happens inside it. If the guest asks what the person does, they answer plainly and turn the attention back to the guest. This restraint wins for practical reasons as much as moral ones: the person who visibly didn't need to pitch is the one who gets hired, and a guest who feels used tells the other guests.

The operating line, worth saying to the person once: never sell directly, always move in angles. The angle is the collaboration, and the sale, when it comes, arrives as a request from someone who now knows the work.

## 6. The generosity rule

As a starting default, about a third of all activity around the list expects nothing back: introductions between guests who should know each other, useful things sent with no thread attached, insight given in comments and replies. This isn't decoration. A network where every touch carries an ask reads as a funnel, and people can tell. The introductions the person makes are themselves part of what makes them worth knowing, and that builds in their favour without a single ask.

## 7. Follow-up and evaluation

For someone who doesn't reply: one short, warm follow-up after two weeks, something useful after a month, then stop chasing and leave the door open. The list is long, and care spent on the next name beats pressure on the last one.

The horizon: ten invitations before judging the method. What gets judged is how specific the invitations were and how well the five fit, not week one's reply rate. Each published piece makes the next invitation warmer, so round two should reach names round one couldn't. That's the sign it's working.
