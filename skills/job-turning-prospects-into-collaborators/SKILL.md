# /turning-prospects-into-collaborators

Method by Josué Valles, the studio's author.

Take the person from a list of names to a running system: one collaborative content series, five invitations ready to send, and a clean path from each collaboration to a real conversation.

The change that makes it work: instead of making something about the people they want to reach, the person invites them into the making. A pitch asks for something. An invitation to be featured offers something, and the difference shows up in who replies.

The name for it is a dual harvest: every invitation produces content whether or not it produces a client, and every piece of content opens a door whether or not it performs. Outreach and publishing stop being separate jobs, and the people who would have been targets become participants.

This is a working session with heavy drafting. The person brings the names and what they know; the studio drafts the series and all five invitations with them, so they leave with messages ready to send, not intentions.

## What they walk out with

1. **A dual harvest plan:** one series designed (name, format, core questions), five people matched to it, and five invitations drafted in full, in their voice, judged in a fresh context.
2. **The transition plan:** how each collaboration becomes a conversation, the natural next-step question, and the line that keeps it honest.
3. **The rhythm:** send order, follow-up cadence, the tracker, and the generosity rule that keeps the whole thing from reading as a funnel.

## What it reads

- `canon/strategy/brand-strategy.md`: the `relationships` (ideal clients, peers and connectors who send clients), the segments, who it's not for, the anti-associations. The relationships list is where the five come from first.
- `canon/person/thesis.md` and the branches: what the series has to prove.
- `canon/strategy/channels/`: where the person publishes, which sets the series format.
- `canon/vehicle/vehicle.md`: what a conversation could lead to, so the transition stays honest about it.
- `material/`, `inbox/`, and past pieces in `pieces/`: anything the person has already written for or about one of these people, and how it went.
- When the canon is empty: what the person says today.

**How it relates.** A one-off piece written for a single person runs through `/post` or `/essay`. This skill scales that motion to five people at once by inviting them in. When a guest says yes to a call, `/diagnosis-call` preps it. Each published interview is a piece of its own, made with `/post` or `/essay` from the guest's answers.

## Shape of the session

Seven stages, about 40 to 55 minutes. Before stage 1, say in one short paragraph what's about to happen, why, what they'll have and how long it takes (`CLAUDE.md` rule 7).

## Rules for the whole session

**Anchor every question with an example.** A bare question makes people freeze, so attach a short example of the shape of a good answer, from a field far from theirs. The scenarios in `references/dual-harvest-frameworks.md` are the running examples. Questions about the person's own history ask about events (`skills/craft-interview`): "Who replied to something of yours in the last three months?" works; "Who's in your network?" doesn't.

**Everything sent must be true on the day it's sent** (`skills/craft-sales-copy`, truth test 2). For a new series that means launch language: the first invitations can't name earlier guests, because there are none. These guests often know each other, so one inflated claim tends to get found out.

**Never sell directly; always move in angles.** The collaboration is real and valuable on its own, and the conversation it opens is a follow-up between two people who now know each other. The moment an invitation or a transition reads as a disguised pitch, the method dies. Every draft is checked against one question: if this person never becomes a client, is the exchange still worth what it asks of them?

**Each invitation is written for one person.** Each of the five carries at least one thing that could only be about its person: a decision they made, a thing they built, a position they took. If the person can't supply it, the research comes first. The swap test (`skills/craft-sales-copy`, "The craft") catches a template wearing a name.

**Never invent anything about a guest.** Every claim about a guest comes from the person's own knowledge (`said today`), something the guest published (with the link), or a source checked on the web, and the studio says when it searched. A guest fact nobody can source stays out of the invitation.

**One question or one drafting move per message.** React before moving on. From stage 3 on, it's mostly drafting: offer material, let them redirect, keep the voice theirs.

**When they stall, use the ladder** in `references/session-moves.md`.

Everything the person reads, from the first message to the document, follows the house rules (`skills/craft-house-rules`): full sentences, plain words, no em dashes, no devices built for effect. Warmth is fine. It should read like a smart person talking across a table.

## Stage 0: Orientation and inputs

Open with the idea in a few plain sentences, adapted to the person: so far they've made content for people; now they make content with them. Instead of publishing and hoping the right people notice, they invite the right people into the content itself, a micro-interview, a short series, a few questions those people would enjoy answering. An invitation offers a platform instead of asking for something, which is why it gets answered. Every move pays twice: each invitation produces content whether or not it produces a client, and each piece opens a door whether or not it performs.

Then say what they'll have by the end (the three things above), that it takes about 45 minutes and the second half is mostly drafting together, and that each question comes with a short example so they know the depth wanted. Mention that people think out loud better than they type, so dictating is fine.

Then ask for the inputs: the relationships list in their strategy if there is one (read it; don't make them paste it), anything they've already made for one of these people and how it went, and what they want these five conversations to lead to. With no strategy yet, ask instead: what do you do, and who are five people, or kinds of people, you'd love to have a real conversation with?

From the relationships list, note the peers and the names with a clear reason to talk, because those are the natural first five. A person who already got a response to something they made for one of these people may belong in the five, with a warmer invitation.

**Exit test:** inputs read, and a shared picture of what they want these five conversations to lead to, in their words.

## Stage 1: The five people

Open with: "Stage 1 of 5. We're choosing five people, and the mix matters more than the individual names. This first round should be winnable, because its job is to get the series started and give you guests you can name in later invitations."

Build the five with them, one or two at a time, using these criteria and saying them out loud:

1. **Start with the warmest:** someone from the peers list or a dormant contact who already knows their name. A near-certain yes gets the series real. "Who's your near-certain yes?"
2. **Then two or three people with a clear reason to talk and visible activity:** people who post, launch or speak in public, because visible activity gives the invitation its hook.
3. **At most one aspirational name in round one,** and only with a real angle. The bigger names come in round two, when the series exists and has guests to show.

For each candidate, two checks: is there something specific and true to reference about them, and would the collaboration be worth their time even if nothing else ever happens? A name that fails either swaps out.

**Exit test:** five names, warmest to coldest, each with the specific true thing noted and its source.

## Stage 2: The series

Open with: "Stage 2 of 5. One series, not five separate ideas. A single named series makes every invitation stronger, because by the third invitation you're offering people a place in something that visibly exists."

Design it together, one decision at a time:

1. **The subject.** Where what the person wants to learn meets what the five would enjoy talking about. The shape, from the scenarios: "how senior leaders make decisions under pressure" for someone who wants to know CEOs; "how high-growth startups survive going from 10 to 100 engineers" for someone who wants to know CTOs. "What's the version where your five have stories they'd enjoy telling?"
2. **The format.** Default to a written micro-interview of five or six questions turned into a post, because it's the lightest ask and the easiest yes, with video or audio as the step up later. Check that the format matches where the person actually publishes (`canon/strategy/channels/`).
3. **The core questions.** Draft five or six together, written to draw out what the guests know and the stories behind it, not to promote anything: questions the guests will enjoy answering and readers will enjoy reading. Test each: would the answer be interesting even from a stranger? Mark which questions get changed for each guest.
4. **The name.** A plain, lasting series name the person can say out loud without wincing, because it goes in every invitation.

Check the whole design against their thesis and branches: does running this series show what they specifically know, and do its readers overlap the segment they're building for? A fun series aimed at the wrong room wastes the effort. With no thesis yet, ask the question in plain words: "What would you want these five, and the people reading, to know you for after this series?"

**Exit test:** a named series with a format and a drafted set of core questions.

## Stage 3: The five invitations

Open with: "Stage 3 of 5, the drafting stage. Five invitations, each written for exactly one person. I'll draft, you correct, and we keep your voice."

Draft each invitation using the four-part shape in `references/dual-harvest-frameworks.md`: one true, specific line about what caught their attention in this person's work; the invitation, with the series named and described in one sentence; the small time ask stated honestly; and what the guest gets. Roughly 60 to 110 words, written like a message between two people. Two constraints come first:

1. **The truth constraint** (`skills/craft-sales-copy`, truth test 2). The first invitations use launch language: "I'm starting a series and I'd like you to be one of the first." From the third on, name the real guests if the earlier ones said yes, and update the drafts when reality changes.
2. **The one-person constraint.** Each draft carries the specific thing from stage 1, worked in naturally, and passes the swap test.

Each invitation is its own piece through `skills/craft-sales-copy`, with the copy kind `invitation`: five piece folders, `pieces/<yyyy-mm-dd>-invite-<guest-slug>/`, each with its own `brief.md`, three distinct drafts in `draft-v1.md` (`## Post A`, `## Post B`, `## Post C`, different in what leads, not in wording), the scripts, and its own judge in a fresh context, which picks one draft and checks the four parts, the truth test and the swap test. Draft them one at a time, warmest first; what the person corrects in the first carries into the next. While teaching the register, a deliberately templated version beside the real one helps the person hear the difference; the templated one is never sent.

**Exit test:** five judged invitations the person would send without editing, each passing the truth and swap tests.

## Stage 4: The transition from content to conversation

Open with: "Stage 4 of 5. The series opens doors, and this stage is how you walk through one without turning into a salesperson in the doorway."

Build three things with them:

1. **The natural next step inside the collaboration.** For a written series, a closing question like "would you be open to a video version of this?" turns a written exchange into a scheduled live conversation. Choose the version that fits their format, and state the rule that keeps it clean: the written piece is complete and valuable on its own, so the step up reads as a continuation, never as the reveal of a hidden agenda.
2. **The follow-up transitions.** Draft two or three shapes in their voice, each anchored to something the guest will actually say: "you mentioned the hiring wall you hit at 40 people, and I had two thoughts that might be useful, want me to share them over a call?" The anchor is what separates a continuation from a pivot, so the real transitions get written after each interview, from the guest's real answers. The drafts here are shapes, and they say so.
3. **The line they won't cross,** written into the plan in their words: the interview is never bait for a pitch, no selling happens inside the collaboration, and if a guest asks what the person does, they answer plainly and turn back to the guest. This restraint is what produces the sales conversations, because people hire the person who didn't need to pitch.

Add the generosity rule and make it a number, as a starting default: roughly a third of everything they do around this list expects nothing back. Introductions made, useful things sent, insight given in comments and replies. A network where every touch has an ask reads as a funnel, and people can tell.

**Exit test:** the next-step question chosen, two or three transition shapes drafted, and the no-selling line written in their words.

## Stage 5: The rhythm and the send order

Open with: "Last stage before I write it up. A plan without a rhythm is a burst of enthusiasm, so let's put dates and a weekly shape on it."

Set, one at a time:

1. **The send order and the first date.** Warmest first, on a named day this week. The second invitation goes out when the first is answered or after one week, whichever comes first; the other three follow the same rule, so the whole first round is moving within three weeks.
2. **The production promise.** When a guest says yes, the questions go out within two days, and the finished piece publishes within a week of their answers, because slow production is how series die and guests remember being left hanging.
3. **The follow-up for no reply.** One short, warm follow-up after two weeks, then something useful after a month, then stop chasing and leave the door open. No reply is information about the invitation, not a verdict on the person.
4. **The tracker:** per person, the invitation date, the response, the interview status, the publish date, the transition tried, the outcome, the next touch.
5. **The horizon.** Ten invitations before judging the method. What gets judged is how specific the invitations were and how well the five fit, not week one's reply rate. Round two is planned from what round one teaches, including which bigger names the finished series now gives a reason to invite.

**Exit test:** dates on days, the production promise stated, and the tracker defined.

## Stage 6: Write it up

Read `references/document-template.md` and follow it. Save:

- `pieces/<yyyy-mm-dd>-dual-harvest-<series-slug>/plan.md`: the plan, the five judged invitations (each copied from its piece, with a link to its folder), the transitions, the rhythm and the decisions. This folder has no `brief.md`, so `/status` counts the invitations, not the plan.
- `pieces/<yyyy-mm-dd>-dual-harvest-<series-slug>/tracker.csv`: one row per guest, with the folder of their invitation.
- The five invitation pieces, each in its own `pieces/<yyyy-mm-dd>-invite-<guest-slug>/` folder from stage 3.

Every decision traces to the session. When a guest answers, their answers go in `inbox/` as a file, and the interview piece is made with `/post` or `/essay`; the guest's words are quoted with their permission and cited to that file as `borrowed: verify`, never as the person's. Whatever the series shows about the person goes along `skills/setup-brand-tree/SKILL.md`, "Where a picked-up belief goes": a belief they keep stating to guests becomes a `draft` POV entry in `canon/person/povs.md` with the interview files as its sources; a connection across confirmed entries goes to `proposals.md` as `seen`; nothing is confirmed without the person (`CLAUDE.md` rules 6 and 8).

## What goes wrong

**Invitations that become true only if things go well.** Launch language until there are guests, real names only after real yeses.

**The template wearing five names.** The swap test catches it. Each invitation carries something only its person did.

**The hidden agenda.** If the collaboration is a pretext, guests feel it, and the first guest who feels it tells the others. The no-selling line exists so the person decides where the boundary is before the first conversation tempts them across it.

**Five series instead of one.** One named series, building on itself, with round two earned by round one.

**Slow production.** A guest who answered three weeks ago and has seen nothing published is a relationship going backwards. The production promise is part of the plan.

**Chasing.** Two touches after the invitation, then the door stays open and the effort moves to the next name.

**Judging on round one.** Ten invitations before judging, and every published piece makes the next invitation easier, which is the reason to start with a winnable five.
