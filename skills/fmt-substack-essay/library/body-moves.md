# Substack body moves

Pilot, built from the same 57 essays as `hook-moves.md`. Same card format as the LinkedIn body moves, with the same four layers:

1. **The structure.** The spine of the whole essay. One per essay.
2. **The moves inside.** What a section or a paragraph does. An essay uses many.
3. **The close.** The last beat before the sign-off. One per essay.
4. **The furniture.** New for Substack: paywalls, plugs, sign-offs, subscribe asks, video notes. Not moves, because they don't do a job for the reader, but they change how the essay lands, so they get their own section.

The title and opening make a promise. The structure keeps it. The inside moves keep the reader going across 1,500 to 27,000 words, which is the problem a LinkedIn post never has. The close is what the reader leaves with.

Numbers are relative engagement, as in the hook file: likes plus restacks against the median of that writer's own set. Kieran Drew's posts and Justin Welsh's three oldest posts in the tag are left out of performance notes (see the hook file for why).

# Layer 1: Structures

## E1. The argument

LinkedIn: S4, at essay length.

**Job.** "Convince me."

**Mechanism.** A claim, the reasons, the other side, the claim again. At essay length it has room for two or three named traps or reasons, each with its own example, and for a practice section at the end that tells the reader what to do with the conclusion.

**What it needs.** A claim you hold. Two or three reasons that are different from each other, each with a real example. An honest version of the objection.

**Shape.** Claim. Reason one with its case. Reason two with its case. The objection, stated fairly, and the answer. What changes if the reader accepts it. Claim restated in stronger words.

**How it fails.**
- Reasons that are the claim reworded.
- One example stretched across the whole essay.
- Unbroken prose with no sections, so the reader can't see the steps (works under 800 words, not over).

**Pairs with.** The arguable claim (T8), the reader named (T9), the plain command (T5).

**Examples.** NA03 (2.2x: rule, Naval's line, busywork vs meaningful work, two named traps, simple not easy, commit), NA05 (four headed sections ending in a practice section and "A Final Note"), JW04 (complaint, flip it, named friends as proof, projection), KD10 (one example he lived, three signs, the claim).

## E2. The diagnosis

**Job.** "What's wrong, why, and what do I do?"

**Mechanism.** The essay treats a problem the reader feels the way a doctor would: what's happening, what it means, what to do about it. The first two parts earn the third. The reader trusts the prescription because they watched the cause get found.

**What it needs.** A problem your readers have now. A cause you can show with history, data or a case. A prescription that follows from the cause, not one you'd give anyway.

**Shape.** The problem, often in the reader's own terms. What's happening (how it got this way, sometimes in named phases). What it means (consequences, numbered). What to do (a rule, then how to apply it). Often a personal commitment at the end.

**How it fails.**
- The prescription doesn't follow from the cause.
- The paywall falls between the diagnosis and the prescription, so the free reader gets the bad news only (AS03, KG06).
- The diagnosis runs at civilizational scale and the fix is small, so the middle feels long (DK08).

**Pairs with.** The hidden reason (T4), the reader named (T9).

**Examples.** AS01 (3.05x: I. What's happening, the creator market in three phases; II. What this means, three consequences; III. What to do, "make it hard again" in three parts), KD05 (image, story, principle, fix, back to the image), DK08 (the problem, two layers of cause, the personal fix).

## E3. The named idea as a lens

**Job.** "Give me a new way to see this."

**Mechanism.** The essay names one law, term or condition, proves it shows up in many places, then turns it on the reader's own life. The name is what the reader takes away and repeats. The proof across fields makes it feel like a law of nature instead of an opinion.

**What it needs.** A named idea (yours, or a borrowed one you credit) that explains more than the usual explanation. Three or more places it shows up, ideally far apart. A way to apply it to the reader with their own numbers or situation.

**Shape.** The puzzle or the trend. The name and its definition, with where it came from. "Once you see it, you can't see it any other way": the idea in other fields. What it means for the reader, often in numbered areas. The catch or the paradox the idea creates, and how to live with it.

**How it fails.**
- The idea is named and defined, then the essay ends before applying it (the paid previews that cut here).
- The applications are generic and could follow any idea.
- The opening starts so far from the reader that they leave before the lens is turned on them (KG10 opens on the odds of the universe).

**Pairs with.** The coined term (T7), the hidden reason (T4).

**Examples.** KG01 (71.4x: Spotify Wrapped puzzle, Price's Law and its origin, the law in companies, stars, YouTube and rivers, then the reader's content, skills and time, then the paradox), JW02 (a borrowed idea, the coined term "second choosing", why people wait for a crisis, permission), NA07 (compression explained, the danger, the turn, incompressible defined), AS10 (hyper-abundance coined, music as the industry that already went through it, three remedies).

## E4. The ladder

**Job.** "Where am I, and what's the next level?"

**Mechanism.** The body is a set of levels or stages in order, from low to high. The reader finds themselves on it, which is the moment the essay becomes personal. Each rung needs signs (how you know you're here) and a way up.

**What it needs.** A real progression, where each level includes the one below it. Signs for each level that a reader can check against their own life. Proof at each rung, ideally from the writer's own path.

**Shape.** The problem. The model, often as a diagram. Level one, its signs and its trap. Level two. Level three. Why the top level wins. Where the reader probably is, and the order to work in.

**How it fails.**
- The levels are a list in disguise and don't build on each other.
- Too many coined terms for one essay (DK06 has quadrants, levels, phases, traits, channels, glitches and metatypes, and sits at 0.5x).
- The reader can't place themselves because the signs are vague.

**Pairs with.** Think like the person you want to be (T3), the reader's own question (T12).

**Examples.** DK02 (5.6x: one foundation idea, then beginner, intermediate and advanced frameworks, all applied to one running example), AS05 (the "Attention Altitude" model, three levels, why level three wins, then a warning not to say "we're too small to make a difference"), KD02 (Explorer, Experimenter, Expert, each with the writer as proof), NA06 (four kinds of luck in rising order of control), DK09 (one dimension of thinking per section).

## E5. The story essay

LinkedIn: S3, at essay length.

**Job.** "What happened, and what does it mean for me?"

**Mechanism.** One lived story carries the essay, with the idea arriving in the middle or at the end. At essay length the strongest versions add something a LinkedIn story can't fit: a second story that proves the first wasn't a one-off, a reread of an old text, a text to the person in the story asking what really happened.

**What it needs.** A real story with a turn. The idea it proves, stated plainly once. A bridge to the reader, usually near the end, in the second person.

**Shape.** The story to the turn. The idea, named. A second case (a friend, a reader, another time in the writer's life). How the writer uses the idea now. The reader's version.

**How it fails.**
- The idea comes so late that the title's promise is paid in the last few hundred words (KD09).
- The story is a diary entry with no turn.
- The bridge to the reader is one line tacked on.

**Pairs with.** The plain command (T5), the paradox (T6), the saying explained (T11). It is the most common structure in the set (9 of 57), mostly from writers whose readers follow them as a person.

**Examples.** JW01 (2.2x: the speaking fee story, the real reason people don't ask, how to ask, the math of 100 asks, the admission that the fear stays, the reader's own avoided conversation), JW05 (a story inside a story, a reread that finds four missed words, a text to the subject), KD04 (Harry's story in full, then "Your job as a writer"), KD01 (a personal update letter told as a story).

**Note.** A story essay can still state its idea early and use the story as the proof. If the person's voice guide says to lead with the idea, build it that way.

## E6. Changing my mind

**Job.** "Why did you change your mind, and should I?"

**Mechanism.** The writer states something he believed and acted on, shows why it was right at the time, then shows the evidence that piled up against it. Defending the old view first is what makes the change believable. Readers trust a conclusion more when they watched the writer resist it.

**What it needs.** A belief you really held, ideally one you're known for. Evidence that changed it, in the order it arrived. A new position that keeps what was true in the old one.

**Shape.** The old belief. Why it worked (one or two stages of life or market). The first crack. More evidence. "OK, I give up." The new position, with a hedge on how far it goes. What the reader can do with it.

**How it fails.**
- The old belief was a straw man the writer never held.
- No evidence, only a feeling.
- The new position throws away everything the old one got right.

**Pairs with.** The shift (T10), the paradox (T6).

**Examples.** AS07 (career-long belief that quality is dead, the Octopus Energy case, more exceptions, the final straw, a hedged new view, three questions), JW03 (the rule "More", why it was right at 28 and again in 2019, what's scarce now, how he decides today).

## E7. The worked case

LinkedIn: S5, at essay length.

**Job.** "Show me how it was actually done."

**Mechanism.** A real piece of work, the writer's own or a client's, taken apart stage by stage, with the general rule pulled out at each stage. The reader gets a method and the proof that it worked in the same pass. On Substack it can be shown on the essay the reader is reading, or on a named essay the writer published.

**What it needs.** A real result. Access to how it was done, in detail. The ability to say what the general rule is at each stage.

**Shape.** The result and why you're sharing it. The starting point. Stage by stage, each with the rule it shows. The result in numbers. What the reader should take from it.

**How it fails.**
- Stages are described but no rule is pulled out, so the reader learns about one case and nothing else.
- The interesting part is paywalled.
- The case is so specific to one business the reader can't see themselves in it (AS09 brings clients more than likes).

**Pairs with.** Proof, then payoff (T1), the how-to with the catch removed (T2). The best performing structure in this set: KG02 (6.6x), KG03 (6.5x).

**Examples.** KG03 (6.5x: the idea, the payoff, the map, the movie, each step shown on one of her own essays), KG02 (6.6x: a manual with numbered chapters, each rule given as "Mistake" then "Rule"), AS09 (a client case in six numbered parts with lessons pulled out), KD07 (a named strategy shown as a diagram, then his real workflow).

## E8. The protocol

**Job.** "What do I actually do, starting when?"

**Mechanism.** The first half explains why the usual approach fails and why this one works. The second half is a protocol the reader can follow: dated, timed or numbered, often with questions to fill in or prompts to copy. The theory earns the protocol, and the protocol makes the theory worth having read.

**What it needs.** A method you've used, with steps that are specific enough to follow without asking. A reason the usual approach fails, so the reader doesn't think "I've tried this."

**Shape.** The promise and how long it will take. Several numbered ideas that build the case. The protocol, step by step. A recap line per step at the end.

**How it fails.**
- The protocol is common advice with new names (DK10 is close: walk, lift, diet, reflect).
- The real work is handed to a template or a product outside the essay (DK05).
- The theory half is so long the reader never reaches the protocol.

**Pairs with.** The how-to with the catch removed (T2), the reader named (T9).

**Examples.** DK01 (3.1x: seven numbered ideas, the sixth of which is a one-day protocol with morning, daytime and evening questions, and the seventh turns the plan into a video game), DK03 (3.9x: seven numbered ideas, the first two on why multiple interests are an advantage, the rest a path to a one-person business), AS08 (a named weekly system in five steps with the exact tools), DK10 (why a detox works, then five steps).

## E9. The explained list

LinkedIn: S2, at essay length.

**Job.** "Give me the items, and tell me why each one matters."

**Mechanism.** Numbered items, each with a section of reasoning and an example. At essay length the strongest versions end by turning the list into something the reader keeps: daily questions, a poster, a checklist.

**What it needs.** Items that each stand alone and earn a section. An order that means something. A way to turn them into use.

**Shape.** Why these matter. Item one, with its reasoning and example. Repeat. A section that turns the list into a habit or a tool. Optionally, the last item grows into the close.

**How it fails.**
- Uneven items: two with substance, the rest padding.
- The essay strings unrelated material together and calls it principles (DK04 runs from chess to Musashi to ego stages to seven principles).
- The list sits behind the paywall (AS06).

**Pairs with.** Think like the person you want to be (T3), the numbered promise (T16).

**Examples.** AS04 (four rules, then "Turning these concepts into habits" with four daily questions, then a printable poster), KD03 (five lessons, the fifth flagged early as the most important and grown into the close).

## E10. The annotated source

**Job.** "Walk me through a famous text and tell me what it really means."

**Mechanism.** The essay is a reading of one known text (a tweet thread, a quote, a guide), broken into parts, each opened with the source's words and followed by the writer's notes. The source supplies trust and a structure. The notes are where the writer shows thinking of their own.

**What it needs.** A source your readers know and respect, quoted exactly and credited. Notes that add something: a connection, a correction, an example, a link between parts.

**Shape.** Why this source matters. Part one: the source's words, then the notes. Each part ends on the problem the next part solves. The whole thing pulled together. The writer's own answer to the question the source raises.

**How it fails.**
- The notes restate the source.
- The essay is a summary of something readers can read for free.
- The commentary starts with admin and the paywall cuts before any note (NA02).

**Pairs with.** The saying explained (T11), the how-to with the catch removed (T2).

**Examples.** KG09 (Naval's wealth thread in six steps, each ending on a problem, with her own correction from a reader), NA08 (one Naval tweet, two categories with mirrored traits), NA10 (one quote, a reframe, a second quote by the same person read in two halves).

## E11. The resolved paradox

**Job.** "Show me how both are true."

**Mechanism.** The essay sets up two things as opposites, gives each its due, then shows how they fit together. The headers often restate the resolution as a pair ("Discipline is the Frame. Spontaneity is the Art."). Short, symmetrical and easy to quote.

**What it needs.** A real tension the reader feels. A resolution that's more than "balance". A practical section on how to live it.

**Shape.** The two sides as opposites. Why each matters. Why one alone fails. How they fit. How to live it, in a few bullets. A mirrored closing line.

**How it fails.**
- It becomes a template and every essay sounds the same (Naval's Archive after 2025).
- The resolution is a slogan.
- The "how to live it" bullets are generic.

**Pairs with.** The paradox (T6), the saying explained (T11).

**Examples.** NA01 (19.6x: discipline and spontaneity, the jazz analogy, why discipline alone isn't enough, four habit-and-freedom pairs, closing aphorism), KG08 (why the saying is true, where it breaks at the top, four ways to fix it, back to the skater), NA09 (smart and happy, how intelligence makes better excuses, four bullets).

## E12. The objections answered

**Job.** "You've named my fears. Answer them."

**Mechanism.** The writer lists the objections or fears that stop people (often his own, from a real conversation), then answers each in its own numbered section. The reader sees their own reasons listed and answered.

**What it needs.** The real objections, in the words people use. An answer for each that isn't a pep talk.

**Shape.** The situation. The fears, listed. Fear one and its answer. Fear two. Fear three. What changes once they're gone.

**How it fails.** Straw fears. Answers that are encouragement rather than reasons.

**Examples.** KD08 (his doubts in Barcelona, his fears from a talk with his best friend, three numbered answers, an invitation).

Thin card.

## E13. The book

**Job.** "Give me the whole thing."

**Mechanism.** A full book published as one post, held together by the moves that keep a reader across chapters: a table of contents that states each chapter's claim, a reading contract, a few ideas that come back in every chapter, forward pointers, and a bridge line at the end of each chapter.

**What it needs.** A complete body of thought. The inside moves N2, N3 and N4 below, or the reader gets lost.

**Shape.** Furniture and asks up front. Table of contents with a claim per chapter. Introduction as a reading contract. Diagnosis. Redefinitions. The ladder. The method. The writer's own story, late, as proof. The path. A short rally to close.

**How it fails.** A long essay padded into a book. No map, so the reader can't tell where they are.

**Examples.** DK07 (27,600 words in 12 chapters).

Thin card. Worth having because a person with a body of work may want to publish it this way.

# Layer 2: Moves inside the body

The nine LinkedIn inside moves (M1 to M9) all show up in essays and keep their cards: the analogy (M6, in 21 essays), the subject's own words (M8, 17), the other side taken seriously (M7, 17), the side-by-side (M5, 15), the data line (M4, 14) and the turn (M2, 11) are the most used. The moves below are the ones long-form adds, mostly because an essay has to keep a reader across many sections.

## N1. The coined term and its definition

**Job.** "What do I call this?"

**Mechanism.** The writer names a pattern, sets the name on its own line, and defines it in one sentence. The name becomes the handle the reader remembers and repeats. Used in 16 of 57 essays, more than any other new move.

**What it needs.** A real pattern without a good name. A one-sentence definition in plain words.

**How it fails.** Too many coined terms in one essay. A name for something that already has a plain name. A term that's never defined.

**Examples.** AS10: hyper-abundance, defined as "A multiplication of options, but without a corresponding multiplication of capacity." JW02: "your second choosing." DK09: "5d thinking", defined in its own paragraph. KG01 borrows a named law (Price's Law) and tells its origin.

## N2. The map up front

**Job.** "What's coming, so I can decide to stay?"

**Mechanism.** Right after the hook, the writer lists the parts of the essay. On a long essay it tells the reader the length is organized, and lets them see the whole argument before starting.

**What it needs.** An essay with real parts. Three to twelve, each stated as what it gives the reader.

**How it fails.** A map on a short essay. Parts named as topics instead of claims.

**Examples.** AS01: right after the hook, a three-item list (what's happening, what it means, what to do about it). DK07: a table of contents with a one-line claim for each of 12 chapters. DK01 and DK03: "7 ideas", promised up front.

## N3. The reading contract

**Job.** "How should I read this?"

**Mechanism.** The writer says what the piece is and isn't, or how to read it (bookmark it, set aside time, read it twice). On long pieces it turns the length from a cost into a sign of seriousness.

**What it needs.** A piece that's genuinely different from what readers expect, or long enough to need instructions.

**How it fails.** It delays the idea. It sounds defensive.

**Examples.** KG02: "This is not a course." DK07: "This is not a practical business book." DK01 and DK04: reading instructions after the promise.

## N4. The bridge

**Job.** "Why should I read the next section?"

**Mechanism.** The last line of a section names the problem or question the next section answers. Each section ends with a reason to keep going. It's the main thing that keeps readers moving through long essays.

**What it needs.** Sections that really do follow from each other.

**How it fails.** The bridge is announced ("Now let's move on to...") instead of made. The next section doesn't answer what the bridge raised.

**Examples.** KG09: each of Naval's steps ends on the problem the next one solves (paid the same salary, so you need ownership; ownership needs judgment). DK07: "You have the path; now you must learn to navigate it." KD02 and DK01: one-line bridges between sections.

## N5. The epigraph

**Job.** "Whose idea is this section standing on?"

**Mechanism.** A thinker's quote under a section header frames the section before the writer says anything. The strongest version argues with the quote instead of agreeing with it (DK03).

**What it needs.** Quotes that are exact and credited, and that the section actually uses.

**How it fails.** Decoration. A quote per section by habit, unrelated to what follows.

**Examples.** DK01, DK03, DK04 (an epigraph under most headers; DK03 then argues with it). KG09 opens each section with a quote from the source it's annotating.

## N6. The precedent

**Job.** "Has this happened before?"

**Mechanism.** The writer shows the same change already happened in another era or another industry, and uses what happened there to predict what will happen here. It makes a forecast feel grounded rather than guessed.

**What it needs.** A real historical case or an industry that went through the same thing, with facts you can check.

**How it fails.** The parallel is loose. The case is told at length and the link back is one line.

**Examples.** AS10: the music industry after streaming, as a preview of every industry in hyper-abundance. AS05: the move from the supply era to the choice era. DK03: Adam Smith's pin factory, then the printing press and the Renaissance.

## N7. The cross-domain sweep

**Job.** "Is this a law, or just a pattern in one place?"

**Mechanism.** The same pattern is shown in fields that have nothing to do with each other (companies, stars, YouTube, rivers). The distance between the fields is the proof.

**What it needs.** A pattern that really does hold across fields, with a fact for each.

**How it fails.** The examples are from neighboring fields, so they prove nothing new.

**Examples.** KG01 (71.4x): "Once you see it, you can't unsee it", Price's Law across companies, stars, YouTube and rivers. One example in the set, in its best-performing essay.

## N8. Locate the reader

**Job.** "Where am I in this?"

**Mechanism.** After a model or a ladder, the writer tells the reader where they probably sit, or asks them to look at their own feed, friends or life as the evidence. It turns a description into a diagnosis of the reader.

**What it needs.** A model with levels, or evidence the reader can see from where they are.

**How it fails.** It flatters ("you're probably at the top"). It insults without reason.

**Examples.** DK01 places the reader between stages 4 and 8 of his model. NA09 asks the reader to look at the smart, unhappy people they know. NA07 points at the reader's own feed. JW04 fast-forwards the reader five or ten years.

## N9. The writer's own system, shown

**Job.** "What do you actually do?"

**Mechanism.** The writer shows the real thing: their workflow, tools, template, numbers or a decision they made. It proves the advice is used, not theorized, and it's the part readers copy.

**What it needs.** A system you really use, specific enough to copy.

**How it fails.** A product pitch dressed as a system. Tools named with no reason.

**Examples.** AS08: five steps with the exact tools (Granola, Claude, Instagram Edits). KD07: his real AI workflow, with the newsletter left out of it. KG01: her own Substack numbers run through the law. KD10: three newsletter swaps he turned down.

## N10. Questions to apply

**Job.** "How do I use this on my own life?"

**Mechanism.** The writer turns each idea into a question the reader can ask themselves, or gives a worksheet to fill in. Reading becomes doing.

**What it needs.** Questions with specific answers, one per idea.

**How it fails.** "Reflect on your goals." Too many questions to act on.

**Examples.** AS04: four rules become four daily questions. AS05: a list of questions for each level. DK01: a full worksheet of morning and evening questions. KD06: reflection prompts before the sign-off.

## N11. The recap

**Job.** "Where are we?"

**Mechanism.** Partway through a long essay, or at the end of a protocol, a short summary of what's been established before the next turn.

**What it needs.** An essay long enough that the reader could lose the thread.

**How it fails.** A recap on a short essay. A recap that repeats instead of compressing.

**Examples.** DK03 ("There are a few things we know so far:", partway through), DK10 (one recap line per step at the close), KG03.

## N12. The named enemy

**Job.** "Who am I against?"

**Mechanism.** A belief or a group gets a label, and the writer argues against it. It puts the reader on the writer's side. On a long piece it gives every chapter something to push against.

**What it needs.** A real position held by real people, described fairly enough that the argument against it means something.

**How it fails.** Punching at strangers. A straw enemy.

**Examples.** DK07 ("money-haters", "freeloaders"). One example in the set.

## N13. Name the reader's reaction

**Job.** "Are you reading my mind?"

**Mechanism.** The writer names what the reader is probably thinking at that point (the excuse, the objection, the urge to stop) before the reader can act on it. Done once at the right moment it keeps a reader who was about to leave.

**What it needs.** An accurate guess at the reaction, at the point it happens.

**How it fails.** Done several times, it reads as a trick. A guess that's wrong.

**Examples.** AS05 rules out the "we're too small" excuse. DK10: "you've read this far" and a prediction about quitting. KG08 warns that the next point will lose part of the audience. AS02 checks in: "Does this make sense?"

## N14. The callback

**Job.** "Oh, that's where this was going."

**Mechanism.** The close returns to the image, person or question from the opening. It ties a long essay together and makes the ending feel planned.

**What it needs.** An opening image or person strong enough to bring back.

**How it fails.** A callback to something too small to remember.

**Examples.** KD05 returns to the tea. KG08 returns to the skater on the ice. JW05 shows the reader in the opening doing what the writer did. KG09 answers the question she planted at the start.

## N15. The borrowed thinker

**Job.** "Who else thinks this?"

**Mechanism.** A named thinker's idea (a book, a post, a framework) becomes one step in the argument, credited. It lends weight without the writer having to invent the idea, and shows reading.

**What it needs.** An idea you can credit exactly. A step in your argument that the idea genuinely carries.

**How it fails.** Name-dropping. An idea used without credit (NA06 uses the four kinds of luck without crediting its original sources).

**Examples.** JW02 (Moses Kagan's idea of the tunnel from 15 to 35), JW06 (Brad Stulberg on progress fading after the honeymoon), NA05 (Taleb's skin in the game applied to ethics), DK08 (Daniel Schmachtenberger's generator functions).

## N16. The scene as proof

**Job.** "Show me, don't tell me."

**Mechanism.** A long scene told move by move (a chess game, a day at a dental practice) that ends with the lesson labeled. It makes an abstract point physical.

**What it needs.** A real scene with a clear outcome that shows the point.

**How it fails.** The scene runs long and the lesson is small.

**Examples.** DK04 (a chess match ending in a fork, then "The lesson:"), KD09 (the quitting scene).

## N17. Your own line, set apart

**Job.** "This is the line to remember."

**Mechanism.** The writer sets their own key sentence apart as a block quote or a line of its own, so it reads like a saying and is easy to screenshot.

**What it needs.** A line that genuinely compresses the idea.

**How it fails.** Several per essay, so none stands out. A line that only works in context.

**Examples.** AS01's rule on its own line ("If it's not difficult, it won't work."). NA05 sets its own lines as pull quotes. DK02 and DK08 put the thesis in a pull quote near the top.

## N18. The diagram

**Job.** "Let me see it."

**Mechanism.** A named model shown as an image, then walked through section by section. The reader can return to it and share it.

**What it needs.** A model with a real shape (levels, quadrants, a barbell). Labels in plain words.

**How it fails.** The essay depends on a diagram the reader can't read in the email.

**Examples.** AS05 (the "Attention Altitude" model), KD07 (the barbell AI strategy), AS10 (a chart image inside the music section).

## N19. The admission inside

LinkedIn: 15, used mid-essay.

**Job.** "You struggle with this too?"

**Mechanism.** Partway through, the writer admits a failure, a doubt or how hard the piece was to write. It resets trust in the middle of a long argument.

**What it needs.** Something true that relates to the point being made.

**How it fails.** Humblebrag. An admission that changes the subject.

**Examples.** JW01: the fear of asking never left him. DK08: how hard this letter was to write. DK10: his own slump in the gym.

# Layer 3: Closes

The LinkedIn closes all appear: **C1 the restated principle** (16 essays, still the most common), **C2 the question back** (4), **C3 the small instruction** (4) and **C5 the aside** (1). Justin Welsh adds a reply ask to C2 ("Jennifer and I read every response"), which suits a newsletter because the reply goes to the writer's inbox, not a public comment box.

Eleven essays have no visible close because the paid preview cuts first (P0 in the sorted file).

New closes in long-form:

## C7. The callback close

**Job.** "Bring it home."

**Mechanism.** The last beat returns to the opening image or person (see N14) and states the lesson through it.

**Examples.** KD05 ("But have faith that doing the best you can is all you need to do." after returning to the tea), KG08 (back to the skater, then "Out-fun them all.").

## C8. The identity line

**Job.** "Who does this make me?"

**Mechanism.** The close tells the reader who they are now, or who they could be, instead of restating the idea. "You're not a content creator." It turns a lesson into a self-image, which readers keep longer than a rule.

**Needs.** An essay that earned the new identity.

**Fails.** Flattery. An identity the essay never built toward.

**Examples.** AS08 (you're not a content creator, and readers of this newsletter will be the first content thinkers), AS01 (your reluctance is the proof; "I'm ready to make things difficult."), DK07 (a run of short commands that restate who the book asks the reader to become).

## C9. The reader's own version

**Job.** "Where is this in my life?"

**Mechanism.** The close describes the reader's version of the situation in the second person and leaves them to act. Softer than an instruction, more personal than a question.

**Examples.** JW01 ("But you don't really know. Maybe today is a great day to find out."), JW04 ("Imagine how different your life could look if that changed today.").

## C10. The invitation

**Job.** "Can I do this with you?"

**Mechanism.** The writer invites the reader into something: a challenge he's starting, a reply, a project. On a newsletter it's the natural bridge from essay to relationship.

**Needs.** A real thing to join. An essay that earned the ask.

**Fails.** The invitation is a sales pitch.

**Examples.** KD08 (a weekly depth challenge he's taking on), AS09 (reply to this email about your business), KD02 ("Reach out if you have any questions").

## C11. The next-issue tease

**Job.** "What's next?"

**Mechanism.** The close promises the next essay, often the paid one. It turns the essay into an episode.

**Fails.** The essay held back its own payoff to sell the next one (AS10 sits at 0.8x).

**Examples.** AS10 ("Next week, for paid subscribers only, I'm going to be breaking down the component parts of a charismatic strategy in detail...").

## C12. The P.S. close

**Job.** "Here's what it really meant."

**Mechanism.** The essay signs off, then the real lesson sits in a P.S. below the name. The letter ends as a letter and the lesson feels like an afterthought from a friend.

**Examples.** KD01 (signs off with excitement, then a P.S. that reframes the lost book as already a success).

## C13. The handoff

**Job.** "Where do I go from here?"

**Mechanism.** After the sign-off, a list of resources: the writer's products, prompts, other essays. It's furniture as much as a close. Dan Koe uses it on most posts.

**Fails.** It replaces a real close, so the essay's last beat is a product list.

**Examples.** DK02 ("Here are further resources for writing and articulating yourself:"), DK03, DK04, DK08, DK10.

## C14. The choice

**Job.** "Which side are you on?"

**Mechanism.** The close splits the world in two and asks the reader where they'll stand.

**Examples.** AS05 ("The only choice left for you, is to decide whether you want to suffer the weather - or be the one to make it."), KD10 ("The internet will be divided by real people and robots. Think about where you'd like to land.").

## C15. The planted question, answered

**Job.** "So what's your answer?"

**Mechanism.** A question asked at the start (often in the reader's voice) is answered at the end in the writer's own words. It closes the loop and shows the writer applying the essay to themselves.

**Examples.** KG09 (opens on what you really want from money; ends "To me, the F in fuck you money stands for freedom").

# Furniture

Not moves, because they don't do a job for the reader inside the essay. But they decide whether the essay's job gets done.

**F1 and F2. The paywall.** The biggest single effect in this set. Paid previews that stop before the payoff (F1, 10 essays) have a median of 0.5x. Free essays (33, excluding the noisy sets) have a median of 1.1x. The one paid post that does well, KG02 (6.6x), gives about 1,200 words of usable rules before the paywall (F2). The rule that comes out of this: if the post is paid, put the paywall after the free reader has received something whole (a complete argument, a usable set of rules), and before the extension (the full playbook, the templates, the worked examples). Never at the turn. Most F1 posts cut right at the turn or just before the list the title promised (AS02, AS06, KG06, KG07, KG10).

**F3, F4 and F5. Plugs.** Plugs above the essay (event promos, trials), inside it (a boxed ad between sections, a product named inside the method) and after the sign-off (resource lists, paid tier asks). Dan Koe's two best posts in this set both carry plugs inside and after (DK02, DK03), so plugs don't sink an essay that delivers. The cost shows when the essay's value moves into the product: DK05 hands its real work to a template, DK10 doubles as a launch post, and both sit near 1x.

**F6. The reply or subscribe ask.** A mid-essay subscribe ask after a relevant section (KD02), or a reply ask in the close (Justin Welsh). Reply asks build a relationship; subscribe asks inside the essay interrupt it.

**F7. The video version note.** "This letter is also a video." Dan Koe, three posts.

**F8. The standing sign-off.** Kieran Drew signs every post with a line that carries the essay's instruction ("You're in the business of transformation. Write like it,"). Justin Welsh uses the same tagline and "See you next Saturday" every week. A standing sign-off is fine; a standing closing sentence the reader can predict is a template showing (the same closing line on every essay, "Hope that's helpful" for example, is one of the clearest signs a series was written from a template).

**F9. The hero image.** Alex M H Smith opens almost every post with a classical painting and a caption, which sets the register before a word is read.

## Notes from the sort (all 57 essays)

**Counts.** Structures: the story essay (9), the argument (7), the named idea as a lens (6), the ladder (6), the diagnosis (5), the protocol (5), the worked case (4), the explained list (4), the annotated source (4), the resolved paradox (3), changing my mind (2), the objections answered (1), the book (1). Closes: the restated principle (16), paywall cut (11), the handoff (6), the question back (4), the small instruction (4), the invitation (3), the identity line (3), and one or two each for the rest.

**The title doesn't pick the structure on Substack.** On LinkedIn the hook move predicts the structure with high confidence. Here it doesn't: the how-to (T2) appears over a protocol, a ladder, a story, a worked case and an annotated source. Four pairings do hold:
- The hidden reason (T4) goes to the diagnosis (E2) in 3 of 6.
- The coined term (T7) goes to the named idea as a lens (E3) in 3 of 5.
- Proof, then payoff (T1) goes to the worked case (E7) in 2 of 2.
- The numbered promise (T16) goes to the explained list (E9) in 2 of 2.

For everything else, choose the structure from the material, not from the title: a real progression asks for a ladder, a method you've used asks for a protocol or a worked case, a lived story asks for a story essay, a named idea asks for the lens. A skill should ask what the person has, then offer the structures that material can carry, the same gate the hook stage already uses.

**What performs, within this small set.** The worked case (E7) has the highest median (KG02, KG03), then the resolved paradox (E11, carried by NA01). Diagnosis, argument and lens essays sit near 1x. These are small numbers and each writer's top ten already, so read them as hints, not rules.

**Closes.** As on LinkedIn, the restated principle is the default close and performs at the median (1.0x). The closes that hand the reader something to do or be (the reader's own version, the identity line, the small instruction, the callback) sit above it, but on one to three essays each. The paywall cut is the real outlier (0.5x).

**What makes long essays hold together.** The essays that stay readable at 3,000 words and beyond share a small set of inside moves: a map up front (N2), a bridge at the end of each section (N4), a coined term the reader can carry (N1), the writer's own system shown (N9), and one idea that comes back in every section. DK07 holds a reader across 27,600 words with exactly these. The essays that sprawl (DK04, DK06) have many coined terms and no single idea coming back.

**Things that aren't moves.** "Hope this helps", "Thanks for reading", standing taglines, "originally published here", "Let's dive in", video notes, em dash disclaimers. Furniture.
