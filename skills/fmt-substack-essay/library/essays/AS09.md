## Essay

Mother Feeding Child, Mary Cassatt

I don’t often share details of my projects.

After all, most clients don’t necessarily want to share this kind of information.

But the other day I had lunch with one of them - Dean Brown, CEO of Little Dish - and we agreed that their strategy story is one that’s worth telling. So I’m very grateful to him for letting me write this up.

It really is beautifully elegant, and just as strategy should be:

- Take a strong but low-growth business

- Make a couple of tweaks to it

- Watching it 3x over the next 18 months

Simple as that.

Here, I’m going to explain how we did it. But I’m not going to do it in a boring “case study” case study fashion. Instead I’m going to extract all the lessons I can for you each step of the way, so you can understand the universal mechanics at play, that ring true for every business at any size.

Let’s get into it.

## I. The status quo

Little Dish are the UK category leader in kids’ chilled prepared meals (or what I guess in America you call “TV dinners”). They were founded by Hillary Graves back in 2006, with her husband Dean becoming CEO a few years later.

As Dean explains:

> “The business had grown steadily since being founded, but had never had a real breakout moment”.

Although they were the category leader, that wasn’t saying much, because it was a small category with low penetration. A big part of the issue was the fact that these are chilled products, whilst the toddler and baby aisle is ambient - meaning they had to sit with adult ready-meals, which isn’t somewhere people are shopping with their “kid hat” on.

Bigger than this however was probably the fact that although the product was very simple, it wasn’t entirely clear what the point of kids’ microwave meals really was.

Why did they even need to exist?

Clearly there were many benefits to the product - but a common trap you find with many businesses is confusing benefits for strategic advantage. These are not the same things. For Little Dish their benefits included convenience and nutrition, which sounds great, until you realise that there are many convenient ways to feed a child, and there are also many nutritious ways to do it too.

This meant - as I’ve seen many times - that the brand presented a new way for customers to get old value . Which always appears very exciting, but doesn’t actually work all the well since new value is the key thing we’re looking for.

Perhaps the most unique thing about the brand was the offer implied by their tagline:

“Proper food for kids”

…the idea being that the product allowed you to feed 1 and 2 year olds their first “sophisticated adult dishes”, such as fish pie and risotto, but formulated for their specific nutritional needs.

This wasn’t bad at all, and it grew them into a healthy business. But let’s face it, it was also quite niche . It assumed a very sophisticated parent who, among all their other worries, would be fretting that little Timmy hadn’t tried any fish pie yet. Not exactly a common mindset.

So what we had here then, was a classic “normalising the weird” problem.

Many companies struggle because they make something in high demand, but with extreme levels of competition . They need to “weird the normal” - i.e. take their generic offering, and make it stand out. Other companies (like Little Dish) also have the reverse problem; where they make something quite unusual but with limited market demand , because customers don’t quite “get it” or what value it offers. In these cases the job is to “normalise the weird” - i.e. take their peculiar thing, and give it mass appeal.

So that laid down the challenge for Little Dish:

How could we differentiate from competitors, AND significantly grow the category into the mainstream, simultaneously?

## II. Noticing

I believe that the key to a strategic breakthrough isn’t about gathering new information. Most companies are drowning in too much information already, quite frankly. No, instead it’s about noticing things in the information you already have .

You need to look for clues as to what this business really is for - and often they’re not that hard to spot, at least for an outsider.

In the case of Little Dish, we noticed some really odd things.

- Despite their tagline, built around “proper food” and exciting recipes, their more basic recipes that mums often make performed really strongly.

- Even more surprisingly, the LTV of their customers was higher the later they entered the brand.

This second point was particularly crazy. You see, a big part of their strategy was to target kids young (at 1), in order to lock them into the brand for the longest possible time, all the way up to 5 when they age out of it. All very sensible. But what was happening in reality, was that the LTV of customers who bought when their kids were 1 was lower than if they first bought when they were 3 or 4.

What the hell was going on here?

A bit of good old fashioned “insight” figured things out.

For anyone who’s had kids, you will know that when they are babies (i.e. 1), they don’t have a lot of agency, and will pretty much do (and eat) what you tell them to. They haven’t really figured out what they like yet.

However when you enter the toddler stage, things change.

(As a dad of a 2 year old and 5 year old, oh boy, take my word for it)

The little buggers start having a mind of their own . They start being fussy. They start saying “no”. They start having the most insanely irrational and infuriating opinions on everything, but especially food.

So mealtimes? They become a nightmare.

This then is when a parent will start to flounder and look for something - anything - that will be accepted by the kid, and bring a bit of peace to the dinner table. And so this is when a certain subset of highly motivated buyer would first try Little Dish.

- Parents of 1 year olds were just casual dabblers, indulging in a luxury purchase, which they could take or leave

- Parents of 3 year olds were desperate buyers, who would be extremely loyal if the product worked and the kid just bloody-well ate it

This was the truth of the brand all along, although nobody had ever really noticed it. And it also gave us our new hypothesis…

## III. The strategy

Rather than being a vehicle for culinary exploration (too niche), nutrition (often not an immediate concern for struggling parents), or convenience (too much competition), the real potential for Little Dish was this:

Creating healthy meals that 2-5 year old would actually eat, thus removing huge amounts of stress for the parents.

The new value here is low-drama mealtimes, driven not only by convenience but more importantly the enthusiasm of the kid .

The microwave meal is a great format for this for a number of reasons:

- Most kids love eating anything out of a package, much more so than home-cooked food, alas

- The meals could be designed and formulated for maximum kid-appeal

- The brand could do complex things with the recipes to make superficially unhealthy food (that kids want) be more nutritionally sound, such as smuggling in hidden-veg

This shift totally up-ended assumptions across the brand:

Instead of formulating meals with 1 year olds as the target ↓ Formulate with 3 year olds as the target

Although we still wanted to be acceptable for parents of babies, this wasn’t the true centre of gravity for the business - so thinking needed to shift to an older child.

Instead of focusing on sophisticated recipes ↓ Focus on crowd-pleasers

If we were targeting “kid acceptability” as our core value offering, we needed to make sure we gave them things that they would instantly say “yes” to.

Instead of pushing nutrition as far as possible ↓ Prioritise taste for the child

Although the products remained extremely healthy, instead of going as low as possible on things like salt, the recipes were adjusted to improve taste so we could be sure kids would finish them.

See what I mean?

What I want you to notice here is how one big idea can have this profound cascade of effects, and can yield many executional ideas without the need for much creativity.

That is what makes it a strategy.

We are not talking about a bland platitude that lacks sufficient specificity for us to take action (e.g. “delight the customer”). And we are also not talking about an itemised plan listing a bunch of sensible things to do but without a grander vision to bind them all together.

We are talking about identifying a unique form of value that the business can bring into the world, and then aligning everything within the business to drive that as hard as possible.

Something most companies never do.

## IV. Execution

I won’t labour the point here, other than to say that this tweak in direction meant a bunch of changes had to be made to the business to make it true.

Remember, what you have written down as your “strategy” means absolutely nothing until it is obvious to an outside observer that this is what you’re doing.

For Little Dish, this meant doing stuff like this:

- Designing new kid-focused packaging. The old packaging had been designed more from the POV of communicating to the parent on-shelf. But we knew that for kids to be excited by it, the product had to look fun so it would have some “pester power”. Thus all-new packaging was designed with this in mind.

- Adding “permissible junk food”. The big new frontier opened by this strategy was to take food that kids love but that parents feel guilty about, and create our own nutritious version. The first example of this is frozen chicken nuggets - the only nutritionally sound ones in the whole market, and already a hit product.

- Marketing shift towards “mealtime stress”. Instead of highlighting the products the way marketing used to do, the brand now positions itself as a remedy for mealtime stress; something that all parents understand and relate to.

## V. Results

Like I said at the outset, simply by enacting these changes, the brand has now 3x’d in size since the work was done - with that momentum still going strong at the time of writing, Q2 2026.

As Dean says:

> “We’ve seen a step change in our competitive position. It would be crazy to suggest that we have market power over the grocery multiples, but I do believe they now recognise us as the category expert; and there is clear blue water between the Little Dish brand proposition and alternatives (like retailer private label and other, copy-cat ranges) which are more straight product offerings”.

Beyond this, the beauty of true strategy - unlike tactical sales and marketing activities - is that it doesn’t only help you shift more product. It transforms the business holistically .

Two examples of this Dean has emphasised are:

- Improved recruitment and retention. The team has doubled in size since hiring has become far easier with a powerful story to sell to them. Plus, as Dean puts it “everyone enjoys being on a winning team”.

- Improved productivity. After a certain size, the CEO can’t micro-manage the whole organisation, so in that scenario you need to the strategy to step in, and act as the “instructions” that keeps everyone aligned and active. In Dean’s words, “the team is too big, and we’re doing too many projects for a founder to be the decision maker on everything (gatekeeper / bottleneck); it’s hugely clarifying to have a shared framework to help prioritise what we should and shouldn’t be doing”.

Finally, on a grander scale, Little Dish now has this huge cultural space it can own - stress free mealtimes with kids - which gives it the potential to enter into a wider conversation and become a brand with deep relevance and meaning.

This is crucial for the world in 2026 and beyond, where low barriers to entry means that widget-like brands will be swamped, and the winners will be those with a charismatic vision.

## VI. The last words

Let me tell you, not everyone is as good at staying on-strategy and executing with conviction as Hillary and Dean are. The personality of the leaders truly is the secret sauce in all of this. So I asked Dean what his advice would be to other CEOs wanting to achieve similar results.

His answer?

“Listen to Dolly Parton”

As some of you may know, this is a reference to her wise counsel to “find out who you are, and do it on purpose”. Which is exactly what Little Dish have done.

This isn’t easy, because it takes the insight and humility to recognise the delusions we all carry about our businesses. To unravel the fantasy identity we have built for ourselves, so we can reveal the true one that lurks underneath.

Honestly, not many have the chops for this. Not many are really interested in the truth.
