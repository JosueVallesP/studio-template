## Essay

There are a few things that the internet has no shortage of among them are people telling you how to get rich.

It’s a reflection of simple market economics, as long as there is demand, the supply will be steady.

99% of the time, it’s people who got rich by selling that dream, never by doing the thing that they teach

A scam as old as time.

But in 2018, a very wealthy Venture capitalist decided to give away the “secrets to wealth” for free.

Ravikant published a 40-tweet thread on Twitter.

He titled it “how to get rich without getting lucky” and to date, this is still one of the best publicly available resources on the topic.

I recently revisited that thread, and this article is my notes on it.

## What is it that you really want?

Status, money, and wealth???

Many people would say that money and wealth are interchangeable… they are not.

Wealth is productive ownership. Think businesses, programs, investments, and other assets capable of producing value without requiring you to continuously put in work.

Money, on the other hand, is an IOU that is given to you by society. You created value in the past, and money acts as a store of that value.

It represents society’s obligation to give you value later.

It is essentially the accounting mechanism that we use to transfer claims on wealth.

So remember to make that distinction, you can receive a lot of money without necessarily having built significant wealth, while someone else could have a lot of productive assets without holding most of their net worth as literal cash.

Now let’s talk about the third thing, status. Naval defines this as your relative social hierarchy.

This is what we had in place as a society long before we streamlined commerce and had better ways to reward the people in our tribes who created most value.

It’s still ingrained in our minds, and we use money to signal status now, but status is the one thing you should avoid, because status is a zero sum game.

It’s inherently positional, if someone climbs to number 2 someone else has to drop to 3.

Wealth, on the other hand, is different.

Wealth can be created. My owning a productive asset does not inherently prevent you from creating one yourself.

Wealth is a positive sum game.

## 1. Specific Knowledge

> “Specific knowledge is often highly technical or creative. It cannot be outsourced or automated.”  - Naval

The first step is becoming hard to replace. Scarcity is one of the most important attributes of value.

So when I say become hard to replace i don’t mean become good at x, y or z.

It’s not that simple.

You can be very skilled and still be extremely replaceable.

If a skill can be reduced to a syllabus and taught to thousands, then eventually there will be thousands lining up to perform it and replace you.

Scarcity disappears... you become a commodity.

Specific knowledge is different. It is what is birthed from the cross pollination of your curiosity, temperament, experience, and natural ability. It is almost always learned by doing, not by cramming theory.

When it is taught, it is done through apprenticeship, one on one in the field doing the thing. Humans have understood this for millennia... it has become a lost art, but i digress.

This is why telling someone to simply, “learn a valuable skill” is completely missing the point.

The real advantage is the ability to do something valuable in a way that cannot be easily separated from you.

A programmer who can write code is useful but a programmer who also understands design, architecture, human nature... that’s an entirely different beast.

Each individual ability may be learnable, but the combination, coupled with their own personal life experiences and belief systems, becomes difficult to reproduce.

So the goal moves from becoming the best person in the world at one obvious thing to becoming a monopoly of one.

But there is a problem.... You could possess a truly valuable and specific knowledge and still capture very little of the value that you end up creating.

You could be brilliant anonymously. Other people could own the company and take the credit and collect most of the upside. Steve Jobs didn’t actually make the iPhone, but he got all the glory and his company got all the money.

Essentially, being valuable is not enough, you have to attach yourself to your work.

## 2. Accountability

> “Embrace accountability, and take business risks under your own name.”  - Naval

In 2005 Michael Burry put his reputation on the line and bet against the housing market... that was nothing short of crazy at the time, but he did it anyway.

In 2008 his bet paid off, his reputation increased by orders of magnitude

The truth is that everyone wants the upside of being right, but no one wants their name on the call sheet when shit hits the fan.

Accountability requires you to accept both.

When you attach your name to something, you create a direct relationship between your reputation and the outcome.

If you are wrong, everyone knows, but if you are right, they will know that too. Taking accountability creates this wave of leverage that moves in front of you.

Every correct move you make builds a record, the next person down the line doesn’t have to judge you from scratch, your previous work has already spoken for you.

I mean, it is a double edged sword, but that willingness to accept visible risk is what builds the credibility required for greater responsibility later.

But even accountability has a ceiling, to maximize on its value, you have to do it on your own terms.

Think of music artists, they have their names on everything they make but their labels and distributors take comically large amounts of the profits they generate.

And the exact same thing will happen to you too.

Suppose you are unusually talented. You put your name behind your decisions. You take responsibility for the outcome.

And then you are paid the same salary whether your work creates $100,000 or $10 million in value.

Now we have another problem.

## 3. Ownership

> “You’re not going to get rich renting out your time. You must own equity.”

I have nothing against salaries, but the problem we have here is a mathematical one.

Your input and output are intertwined... you work for an hour, and you get paid for that hour. When you stop working, the money stops flowing.

This applies to almost everyone, really expensive lawyers, even more expensive surgeons, those consultants who you pay like $10k just to tell you what you already knew but with a fancy slide deck... all of them.

As long as your income is tied to your time, then there is a hard cap on how much you can make.

Naval’s argument is that extreme wealth generally appears when this relationship ends... when the amount you can earn is no longer tightly constrained by the number of hours you personally work.

Ownership is the answer here.

Equity in a company.

Software.

Intellectual property.

Media. ( like a newsletter )

Investments.

A product.

The idea is to create or acquire something that can continue to produce value even after the original work is completed.

Now, don’t take this to mean that ownership is magically better in every possible circumstance. If you are a business owner, you already know what I mean.

Ownership means more risk.

Everyone gets paid before you do. One bad month for the company means nothing to everyone except you, because they still expect to see that cheque.

But for all that downside, there is an equal amount of upside.

The only issue is that that upside is not guaranteed, and to harvest it you need something more refined than raw knowledge.

## 4. Judgment

> “Judgement requires experience, but can be built faster by learning foundational skills.”  - Naval

Knowledge and judgment are not the same thing.

Knowledge is the information that you have already internalized and understood, hence the “know”

Judgment, on the other hand, is how you make your decisions based on the information you have.

The more refined your judgment is, the less information you need to make decisions.

What markets should you enter? What ads should you run and when? When should you keep pushing, and when should you cut your losses and quit?

The answers to these questions do not exist, and they never will.

This is why judgment takes time to build.

You have to make decisions, watch the consequences, course correct and slowly become less wrong.

When I began to write this essay, I had judgment as the 5th step, not the 4th. I posted a note about it on my Substack, and someone called me out on it. We had a discussion, and I realized he was right, and so here we are.

That slightly wrong decision only cost me a few minutes to correct. But a similar miscalculation while controlling a billion dollars, a company of 10,000 employees or an audience of 50 million people can be catastrophic.

But the same applies to a brilliant decision.... but in the opposite direction.

At the beginning of your career, society does not trust your judgment enough to place much behind it.

Over time, if you demonstrate that you are right often enough, you are given bigger decisions.

More money.

More people.

More attention.

More responsibility.

The quality of your judgment may improve gradually.

But the consequences can grow exponentially.

Which finally brings us to the multiplier.

## 5. Leverage

> “Give me a lever long enough, and a place to stand, and I will move the earth.” - Archimedes

10.5 million light-years.... that’s how long a stick you would need for one human to move the Earth. I know you were thinking it so I decided to get that out of the way.

Let’s continue. Without leverage, even exceptional ability remains constrained by what one human can physically do.

You could be capable of digging faster than everyone else but there is still a limit to how much dirt you can move with your hands.

Naval separates business leverage into 3, labour, capital, and products that can be replicated at near zero cost ( code and media)

Labour is simple enough, 100 people can do more than one person. Capital is similar, if you have the right judgement, more capital to back up that judgement magnifies the consequence of being correct.

The issue with labour and capital is that they are permissioned. you need 100 people to agree to work for you and you need to convince your rich Uncle Tom to wire you $300,000 for your AI startup.

Code and media are different

“Code and media are permissionless leverage.”

You don’t need anyone’s permission to write code or write an essay, record a podcast or upload a vlog. and once created these things can be copied at literally zero cost.

Comparative margin expansion: As revenue scales to $10M, hardware caps out at a 30% net margin due to physical COGS, while software scales to an 88% net margin.

Your essay can reach hundreds of thousands of people, but you only have to write it once.

And by the way, this is part of the reason I started the 90 day writing circle, to build a newsletter as an asset that takes advantage of this, go here to learn more about it.

The thing about leverage tho is that it’s neutral, you need to give it direction or it will just amplify whatever it picks up.

It just makes the consequences of what you already are much much bigger.

Naval calls it a force multiplier for judgement.

Up until this point, every step has been... for lack of a better word, visible. Or easy enough to identify.

You have some specific knowledge, you put your name on your work, you own the upside (and downside), you have become a great judge and through all this you have acquired and been able to apply leverage.

You are rich.

But to become wealthy, to have fuck you money, you have to make sure you don’t keep restarting, you need to let the 8th wonder of the world, according to Einstein, do its job.

## 6. Compounding

> “All returns in life, whether in wealth, relationships or knowledge, come from compound interest.”  - Naval

In truth compounding is boring, but only in the beginning.

Everyone understands compounding to some extent... and everyone only thinks of compounding in relation to money.

Put money in the S&P 500 and retire after 30 years with a million dollars.

But compounding extends far beyond that, and dare i say it’s the literal lifeline of this entire framework.

Let me explain.

Compounding rewards continuity.

Knowledge compounds because new ideas attach themselves to things you already understand.... that ends up creating a latticework of specific knowledge

Relationships compound because trust removes friction. People who have worked together successfully for ten years can make decisions faster than two randoms who need contracts, verification and constant reassurance.

Reputation compounds because every good outcome makes people a tad more willing to bet on the next one.

Capital compounds for the obvious mathematical reason.

And all of these forms of capital can begin compounding together .

Your knowledge gets you better opportunities.

Those opportunities improve your judgment.

Good judgment builds your reputation.

Reputation attracts capital and talented people.

Those give you more leverage.

Leverage creates larger outcomes.

Larger outcomes increase your ownership and reputation.

Then the wheel turns again.

At some point you will hear people call you lucky, but don’t blame them because honestly, from the outside that is exactly what it looks like.

15 years of work and quiet compounding becomes an overnight success story.

This is also why Naval places a ton of emphasis on playing long-term games with long term people.

Repeated interaction allows trust to accumulate, and trust allows increasingly ambitious things to happen with less friction.

I have asked people this question many times and sometimes they will give me a number

“F you money is 10 million, no 20 million”

“Holidays in Cannes with the wife and kids and a garage full of v12 engines”

Not having to be somewhere I don’t want to be.

Not having to work with people i don’t like

Having an empty calendar but being busy all the same.

To me, the F in fuck you money stands for freedom
