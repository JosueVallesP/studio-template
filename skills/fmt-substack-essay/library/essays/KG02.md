## 1: The Primacy of Craft (You Have to Be a Good Writer)

## 1.1 The Wall

Here is the number one truth that growth coaches leave out of their funnels: You have to get good at writing. There is no way around this wall. No growth hack, perfect posting schedule, or formatting trick will save an essay that has nothing interesting to say.

When I started on Substack a year ago, I already had some writing practice. Years back, during my university days, I ran a free WordPress site where I wrote deep-dives about music. It never gained an audience and it never made a dime, but I loved doing it.

My Substack was created with a singular, selfish intent: to serve as a clean place for my thoughts to live outside of my own head. Audience growth was completely secondary; practicing the craft was the entire point.

If you take only one directive away from this entire playbook, let it be this: No amount of algorithm gaming or daily consistency can fix bad writing.

## 1.2 Writing Etiquette

Most people who start a newsletter are fully literate. They know how to form sentences, but they don’t understand why other people’s work sounds punchy and professional while their own text feels heavy, slow, and amateur.

The difference isn’t talent. It’s writing etiquette. Here is the exact checklist of what to do and what to ruthlessly cut out to fix your reader retention.

## 1. Stop Talking to a Stadium

- Mistake: Writing to a crowd. Beginners use phrases like “Hey guys,” “Welcome back everyone,” or “As many of you know.” This immediately dilutes the connection.

- Rule: Write to one single person. Use the word “You.” When someone opens your newsletter on their phone or laptop, they are reading it alone. Your text should feel like a private, direct letter sent from one friend straight to another. Talk to the reader as an individual, not as a metric on a dashboard.

## 2. Kill Your Adverbs

- Mistake: Propping up weak, boring verbs with adverbs (words ending in “-ly”). Phrases like “he ran quickly,” “she argued passionately,” or “I strictly believe.” * The Rule: Cut the adverbs and use a heavier verb. Adverbs usually show that you don’t trust your own writing. Instead of “ran quickly,” write “sprinted.” Instead of “argued passionately,” write “demanded.” Strong, singular verbs give your text immediate authority.

## 3. Stop Using Passive Voice

- Mistake: Letting your sentences be acted upon instead of taking action. For example: “The article was noticed by Michael Burry,” or “The mistake was made by the team.” This creates heavy, slow prose.

- Rule: Use active voice. Make the subject do the heavy lifting. Write: “Michael Burry noticed the article,” or “The team made a mistake.” Active voice creates momentum and pulls the reader down the page.

## 4. Keep It Simple and Visual

- The Sentences: Long, winding sentences confuse people. Keep them brief. Give the reader’s brain a break.

- The Language: Don’t use fancy, high-level words just to look smart. If you use a word like “utilize” when you could just say “use,” you lose the reader. Write exactly how you talk in real life when you’re explaining a smart idea to a friend at a bar.

- The Imagery: Abstract ideas don’t stick. Don’t just talk about general “economic hardship.” Talk about “shoveling dirt for $10 a day.” Give the reader concrete images they can actually picture in their mind.

## 5. The 10% Editing Rule

The editing process is simple math: Second Draft = First Draft – 10% . When you sit down to edit your draft, your job isn’t to add words to look sophisticated.

Your job is to locate the “warm-up fluff” at the beginning, the repetitive sentences in the middle, and the safe, boring filler you used while your brain was getting up to speed, and ruthlessly cut it out.

If a sentence doesn’t actively move your point forward, it is actively killing your retention.

## 1.4 Spacing as Visual Pacing

Digital reading is highly visual. If a subscriber opens your email and sees a massive, unbroken wall of dense text, their brain treats it like schoolwork and they will close the tab instantly. You have to use spacing as visual punctuation.

- Do Not Copy the LinkenIn Robots: Avoid the artificial style of making every single sentence its own line. It looks cheap, robotic, and desperate for attention.

- Vary Your Paragraph Length: Create a rhythm on the page. Use a short, punchy sentence to speed up the reader’s pace. Then, follow it with a three-to-four-sentence paragraph when you need them to slow down, anchor their attention, and take in a more complex idea. The visual white space on the screen gives the reader room to breathe.

## 1.5 Learning the Craft via Osmosis (The Power of Reading)

You cannot become a high-signal writer if you only consume short-form internet garbage. You can’t write clean prose if your brain is fed on a strict diet of short tweets, corporate LinkedIn posts, and basic marketing blogs.

A massive portion of writing etiquette cannot be taught through a checklist, it has to be absorbed through osmosis .

When you read exceptional fiction, classic literature, or great long-form journalism, you are subconsciously downloading formatting, rhythm, structure, and tone directly into your brain.

You begin to instinctively understand how a story flows, how tension is built, and how a sentence lands with real impact.

If you read shallow content, you will write shallow content. If you read deeply researched, beautifully paced books, you will naturally start executing those exact same reflexes when you sit down in front of a blank keyboard.

## Recommended Reading List for learning how to write:

- On Writing: A Memoir of the Craft by Stephen King (The ultimate masterclass on the operational habits of writing).

- The Copywriter’s Handbook by Robert Bly (The definitive guide on clear, persuasive, action-driven text).

- Confessions of an Advertising Man by David Ogilvy (The blueprint for writing copy that commands human attention).

- The Shapes of Stories by Kurt Vonnegut (A short lecture series mapping out the timeless structures of human storytelling).
