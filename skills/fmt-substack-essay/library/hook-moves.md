# Substack hook moves: titles, subtitles, openings

On LinkedIn the hook is two lines. On Substack it is spread across three things the reader meets in order, and each one has its own job:

1. **The title.** Read in an inbox, a feed or a Notes card, next to dozens of others. Its job is the open.
2. **The subtitle.** Read in the same glance. It finishes, qualifies or explains the title. Many writers leave it empty, and that is a choice with a cost.
3. **The opening.** The first 100 to 400 words after the open. Its job is to earn the scroll, and on long essays to tell the reader what the rest will give them.

A move is a job done to the reader, not a sentence shape. Two titles with different words can be the same move. We file by the job, because the job tells you when to use it and when a draft is failing.

This is the pilot, built from 57 essays: the top 10 of five newsletters (Alex M H Smith, Kaguura, Naval's Archive, Dan Koe, Kieran Drew) and the 7 essays in Justin Welsh's "Ambition" tag. Every example below is a real title or opening from those essays. IDs point to `essays-sorted.csv` (the index, with tags and engagement) and `essays/<ID>.md` (the full text of each essay).

**How to read the numbers.** Each essay's engagement (likes plus restacks) is shown against the median of its own newsletter's set, so "6.6x" means 6.6 times that writer's typical top post. These are each writer's best posts already, so a low number means low within their best work, not a failure. Kieran Drew's ten posts sit within a few likes of each other, so they say nothing about performance. Justin Welsh's three oldest posts in the tag show 1 or 2 likes, which looks like a cross-posting artifact, so they are left out of performance notes.

## The card

Same fields as the LinkedIn cards:

- **Job.** The question in the reader's head that the move answers.
- **Mechanism.** Why it works, in a sentence or two.
- **What it needs.** The raw material you must have before you can use it. No material, no move.
- **Shape.** The skeleton in plain words. Not a template to fill.
- **How it fails.** Common ways a draft gets the shape right and the job wrong.
- **Good for.** What the move tends to buy.
- **Examples.** From the library, with IDs.

Where a Substack move does the same job as a LinkedIn hook move, the card says so ("LinkedIn: 1"), and the LinkedIn card's "how it fails" list applies too.

# Part 1: Title moves

## T1. Proof, then payoff

LinkedIn: 1.

**Job.** "Why should I listen to you, and what exactly do I get?"

**Mechanism.** An exact result earns the right to teach, and the payoff names the thing handed over. In a title the numbers carry more weight than on LinkedIn, because the reader decides to open on the title alone and the numbers are the only proof that fits.

**What it needs.** A real result with exact numbers (subscribers, revenue, time, clients). A payoff that comes from that exact result: the method, the full process, the real project.

**Shape.** The exact result, with a time frame. Then a promise of how, usually "everything", "how it actually works" or "here's what I did".

**How it fails.**
- The number is rounded into nothing ("thousands of subscribers").
- The body is a general essay and the promised "everything I did" never shows up.
- The result belongs to someone else and the title hides that.

**Good for.** Opens and saves from people trying to get the same result. Leads, when the result is client work (AS09 is the post most likely to bring in clients, even though it gets fewer likes).

**Examples.**
- KG02 (6.6x): "20,585 New Subscribers in 90 Days: Here's Everything I Did" / subtitle: "For the 43 people who DM'd me asking how Substack actually works."
- AS09: "How one of my strategy projects actually works" / subtitle: "Growing Little Dish 3x with a couple of simple decisions" (the proof sits in the subtitle)
- KD03 (secondary): "5 important lessons on success (from 6 years of writing online)"

## T2. The how-to with the catch removed

**Job.** "Can I do this, without the thing that usually stops me?"

**Mechanism.** A plain "how to" names an outcome the reader wants. The addition in brackets or after it removes the reader's main reason to doubt it: too long, needs luck, needs expertise, too complicated, out of date. The bracket does most of the work. Without it, the title reads like every other how-to on the same topic.

**What it needs.** A method that really does get the outcome under the stated condition. If the title says 4 hours, the method must take about 4 hours. The condition has to be the objection your readers actually have.

**Shape.** "How to [outcome]" plus one condition: a time box ("in 4 hours", "in 1 day", "in 30 days"), a removed requirement ("without getting lucky", "when you don't have the expertise"), a simplicity claim ("the simple way") or a date ("in 2026 (things changed)").

**How it fails.**
- The condition is a lie, or so loose it means nothing.
- The outcome is so big ("fix your entire life") that only readers who already trust you will believe the condition.
- The body hands the real work to a template or a product, so the essay doesn't keep the title's promise on its own (DK05).

**Good for.** Opens and saves. The time-box version is the strongest in this set.

**Examples.**
- KG03 (6.5x): "How to Turn An Idea Into an Essay (in 4 hours)"
- DK01 (3.1x): "How to fix your entire life in 1 day" / subtitle: "do this before 2026"
- KG09: "How to Make F#ck You Money (without getting lucky)"
- KD02: "How to write when you don't have the expertise"
- DK10: "A dopamine detox to reset your life in 30 days"
- DK05: "How to start a one-person business in 2026 (things changed)"
- KD06: "How to get what you want in life (the simple way)"

## T3. Think like the person you want to be

**Job.** "Can I become that kind of person?"

**Mechanism.** The title names a kind of person the reader admires and wants to be mistaken for (a strategic genius, a natural strategist, someone who sounds intelligent). The promise is a way of thinking, not a tactic, so it feels bigger than a how-to and more personal. It works on status: the reader opens because they want the label.

**What it needs.** A real model of how that kind of person thinks, with parts you can name. Ideally proof that you crossed the gap yourself (DK02 opens with the writer as a kid who didn't think he was smart).

**Shape.** "How [admired type] think", "How to think like a [label]", "How to [skill] [admired adverb]", or "The Art of [skill]". Often with a bracket that makes it concrete or keepable: "(print this)", "(5d thinking)".

**How it fails.**
- The label is generic ("wildly successful") and the body sprawls (DK04 sits at 1.2x, against 3.1x and 5.6x for Dan Koe's DK01 and DK02).
- The body describes the person instead of handing over the thinking.
- No proof the writer has the trait.

**Good for.** Reach and saves. Being known for a way of thinking.

**Examples.**
- DK02 (5.6x): "How to articulate yourself intelligently" / subtitle: "The inner album of greatest hits, and 3 frameworks to practice with"
- AS04 (1.2x): "How natural strategists think (print this)" / subtitle: "Brainwash yourself until you think this way"
- DK09: "How to think like a strategic genius (5d thinking)"
- DK04: "The Art Of Strategic Thinking (How To Become Wildly Successful)"

## T4. The hidden reason

**Job.** "Why does this keep happening, and is it happening to me?"

**Mechanism.** The reader already sees the problem (most people never make it, brands are boring, creators are struggling, I keep making dumb mistakes). The title promises the cause behind it, which the reader can't get from looking at the problem. It works when the cause comes from somewhere the reader wouldn't look (math, physics, a hidden cost).

**What it needs.** A problem your readers already feel, named in their words. A cause you can actually show, ideally from a field outside the topic (KG01 uses Price's Law). The essay has to name the cause, not just describe the problem at length.

**Shape.** "Why [common problem]", "The [adjective] reason most people never [outcome]", "The cause of my [problem]", "Why [popular thing] is [hurting] your [thing]". Optionally "(and how to survive)" or "(Do you do this too?)" to pull the reader in.

**How it fails.**
- The cause is the obvious one ("they gave up too early").
- The paywall falls before the cause is named, so free readers get only the problem (AS03, KG06).
- The title promises an industry-wide cause and the body is about the writer's own doubts (KD08 is warm, but the title oversells).

**Good for.** Reach. KG01 (71.4x) is the biggest outlier in the whole library, and it is this move with a named law behind it.

**Examples.**
- KG01 (71.4x): "The Mathematical Reason Most People Never "Make It"" / subtitle: "The uncomfortable truth about effort, outcome, and the math that connects them all"
- AS03: "Why most modern brands are so frigging boring" / subtitle: "The end of relatability and what comes next"
- KG06: "Why Nature Allowed a Trillionaire to Exist" / subtitle: "Extreme wealth is not a moral failure. It's a physics problem... and that's worse"
- KD10: "Why AI is crippling your business opportunities"
- KD08: "Why creators are dying (and how to survive)"
- KD05: "The cause of my stupidest mistakes (Do you do this too?)"

## T5. The plain command

**Job.** "Should I do that? Why?"

**Mechanism.** A short instruction, often ending in a full stop, with nothing else. It reads like advice from a friend rather than a headline. The reader opens to find out why, or because they know they should and haven't. It works best when the command is something the reader avoids, not something they don't know about.

**What it needs.** A behavior your readers know they should do and don't, or do wrong. A reason they avoid it that the essay can name (JW01 names a sharper reason than fear of rejection). A real story or proof in the body, because the title gives no credibility of its own.

**Shape.** An imperative in two to five words ("Ask for what you want.", "Go make some friends.", "Be Incompressible"). Variants: "Do X, not Y" ("Play Natural Games, Not Status Games"), "Don't X. Do Y." ("Don't write about a topic. Write about a transformation."), and the plea ("I'm begging you to write more essays").

**How it fails.**
- The command is a cliché and the body adds nothing new to it.
- The command needs a reason the title doesn't hint at and the subtitle doesn't give (most NA posts have no subtitle).
- The plea version without stakes reads as nagging.

**Good for.** Relationships and loyalty. Readers who already know the writer open these.

**Examples.**
- JW01 (2.2x): "Ask for what you want." / subtitle: "There's something you want. There's a person who can give it to you."
- JW04: "Go make some friends." / subtitle: "Wildly successful entrepreneurs are hiding in plain sight."
- DK08: "I'm begging you to write more essays" / subtitle: "The best way to learn faster, think deeper, and... save humanity"
- NA07: "Be Incompressible"
- NA08: "Play Natural Games, Not Status Games"
- KD04: "Don't write about a topic. Write about a transformation."

## T6. The paradox

**Job.** "How can both of those be true?"

**Mechanism.** The title joins two things that seem to cancel each other (disciplined and spontaneous, luck and purpose, a rule that worked too well, missing someone who doesn't exist). The reader can't resolve it from the title, so they open. A good paradox also works as a quote on its own, which is why it spreads.

**What it needs.** A real resolution: a way the two things fit together that the reader hadn't seen. If the essay only says "you need both", the title was a trick.

**Shape.** An instruction with its opposite attached ("Live a Disciplined Life, Spontaneously", "Get Lucky, On Purpose"). A good thing that became the problem ("The rule that worked too well."). A riddle that can't be literally true ("Missing someone who doesn't exist.").

**How it fails.**
- The resolution is "balance".
- The paradox is clever but the reader has no stake in it.
- A riddle title with no subtitle, so nobody knows what the essay is about (JW07 fixes this with a subtitle that tells the whole story).

**Good for.** Reach and reposts. NA01 (19.6x) is the second biggest outlier in the library.

**Examples.**
- NA01 (19.6x): "Live a Disciplined Life, Spontaneously"
- JW03: "The rule that worked too well." / subtitle: "It made every part of my life better, until suddenly it didn't."
- NA06: "Get Lucky, On Purpose"
- JW07: "Missing someone who doesn't exist." / subtitle: "My old boss texted me during the pandemic. I went back for eight months before I realized I wasn't missing the job."

## T7. The coined term

LinkedIn: closest to 13 (the reframe).

**Job.** "What is that, and is it me?"

**Mechanism.** The writer names something the reader has felt but had no word for (a second choosing, selective editing, hyper-abundance). A name makes a vague feeling into a thing you can think about and repeat. The title only works if the reader can guess what the term means, or the subtitle tells them.

**What it needs.** A term you coined for a real pattern, and a one-sentence definition. A subtitle that explains it in plain words, unless the term explains itself.

**Shape.** The term alone ("Selective editing.", "Your second choosing."), the term with a frame ("Welcome to the era of hyper-abundance (it's not as nice as it sounds)", "Radical Simplicity: The physics of getting things done"), or a branded model ("HUMAN 3.0 – A Map To Reach The Top 1%").

**How it fails.**
- The term means nothing to a stranger and the subtitle doesn't translate it. Coined-term titles are among the weakest titles in this set when that happens: DK06 (0.5x), KG10 (0.4x), AS10 (0.8x).
- The term is a fancy name for an old idea.
- A launch of the writer's own model to readers who don't know the model yet (DK06 reads as reference material, not an essay).

**Good for.** Being remembered for an idea. It's the title move most tied to intellectual property, which is why it's worth getting right even though it's risky. JW02 (1.4x) is the version that works: plain words and a subtitle that makes it personal.

**Examples.**
- JW02 (1.4x): "Your second choosing." / subtitle: "The first time around, you barely knew yourself."
- JW05: "Selective editing." / subtitle: "The stories we make up about our heroes (and why we shouldn't)."
- AS10: "Welcome to the era of hyper-abundance (it's not as nice as it sounds)" / subtitle: "How do you sell to people who already have way too much?"
- KG10: "Radical Simplicity: The physics of getting things done" / subtitle: "and how to stop being your own bottleneck"
- DK06: "HUMAN 3.0 – A Map To Reach The Top 1%"

## T8. The arguable claim

LinkedIn: 6 (the common advice is wrong), and close to 17.

**Job.** "Is that true? I'm not sure I agree."

**Mechanism.** A flat statement the reader can argue with. Usually it denies something the reader believes or does. The reader opens to defend their view or to see the argument. It only works if the claim is specific enough to be wrong.

**What it needs.** A claim you hold that some of your readers will push back on. A real argument, built on your own reasoning or experience.

**Shape.** A denial and a replacement ("Intentions Don't Matter. Actions Do. That's Why Being Ethical Is Hard."), a verdict on a popular practice plus a fix ("Writing with AI is dumb. Do this instead."), or a superlative ("The most powerful money makers are...").

**How it fails.**
- The claim is a safe one wearing a bold sentence.
- Written in generic language that sounds like nobody (NA04, 0.4x, opens with "In our rapidly-evolving digital age").
- "Do this instead" with no real "this".

**Good for.** Point of view. Comments and replies.

**Examples.**
- NA05: "Intentions Don't Matter. Actions Do. That's Why Being Ethical Is Hard." / subtitle: "Most people are good in their heads. The world doesn't live there."
- KD07: "Writing with AI is dumb. Do this instead."
- NA04: "The most powerful money makers are "Individual brands" (a typo in the published title; shown as a warning)

## T9. The reader, named, with stakes

LinkedIn: closest to 7 (the call-out), aimed at the reader instead of a third party.

**Job.** "Is this about me?"

**Mechanism.** The title names a kind of person ("if you make content online", "if you have multiple interests", "every leader with a scrap of ambition", "if you're so smart") and attaches a consequence. The reader who fits the description feels addressed, and the consequence makes it urgent. It sorts the audience in one line: the right reader opens, the wrong one doesn't, which is fine.

**What it needs.** A precise picture of who you're writing for, named by what they do or are, in words they'd use about themselves. A real consequence, or a real problem they have.

**Shape.** "If you [are X], [warning or problem]", "Every [reader] with [trait] should [do this]", "If you're so [X], why aren't you [Y]?", or a verdict in second person ("No One Will Remember Your Name").

**How it fails.**
- The group is so wide nobody feels named ("if you want to succeed").
- The consequence is vague.
- A verdict on the reader with nothing in the body that earns it (KG04 is mostly paywalled).

**Good for.** Reach inside a defined audience. Two of the top eight posts by relative engagement use it (DK03 3.9x, AS01 3.05x), and NA03 (2.2x) sits just behind them.

**Examples.**
- DK03 (3.9x): "If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years" / subtitle: "They want to put you in a box, don't let them. This is your last advantage."
- AS01 (3.05x): "If you make content online, here's the problem" / subtitle: "It was fun while it lasted"
- NA03 (2.2x): "If You Don't Commit to Meaningful Work, Life Will Fill Your Time with Busywork"
- AS08 (1.3x): "Every leader with a scrap of ambition should use this content system"
- NA09: "If You're So Smart, Why Aren't You Happy?"
- KG04: "No One Will Remember Your Name"

## T10. The shift

LinkedIn: close to 17 (a bet on the future).

**Job.** "What's changing, and am I on the right side of it?"

**Mechanism.** The title says something the reader relies on is ending or about to change, and promises what comes next. It plays on the fear of being left behind and the hope of getting in early.

**What it needs.** A change you can show with evidence, not just feel. A specific successor: what replaces the old thing.

**Shape.** "The death of [X] (and what comes next)", "This [simple thing] is about to [big outcome]". Often appears as a secondary move in subtitles ("It was fun while it lasted", "The end of relatability and what comes next").

**How it fails.**
- "The death of X" for something that isn't dying.
- The "what comes next" is behind a paywall, so the free reader gets the funeral and not the successor (AS02, 0.6x).

**Good for.** Point of view. Being referenced later if the call was right.

**Examples.**
- AS02: "The death of strategy (and what comes next)" / subtitle: "A guide for businesses in the era of hyper-competition"
- AS07: "This simple question is about to make some people crazy rich." / subtitle: "Decades of strategic orthodoxy is being unraveled"

## T11. The saying, explained

LinkedIn: closest to 9 (the line someone said).

**Job.** "I know that line. What's actually behind it?"

**Mechanism.** The title is a saying the reader already knows: an idiom, a cliché, a famous quote. Recognition gets the open. The promise is that the essay will explain the mechanism behind words people repeat without thinking. KG08 says this out loud in its opening: "the title is actually one of the most useful things you'll ever hear... and almost nobody understands why."

**What it needs.** A saying your readers know. A real mechanism behind it that most people who say it don't understand. Credit if it's someone's quote.

**Shape.** The saying as the whole title ("You cannot compete with someone who is having fun", "Bored to death.", "Escape competition through authenticity").

**How it fails.**
- The essay restates the saying at length instead of explaining it.
- A quote title with "My thoughts on this" as the subtitle and a paywall before the thoughts (NA02).

**Good for.** Reach. Easy to share because the reader already likes the line.

**Examples.**
- KG08 (1.75x): "You cannot compete with someone who is having fun" / subtitle: "How to take your craft seriously without getting killed by the weight of it."
- JW06: "Bored to death." / subtitle: "At some point I got really good at my job. Nobody talks about what happens after that. I was bored to death."
- NA10: "Escape competition through authenticity"
- NA02: "Cynicism is easy. Mimicry is easy. Optimistic contrarians are the rarest breed." / subtitle: "My thoughts on this"

## T12. The reader's own question

**Job.** "That's exactly what I've been asking."

**Mechanism.** The title is the question already in the reader's head, in their words. No cleverness. The reader opens because the writer clearly understands their problem.

**What it needs.** The real question your readers ask, ideally one you hear from clients or replies. An answer the essay can give.

**Shape.** A plain question ("How do we win when everyone is the same?"). Usually with a subtitle that raises the stakes.

**How it fails.** The question is too broad to answer in an essay. The question is the writer's, not the reader's.

**Good for.** Trust. The reader feels understood before reading a word of the body.

**Examples.**
- AS05 (1.25x): "How do we win when everyone is the same?" / subtitle: "The fundamental strategy problem of our generation"

Thin card: one example in this set. Watch for more.

## T13. What happened to me

LinkedIn: closest to 4 (put the reader in the room) and 18 (the announcement).

**Job.** "What happened?"

**Mechanism.** News about the writer's life, stated plainly, with a number that makes it real. It only works on readers who already follow the writer. For them, it's the post they most want: KD01 is tied for Kieran Drew's most-liked post and is his least instructional one.

**What it needs.** Something that really happened to you that your readers would care about. An existing relationship with them.

**Shape.** "When I [specific event with a number]".

**How it fails.** Announcing to strangers. Drama inflated for the title.

**Good for.** Relationships. Warming readers up to a launch without pitching.

**Examples.**
- KD01: "When I lost 800 hours of my book"

Thin card.

## T14. What you're missing

**Job.** "What could I have that I don't?"

**Mechanism.** The title points at an advantage the reader doesn't know they have, or a version of themselves they could have been. It works on curiosity and a little regret.

**What it needs.** A real, specific capability or benefit, and a method in the body to get it. The subtitle usually has to say what it is.

**Shape.** "The [advantage] you were never taught to use", "In another life, you might have been [X]", "What [thing] will do for you".

**How it fails.** The title is vague and the subtitle has to do all the work. The benefit is well covered elsewhere (flow, talent). Both Kaguura examples underperform her other posts, and both are paywalled (KD09 sits in Kieran Drew's flat set, so it says nothing either way).

**Good for.** Opens from readers who already trust the writer. Weak for reach in this set.

**Examples.**
- KG05: "The Cognitive Edge You Were Never Taught to Use" / subtitle: "How to manufacture the zone, every single day and watch four hours feel like two"
- KG07: "In Another Life, You Might Have Been a Genius" / subtitle: "How to bypass talent and manufacture your own genius."
- KD09: "What writing will do for you"

## T15. The whole thing

**Job.** "How much am I getting?"

**Mechanism.** The size of the thing is the promise: a full book, a complete guide, everything. On Substack this works because readers are used to essays and a whole book in the inbox is an offer, not just a post.

**What it needs.** A truly complete piece of work. A reason it's free, or a real paid value being given away.

**Shape.** "[Title]: A Guide To [big outcome]" with a subtitle that states the size or price ("The full book, for free").

**How it fails.** Calling a long essay a book. Giving it away to readers who won't read 27,000 words in an email (DK07 sits at 0.75x on likes, but it's the post most likely to be saved and returned to).

**Good for.** Saves, restacks and a canonical piece to point new readers to.

**Examples.**
- DK07: "Purpose & Profit – A Guide To Discovering Your Life's Work" / subtitle: "The full book, for free"

Thin card.

## T16. The numbered promise

LinkedIn: 12.

**Job.** "How much am I getting, and can I scan it?"

**Mechanism.** A number sets the size of the promise. On Substack it usually lives in the subtitle, under a title that does something else.

**What it needs.** A real list where every item earns its place, and a credential for it.

**Shape.** "[Number] [things] on [topic] ([credential])", or a banner title with the number in the subtitle.

**How it fails.** The number is the only hook. A banner title too vague to open ("Conversations against mediocrity", AS06, 0.4x, the weakest in its set) with the list behind a paywall.

**Good for.** Saves.

**Examples.**
- KD03: "5 important lessons on success (from 6 years of writing online)"
- AS06: "Conversations against mediocrity" / subtitle: "The 5 questions every company should be asking right now"

# Part 2: Subtitle jobs

The subtitle is read in the same glance as the title. It isn't a second hook. It does one of the jobs below for the title it sits under. Choose the job by asking what the title leaves the reader unsure about.

| Job | Use it when the title... | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **U1. Explains the title** | uses a coined term, a riddle or a reframe a stranger won't get | JW02 "The first time around, you barely knew yourself." / AS10 "How do you sell to people who already have way too much?" / KG06 "Extreme wealth is not a moral failure. It's a physics problem... and that's worse" |
| **U2. Makes the payoff concrete** | promises something big or abstract | AS09 "Growing Little Dish 3x with a couple of simple decisions" / DK02 "...and 3 frameworks to practice with" / KG05 "...watch four hours feel like two" / AS06 "The 5 questions every company should be asking right now" |
| **U3. Names the reader or their feeling** | could be for anyone | KG02 "For the 43 people who DM'd me asking how Substack actually works." / DK10 "Because most of modern life has become a blur, and you're tired of it" / JW01 "There's something you want. There's a person who can give it to you." |
| **U4. Raises the stakes** | is clear but doesn't say why it matters now | KG01 "The uncomfortable truth about effort, outcome, and the math that connects them all" / DK09 "When everyone outsources their thinking to machines, thinking becomes your competitive advantage" / AS05 "The fundamental strategy problem of our generation" |
| **U5. Tells the story, including the ending** | is a riddle, an idiom or a paradox | JW07 "My old boss texted me during the pandemic. I went back for eight months before I realized I wasn't missing the job." / JW03 "It made every part of my life better, until suddenly it didn't." |
| **U6. Says what it is** | could be mistaken for a regular post | DK07 "The full book, for free" / KG09 "My notes on Naval Ravikant's guide to building wealth" |
| **U7. Sets the tone with a joke** | is serious and the writer's voice isn't | AS01 "It was fun while it lasted" / AS04 "Brainwash yourself until you think this way" / DK08 "...and... save humanity" |
| **U8. Adds a deadline** | is timeless but the moment isn't | DK01 "do this before 2026" |
| **U0. None** | carries everything alone | All ten Kieran Drew posts; eight of ten Naval's Archive posts |

**Notes.**
- Telling the ending (U5) seems backwards and works. The reader already knows what happened, so they open for the why. It turns a riddle title from a gamble into a clear promise.
- A coined-term title without U1 is the most common way a good idea gets a weak title in this set.
- "My thoughts on this" (NA02) is a subtitle with no job.
- Going without a subtitle (U0) works when the title is complete on its own (a plain command, a how-to with its condition built in). It fails when the title is a riddle, a coined term or a bare quote.

# Part 3: Opening moves

The opening is everything from the first line to the point where the reader knows what the essay will give them. In this set it runs from 30 words (a quote and one line) to about 600 (a story, a turn and a promise). Long essays often end the opening with an explicit promise line ("Here I'm going to lay out...", "So let me break it down"), and the promise line is worth checking in every draft: if the reader can't say what they'll get by the end of the opening, the opening hasn't done its job.

The top posts in this set use eight different openings. No single opening move predicts performance. What the top posts share is that the opening gets to the reader's own problem fast and makes a promise the body keeps.

## O1. Put the reader in the room

LinkedIn: 4.

**Job.** "What's happening?"

**Mechanism.** A specific lived moment (a date, a place, a line someone said) puts the reader inside it before they've decided whether to care. On Substack the story can run longer than on LinkedIn, so the turn can be bigger.

**What it needs.** A real moment you lived, with exact details. A turn that leads to the essay's idea. A bridge from your story to the reader's life.

**Shape.** Time and place. A small action or a line of dialogue. The detail that doesn't fit. Then the story, then the bridge.

**How it fails.**
- The story runs so long the reader forgets why they opened (JW07: the first half is slow before the question appears).
- The title promised a benefit and the story delays it to the last 250 words (KD09).
- Setup instead of the moment ("Last week I was thinking about...").

**Good for.** Being seen as a person. Strongest when the title is a plain command or a riddle, since the story supplies what the title held back.

**Note on frequency.** This is the second most common opening in the set (9 of 57, after O9) because personal newsletters lean on it. If the person's voice guide says to lead with the idea and open with a story only now and then, follow it; used every week, this opening becomes a template.

**Examples.**
- JW01 (2.2x): "A few years ago, a company asked me to come speak at their annual conference. I wanted to quote $25K plus travel expenses, which felt like great money, and I was pretty sure they'd say yes at that price."
- KD01: ""My book is gone." It's January 2026. I've just finished a horrible phone call with Apple after being robbed in Buenos Aires."
- KD09: "It's September 1, 2021, and I'm standing in an alleyway next to the dental practice, trying to suck air into lungs that feel like they have collapsed."
- KG05: "I was 12 years old, falling face-first toward a kitchen knife."
- KD04: "When I went to Paraguay, a man called Harry changed my life."
- KD08 (variant, success with a hidden problem): "In July 2025, I was in Barcelona, journaling in a cafe nestled in a cobbled side street. I should've been ecstatic."

## O2. Then and now

LinkedIn: 5.

**Job.** "How did it get from there to here?"

**Mechanism.** Two points in time, far apart. The gap is the promise. On Substack it often opens an essay about a change in the world, not just in the writer.

**What it needs.** A real before and after, both specific. Numbers when you have them.

**Shape.** Then: a number, a state, a year. Now: the opposite. Then the cause, which is the essay.

**How it fails.** The "then" is dramatized. The gap is explained in the next line, so there's nothing left to read for.

**Good for.** Trust and reach.

**Examples.**
- AS10: "In 1996, there were around 80,000 Ferraris on the road. In 2026, there are more than 320,000."
- DK02 (5.6x): "When I was young, I was always drawn to people who sounded intelligent. People like Alan Watts, Jordan Peterson, Daniel Schmachtenberger, or other individuals who could explain deep ideas in an exciting yet palatable way."
- JW03: "Ever since I got my first taste of business success back in 2009, I had a very simple way of deciding what I wanted. More."
- AS02: "Two years ago, if I'm honest, nobody gave a shit about strategy."

## O3. The admission

LinkedIn: 15.

**Job.** "They're admitting that?"

**Mechanism.** The writer lowers his own status on purpose, which buys trust. On a teaching newsletter the strongest version is the teacher admitting he has the student's problem (KD02) or doubts his own best-known idea (AS07).

**What it needs.** Something true and a little uncomfortable. A reason it matters to the reader.

**Shape.** The admission, flat. Then why you're telling them.

**How it fails.** A humblebrag. Confession with no point.

**Good for.** Trust. It makes the conclusion of the essay more believable, because the writer has shown he'll say things against himself.

**Examples.**
- AS07: "For my entire career, I've been trying to persuade businesses that quality is dead. You can't win by being "better". Everything's good now." (the essay then argues against it)
- KD02: "A confession: Recently, I've been terrible at following my own advice."
- JW06: "At some point in the last few years, I got really good at my job. I don't say that to brag."
- DK06: "As corny as this may sound, I've always wanted to become a force to be reckoned with."

## O4. The reader, called out

**Job.** "Wait, is he talking about me?"

**Mechanism.** The opening makes a claim or a prediction about the reader, often an unflattering one. The reader keeps reading to check whether it's true of them, or to find out what to do about it. It continues the title's T9 move into the body.

**What it needs.** An accurate read of your reader's behavior, ideally backed by a number or a moment they'll recognize. A way out, which is the essay.

**Shape.** "You're probably going to [fail at X]." / "If you, like me, are [X], you need to read this." / "Everyone is [doing X]. Most will fail, because [Y]." Often followed by a short admission that the writer did the same.

**How it fails.** The prediction is insulting and the writer doesn't share it. The claim is about "most people" and never lands on the reader.

**Good for.** Reach inside a defined audience. Two of the top eight posts open this way (DK01, AS01).

**Examples.**
- DK01 (3.1x): "You're probably going to quit your new years resolution. And that's okay. Most people do (studies show 80-90% failure rates)..."
- AS01 (3.05x): "If you, like me, are that most shameful of beasts, "the online content creator", then you need to read this."
- DK09: "Especially if you consider yourself a smart person, because you are the most likely to think like an idiot."
- DK04: "Everyone is racing to be successful right now. As you'd guess, 99.99% of them will not, in fact, become successful."
- JW04: "I think one of the silliest things I see on the internet is when people get angry about successful entrepreneurs promoting one another."

## O5. The belief you were taught

LinkedIn: 6, as an opening.

**Job.** "I believed that. Is it wrong?"

**Mechanism.** The opening states a belief the reader was raised on, in the voice of whoever taught it (society, the internet, school). The reader recognizes their own script and wants to see it questioned.

**What it needs.** A belief your readers actually hold, stated the way they'd hear it. A counter-case or argument ready in the next section.

**Shape.** "[Society / the internet / everyone] made you think [belief]." The script in its own words. Then the turn.

**How it fails.** A belief nobody holds. A turn with no real counter-case.

**Good for.** Point of view.

**Examples.**
- DK03 (3.9x): "Society made you think that having multiple interests was a weakness. Go to school. Get a degree. Get a job. Retire at some point."
- KG07: "What comes to your mind when you hear the word talent? Probably the image of your favourite sports personality, musician, artist..."
- KG09: "There are a few things that the internet has no shortage of among them are people telling you how to get rich."

## O6. Two words that don't belong together

**Job.** "How do those fit?"

**Mechanism.** The opening names two ideas, defines them as opposites in a few short lines, then hints they belong together. It's the paradox title (T6) carried into the body. Fast and quotable.

**What it needs.** A real pair of ideas your readers see as opposed. A resolution.

**Shape.** "[Word] and [word]." One line on each. "Or so we are told." Then the join.

**How it fails.** It becomes a template (Naval's Archive uses it so often after 2025 that the posts start to sound alike). The resolution is "balance".

**Good for.** Reach. NA01 (19.6x) opens this way.

**Examples.**
- NA01 (19.6x): "Discipline and spontaneity. Two words that don't usually belong in the same sentence."
- NA06: "Luck and effort. One is random. One is earned. Or so we are told."
- NA09: "Intelligence and peace. We assume the first buys the second."

## O7. Someone else's line first

LinkedIn: 9.

**Job.** "I know that line" or "Who said that?"

**Mechanism.** A quote, a film scene or a line from the news comes before any of the writer's words. It borrows attention and authority, and gives the essay something to explain.

**What it needs.** A real quote, credited. A reading of it the reader hasn't had.

**Shape.** The quote, then the writer's first line. Variants: a screenplay excerpt (KG04), a public figure's line at a news moment (KG08).

**How it fails.** The essay restates the quote. An admin opening around the quote ("Dear readers, Today, we'd like to share my thoughts...", NA02).

**Good for.** Reach, through the source's reach.

**Examples.**
- KG08: ""That's what I'm f---ing talking about." Alysa Liu had completed a crazy ice figure skating performance at the 2026 Winter Olympics..."
- KG04: "BOY: The Thessalonian you're fighting, he's the biggest man I've ever seen. [...] ACHILLES: That's why no one will remember your name."
- NA10: ""Escape competition through authenticity." (credited to Naval Ravikant) Competition is what happens to interchangeable people."
- NA08: opens with Naval's full status-games tweet, credited.

## O8. The thing everyone just saw

LinkedIn: 10 (news plus a take).

**Job.** "I saw that too. What did you notice?"

**Mechanism.** The opening starts from a moment the reader just lived through (Spotify Wrapped, a big IPO). Shared attention does half the work. The writer then notices something the reader didn't.

**What it needs.** A recent, widely seen event. A specific thing in it that leads to your idea, ideally a number.

**Shape.** The event. "But that's not what caught my attention." The number or detail. Then the idea.

**How it fails.** Reporting the event without the noticing. A paywall right at the turn (KG06).

**Good for.** Reach. KG01 (71.4x) opens this way.

**Examples.**
- KG01 (71.4x): "The last few years, a new December tradition has emerged: Spotify Wrapped. [...] But that's not what caught my attention this year."
- KG06: "Unless you are living under a rock, you have heard about the SpaceX IPO."

## O9. The idea, stated flat

**Job.** "Is that true?"

**Mechanism.** The first line is the essay's idea, stated as a claim, a law or a belief. No warm-up. The reader is asked to agree or disagree in the first sentence, and the rest of the essay earns it. It respects the reader's time and shows the writer has a point.

**What it needs.** A clear idea you can state in one sentence, and the confidence to put it first. Something concrete close behind it (a number, an example, the reader's problem), or the opening reads as abstract.

**Shape.** The claim. One or two lines that sharpen it. Then the reader's problem or an example.

**How it fails.**
- The claim is generic ("In our rapidly-evolving digital age...", NA04, 0.4x).
- The claim starts at the scale of the universe and takes too long to reach the reader (KG10, 0.4x).
- A big claim with nothing concrete for a paragraph.

**Good for.** Point of view. Fits essays built on one idea.

**Note.** When the person's voice guide says to lead with the idea, this is the default opening, as long as the reader's problem or a real example follows fast. It's the most common opening in the set (11 of 57) and appears in posts that do well (KG03 6.5x, NA03 2.2x) and badly (NA04, KG10), so the difference is in what follows the first line.

**Examples.**
- KG03 (6.5x): "The difference between a blank page and a well-written essay is about 4 hours."
- NA03 (2.2x): "Life abhors a vacuum. If you don't decide what matters, the world will decide for you..."
- JW02: "Some of the most important decisions of your life are made before you even know who you are as a person."
- AS06: "Over time, I've become a believer in this idea: Conversations are destiny."
- AS08: "In some ways, I believe the age of the "content creator" is over."
- DK08: "I'm begging you to write more essays." (the plea version)

## O10. The reading contract

**Job.** "What is this, and is it for me?"

**Mechanism.** The opening says what the piece is and isn't, who it's for, and sometimes how to read it. It resets expectations before the reader can be disappointed, and it filters out readers who wanted something else. On long pieces it makes the length feel intentional.

**What it needs.** A piece that is different from what readers might expect (not a course, not a practical business book). Honesty about what it won't do.

**Shape.** "This is not [what you might expect]." What it is instead. Who it's for. Optionally how to read it ("bookmark it", "read it twice").

**How it fails.** Throat-clearing that delays the idea. A disclaimer that sounds defensive.

**Good for.** Trust, and keeping the right readers through a long piece.

**Examples.**
- KG02 (6.6x): "This is not a course. I want to make that clear right now. If you opened this expecting a magic checklist, an automated posting schedule, or a trick to hack the Substack algorithm, you are going to be disappointed."
- DK07: "This is not a practical business book. At least not by what most people deem "practical.""
- Also used as a short add-on after other openings (DK01, DK04: reading instructions).

## O11. The reader's symptoms

**Job.** "That's me."

**Mechanism.** A run of small, specific signs the reader recognizes in their own day. Each one is a little "yes", and by the third the reader feels described.

**What it needs.** Real, physical, specific signs your readers have. Not feelings in general.

**Shape.** "This is for those times when..." or "When you [sign]. When you [sign]." Then the diagnosis.

**How it fails.** The signs are generic ("when you feel stuck"). It turns into an imagined scene about the reader that isn't true of them.

**Note.** Symptoms the reader actually has are fine. An invented scene ("imagine you're a founder 18 months in...") is a made-up hypothetical; if the person's voice guide rules out made-up examples, it fails. NA05's opening, which describes an ideal version of the reader, sits on the line: it describes a version of the reader everyone recognizes, which is why it works.

**Good for.** Trust. The reader feels understood.

**Examples.**
- DK10: "This is for those times where life starts to lose its wonder and zest. When you feel more tired than you do calm. When you may not realize you're using coffee as a security blanket."
- NA05: "There is a version of you that is perfectly ethical. That version donates to charity, never gossips, always keeps its word..."

## O12. Answer first

**Job.** "Just tell me."

**Mechanism.** The title asks a question and the first lines answer it. Everything after is detail. It rewards the reader for opening and earns trust for the rest.

**What it needs.** A one-sentence answer that's true and a little surprising.

**Shape.** The title's question restated. The answer. Then "here's what that means in practice".

**How it fails.** The answer is so complete the reader stops.

**Examples.**
- DK05: "How do you build a one-person business in 2026? Well, you do everything you would have done before, but you use AI to do it faster, with higher quality, and with less guesswork."

Thin card.

## O13. Proof first

LinkedIn: 1, as an opening.

**Job.** "Why should I believe you?"

**Mechanism.** The opening puts the credential or the decision up front: rare access, a result, a choice the writer made as the person with power.

**What it needs.** A real result or decision, specific.

**Shape.** The access or the decision. The result. Then what the reader will get from it.

**Examples.**
- AS09: "I don't often share details of my projects. After all, most clients don't necessarily want to share this kind of information."
- KD10: "One way I grow my newsletter is through swapping promotions. Recently I spoke to 3 creators with similarly sized audiences." (he turned all three down)

## O14. The writer's vantage point

**Job.** "Why did you notice this?"

**Mechanism.** The writer opens from a part of his own life (being a parent, a daily tea habit) that explains how he saw the pattern. It makes an abstract idea feel lived and gives the reader a small, human image.

**What it needs.** A real, ordinary part of your life that genuinely led to the idea. A small image that can come back at the end.

**Shape.** "When you [have kids / do X], you start to notice..." Then the pattern.

**How it fails.** The vantage point is decoration and has nothing to do with the idea.

**Examples.**
- AS03: "When you have kids, you become keenly attuned to macro changes in culture."
- KD05: "A key part of my writing routine is gulping down an inappropriate amount of English tea (Yorkshire, to be specific)." (the tea returns at the end)

## O15. The occasion

**Job.** "Why this, now?"

**Mechanism.** A reason the essay exists today: an anniversary, readers who replied to the last email, a number of people who asked. It makes the essay feel like part of an ongoing conversation, which is what a newsletter is.

**What it needs.** A real occasion. A reader request is strongest when you can count it.

**Shape.** "Last month, I [milestone]." / "Last month, I shared [X]. A few people replied..." Then what you learned or what you want to clear up.

**How it fails.** The occasion matters to the writer and not the reader.

**Examples.**
- KD07: "Last month, I shared an email explaining why you should not write with AI. A few people replied, saying that I was anti-AI."
- KD03: "Last month, I crossed my 6th year as a writer."
- KG02 (as a secondary move, in the subtitle): "For the 43 people who DM'd me asking how Substack actually works."

## Notes from the first sort

**The title does more work on Substack than the hook does on LinkedIn.** The two biggest outliers (KG01 at 71.4x, NA01 at 19.6x) are carried mostly by their titles: a named reason behind a problem everyone has, and a paradox that works as a quote by itself. NA01's body is short and simple. A strong body under a weak title doesn't get the chance to be read.

**Pairs that work.** From the set:
- A paradox, riddle or idiom title (T6, T11) needs a subtitle that tells the story (U5) or explains it (U1). Justin Welsh does this every time.
- A coined-term title (T7) needs a subtitle that translates it (U1). Without one it's the weakest title in the set.
- A how-to (T2, T3) pairs with a subtitle that makes the payoff concrete (U2) or names the reader (U3).
- A reader-named title (T9) pairs with a called-out opening (O4) and a diagnosis structure (see body moves).
- A plain command (T5) pairs with a story opening (O1). The story supplies the credibility the command lacks.

**Paid previews that stop before the payoff are the weakest posts in every newsletter that uses them.** Nine of the ten paid previews that stop before the payoff sit under 1x (NA02, at 1.1x, is the only one above). KG02 (6.6x) shows the other side: its free part runs 1,200 words of usable rules before the paywall. See the furniture section in `body-moves.md`.

**Things that look like moves but aren't.** "Dear readers", "Let's dive in", "In this essay I will...", "Let's dissect why", standing taglines, "Hope that helps". These are connectors and furniture. A promise line is useful; an announcement that a promise is coming is not.

**Two open questions.**
1. Whether T12 (the reader's own question), T13 (what happened to me) and T15 (the whole thing) stay as their own cards. Each has one example here. They're kept because the job is distinct, and a larger Substack library should confirm or merge them.
2. Whether T3 (think like the person you want to be) belongs inside T2. Both are how-tos. They're kept apart because T3 sells an identity and T2 sells a result, and the bodies differ: T3 essays explain a way of thinking, T2 essays hand over a method.
