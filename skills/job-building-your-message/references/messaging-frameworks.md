# Frameworks behind the messaging interview

Sorting and teaching tools for the interviewer. By default they stay behind the curtain. When someone is stuck, use the examples here the way "When they have no idea" in `skills/job-personal-monopoly/references/interview-moves.md` describes: teach with two or three and ask which sounds closest. Never read out a bare list of options.

The examples below show a shape. They aren't anyone's real case, and none of them goes into a piece. The person's own moments, enemy and stakes come only from the four sources in `CLAUDE.md` rule 1.

1. The three levels of a personal brand
2. The message across time
3. Authority moments
4. Choosing the enemy
5. Stakes and investment proof
6. Vision

---

## 1. The three levels of a personal brand

- **About you.** Your achievements: "I've worked with 50 companies and won three awards." Nobody cares, because there's nothing in it for the reader.
- **About their problem.** "I help SaaS companies fix churn." It works, and it sounds like every competent competitor, because solving problems is the price of entry, not a message.
- **About a change you make together.** "Too many good products die because founders treat retention as a dashboard number instead of a design problem, and we're changing that." This is the level where people take the message as their own and repeat it in rooms the author isn't in.

The session pushes toward the third. When a draft line slips back to the first or second, name it and lift it. On the strategy's character sheet these are the purpose levels: egocentric, customer-centric and purpose-centric.

## 2. The message across time

A complete message covers the three tenses (then, now, future). Their one definition, and what goes wrong when a tense is missing, is in `skills/craft-three-tenses`. Here is only where this session's stages sit against them:

- **Then:** stage 1, the authority moments, failures included.
- **Now:** the current value and method, which mostly arrives from the positioning and doesn't need rebuilding.
- **Future:** stage 4, the vision, set against the enemy (stage 2) and the stakes (stage 3), the way `craft-three-tenses` sets the tenses against a cause.

When a message feels flat, check it against `craft-three-tenses` for the missing tense. `/manifesto` builds the full public version of the three tenses once the thesis is confirmed.

## 3. Authority moments

The shape: **when <situation>, I <action> and <result>, which taught me <lesson>.**

Each part does a different job. The situation makes it real, the action makes it theirs, the result makes it credible, and the lesson makes it transferable, which is what turns a war story into a message.

The shape across backgrounds, for teaching:

- *Corporate:* "When our Q4 revenue came in 30% under target, I went through every deal in the pipeline and found we were selling to the wrong buyer. We changed the whole approach and hit 110% the next quarter, and it taught me that most revenue problems are targeting problems in disguise."
- *Startup:* "When our first launch flopped and we had twelve customers after three months, I called every person who'd signed up and cancelled. We'd been treating a symptom, and that month of calls led to the pivot that raised our Series A. It taught me to question the problem before polishing the solution."
- *Consulting:* "When a large client fired me after two weeks, I realised I'd been giving them what I thought they needed instead of what they asked for. That failure is why I now start every engagement with stakeholder interviews, and the habit became my method."
- *Career change:* "When I moved from engineering into product, I tried to run product decisions with engineering rigour and the team pushed back hard. It taught me that product work is influence without authority, and that changed how I lead."

Three of the four are failures. That ratio is about right, because failures with a lesson build more trust than wins, and they're the stories other people retell.

In the canon, a moment is a story (`canon/bank/stories.md`), its result is proof (`proof.md`), and its lesson, when it's a claim about how the field works, is a POV whose ground is the story.

## 4. Choosing the enemy

The enemy is a practice, a belief or a system. Never a person and never a company: fighting a belief makes you principled and fighting a person makes you bitter, and a named person can answer back while a broken practice can't. It's the same enemy the strategy's character sheet asks for, and it's usually what one of the person's POVs rejects.

When several candidates are on the table, choose the one that does best on four questions:

1. Which one do they have the most direct experience with?
2. Which one hurts the people in their segment, specifically?
3. Which one can they prove a better alternative to, with their own work?
4. Which one do they have real heat about?

The enemy has to be specific. "Bad marketing" isn't an enemy, because nobody defends bad marketing. A good enemy is something reasonable people believe or do today: "the belief that digital-first food companies can scale without understanding physical operations" works because smart people hold it, it causes real damage, and the person has watched it happen.

What makes an enemy land is its cost: what it takes from people (money, time, stress), how it makes them feel, what they miss because of it, and why they can't escape it alone. One founder who burned two million on delivery logistics says more than "startups waste millions".

## 5. Stakes and investment proof

Stakes answer why this is their fight. Investment proof answers what it has cost them, and it's the difference between a mission and a marketing angle.

Investment proof is always an event: a job left, money spent, a safer path declined, an unpopular position taken in public, years committed. "I watched three food-tech startups fail because they treated restaurants like software companies, and I left my restaurant job to stop founders making those mistakes" carries proof. "I'm passionate about operational excellence" carries nothing.

In the canon, this is a value's `cost`: a choice that cost them something real and that they'd make again (`skills/setup-brand-tree/interviews/values.md`).

The honesty rule: not everyone has a dramatic origin story, and manufacturing one is worse than having none, because an inflated story collapses under one follow-up question. If the true stake is that they found the work fascinating and have spent eight years getting good at it, the eight years are the investment proof, and the framework says so.

## 6. Vision

The vision describes the situation after the enemy loses. Its job is to give people something to join rather than something to buy.

Two rules keep it honest:

- **A condition, not a conquest.** "An industry where food innovation is backed by operational reality" describes a state of affairs; "transforming the industry" describes the author's ambition.
- **Sized to the person.** A solo consultant isn't ending an industry's dysfunction. The believable version is what changes for the people who take up their approach, and the client it already worked for is the evidence.

The vision usually writes itself as the mirror of the enemy's cost: whatever the broken practice takes from people, the vision is that cost gone.
