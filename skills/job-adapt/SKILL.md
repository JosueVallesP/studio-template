# /adapt

Method by Josué Valles, the studio's author.

Chopping an essay into five posts, or pasting its quotes onto cards, produces pieces that only make sense to someone who read the original. That's where repurposed content gets worse. Adapting is clipping, the way a short is cut from a long interview: find the ideas inside a finished piece that can stand on their own, then translate each one for the new reader. It's `/shape`'s two jobs (find the clip, translate it) with a finished piece as the raw material instead of a transcript.

| Stays the same | Changes |
| --- | --- |
| The idea | The context the reader needs, because the new reader didn't see the original |
| The person's facts, and their words where they're strong (`CLAUDE.md` rule 1) | The frame and the opening, because a different channel meets the reader at a different point |
| The stance | The form, because the idea's job and the channel decide it |
| The ground behind the claim | The length and the level of detail |

## One idea per piece

Going long to short, the commonest mistake is shrinking: keeping every conclusion of the essay and cutting the steps that joined them. An essay has room to set up a test, define each group, give the reason and answer the objection. A post that keeps all of those conclusions in a fifth of the words keeps none of the steps, and reads as a string of claims the reader has to take on trust.

So a post carries one idea from the source, and carries it whole:

- **Pick, don't compress.** Each clip is one idea you can say in one sentence with no "and". The post gives that idea every step it needs to make sense to a stranger: the setup, the reason, the example that proves it. Everything else in the source stays there, or becomes its own clip.
- **The rest can wait in the source.** The post doesn't need the other ideas to be complete. When the person wants it, the post can point to the longer piece as an open door at the end ("I wrote the long version here"). That pointer is an offer, never the place the missing context lives (see "Never").
- **The check.** If the post's idea needs an "and" to state, or it uses a term, a group or a test it never set up, it's shrinking. Cut back to one idea and give it the room.

## How it runs

1. **Find the clips.** Read the source piece whole. Pull out every idea that could stand alone, each as one sentence with no "and" ("One idea per piece" above). Not every section qualifies; an essay may hold one good post or six. No fixed number (`skills/job-shape`).
2. **Run each clip through the chain** (complete, legible, transferable, unexpected, useful). The commonest failure in adaptation is completeness: the clip leans on something explained earlier in the original. The fix is to bring that context into the clip, never to point back to the original.
3. **Translate for the new reader.** The channel's reader: someone scrolling a feed has seconds and arrives cold; a newsletter reader has chosen to read. The opening meets each where they are: an outcome or a problem the reader already feels. The form: what the idea gives the reader points to the form, as in Shape; the format skill's moves stage picks the move. No template feel: clips from one source don't share one structure or one opening move; each follows its own idea.
4. **The fidelity check.** The adapted piece says what the original meant. Facts and stance don't move; the writer's reasoning, framing and analogies are `writer` lines in the source map as usual.
5. **Draft, judge, refine** like any piece, through the format skill's chain. Most adapted pieces are `kind: test`.

## Rules from the author's standards

- **Every piece stands on its own.** No "as I wrote last week", no opening mid-thought. A reader who never saw the original gets the whole point.
- **Visuals stand on their own too.** A visual made from an essay is fully legible without the essay; a card and its caption each make sense without the other.
- **Nothing new about the person.** Adapting adds reasoning, framing and examples. It never adds a fact, story or belief that isn't in the source or the canon.
- **Not every piece should be adapted.** Adapt clips that meet the bar in `craft-refine`: relevant to the person, on strategy, legible, valuable. A clip that's only filler for the calendar stays in the source.

## Body of work

Adapted pieces are branches of one idea. Each records its source piece (`adapted_from:` in the brief), so the body-of-work index shows how ideas spread across channels and which cornerstone pieces feed the most. A post doesn't have to link back to the essay, but the studio always knows the line between them.

## Directions

- Long to short: an essay, talk, podcast or video into posts and visuals. One idea per post, carried whole.
- Short to long: several posts on one idea into an essay, where the work is finding the thread and the structure, not stitching posts together.
- Across channels at the same length: a LinkedIn post into a newsletter section, an essay into a talk outline.
- Into visuals: the clip's idea goes to `/visual`, which finds its shape.

## Never

- Never chop by section. Clip by idea.
- Never shrink a whole piece into a shorter one. A short piece carries one of its ideas.
- Never point back to the original to supply missing context.
- Never add a fact about the person the source and the canon don't hold.
- Never give every clip the same opening move.
