# Refine

Method by Josué Valles, the studio's author.

## What great means

A piece is great when it is:

1. **Relevant to the person.** It carries their thinking: it traces to their material, their POVs or their stories, and it passes the swap test (another name couldn't go on it as it stands).
2. **On strategy.** It serves what they're building toward: their thesis, a branch of their body of work, the reader in their strategy.
3. **Fully legible.** The reader gets the point without having to guess anything: no missing context, no undefined term, no leap they take on faith.
4. **Genuinely valuable.** The reader can see something differently or do something in their own world afterwards (the last step of `/shape`'s chain).

That's the bar. Everything else (the art, the examples, the analogies, a fresh way of saying an old idea) is a lever for reaching it, not part of the bar.

A piece that fails 1 or 2 stops here. No amount of polish fixes a piece that isn't theirs or doesn't serve where they're going; say so and stop, and send it back to `/shape` or the strategist.

## Two cautions that shape everything below

- **Don't over-optimize.** On social media, overthinking means testing less, and testing is how a person learns what lands. A post polished for a week teaches less than three posts shipped.
- **Great can't be fully known in isolation.** Before publishing, a piece can only be held against the best of its kind, the person's own best work, and the reader it's for. After publishing, the response says more. So Refine gets a piece to the best it can be now, and the librarian closes the loop afterwards.

## The bar depends on the piece

| | Test piece | Cornerstone piece |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which | Most social posts; anything on a weekly cadence | The manifesto, a key essay, a landing page, a talk, an about page, a piece the person names as one |
| Goal | Good enough to learn from | The best it can be before it goes out |
| Rounds | One constraint fixed, then the judge | A second round after the judge, plus the comparison |
| What finishes it | The audience's response | The person's final read |

The brief carries `kind: test` or `kind: cornerstone`. The strategist or the job command sets it from the format and the channel (an essay for a body-of-work brand leans cornerstone; a LinkedIn post leans test); the person can change it with a word.

## The one constraint

Don't polish everything. Find the single weakest lever, deepest first, so lines are never polished on top of a weak idea:

1. **The idea.** Is there one clear idea, and is it sound? (`craft-copylosophy`'s judge pass.)
2. **Legibility.** Where would a reader have to guess? An undefined term, missing context, a leap, or a shape that doesn't fit the idea: a question the reader would ask left unanswered, a part that answers nothing they'd ask, a list where the idea needed an argument.
3. **Concreteness.** Where is it abstract when it could name the thing?
4. **Examples.** Is there a real case that lets the reader see it, where one is needed?
5. **Analogy.** Would a comparison make it click, and does the relation carry over?
6. **The frame.** Is an old idea said in a way that breaks the reader's pattern, or is it a platitude said well?
7. **The craft.** Rhythm, cuts, the human patterns (`craft-human`), the house rules (`craft-house-rules`).

The deepest lever that fails is the one constraint. Fix that. Then compare: hold the piece against the two or three best examples of the same move in the format's library and against the person's own best pieces (`material/`, the right side of `bank/rewrites/`). Then cut what isn't working. Then the judge.

## How the roles use it

- **The editor and the judge** report THE ONE THING: the deepest failing lever, with the quoted line and the fix, before the ranked issues. The ranked issues stay in the review for the record; the writer works from the one thing. Script fails (the house rules, the mechanical checks) are fixed regardless; they're not the one thing, they're hygiene.
- **The writer**, on revise, fixes the one thing in a fresh pass of the paragraphs it touches, plus every fail the review grades, and keeps what passed. It doesn't also take the three "should" lines on a test piece; it may on a cornerstone.
- **Test piece:** revise and judge again up to the revision cap in `skills/role-writer` (one), then the person decides. If it still fails, the person chooses: ship as is, fix the lines themselves, or one more pass.
- **Cornerstone piece:** after the judge passes it, one more round from step 1 of the levers, with the comparison against the library's best written out in the review, then the person's final read.
- **The librarian** keeps the person's edits (`bank/rewrites/`) and, when the same edit repeats, changes the cause (a voice rule, a line in a skill) instead of fixing the symptom each time. After publishing, what the audience did with the piece feeds back into what "great" means for this person.

## What it says to the person

One constraint and its fix, not a list. For a test piece: "The idea's solid. The one thing holding it back is X; here's the fix." Then the verdict. For a cornerstone: the same, plus what the comparison showed in two or three lines.

## Never

- Never list twelve notes where one constraint would do.
- Never polish a line on a piece that fails relevance or strategy.
- Never run a third round on a test piece without the person asking.
- Never grade the levers as a score. The verdict ladder is pass, revise, redo (`role-editor`).
