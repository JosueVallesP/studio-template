# /articulation-ladder

Method by Josué Valles, the studio's author, adapted from a technique a coach ran live with members of a coaching community in 2023. The cases below are real moments from those calls; the people are described by what they do. Use them to show the move, never as templates, and never in a piece (they aren't checkable sources).

People who know their work well can usually say it at one level: a middle register that sounds fine to them and lands with a slice of their market. Ask them to go deeper and they skip steps because those steps feel obvious. Ask them to go simpler and they reach for a headline that sounds like everyone else's. This skill runs an interview that fixes both, then hands back a ladder they can use for any audience.

## Why it matters

- **One register reaches one slice of the market.** A former world champion ballroom dancer who coaches burned-out business owners could only talk about her work in the middle range. The coach's point: put that in an ad and it only lands with the middle of the market, so it costs more to reach anyone.
- **Rushing to a headline boxes you in.** On the calls: rush to a headline and "you're boxed into a certain way of saying what you do, and it boxes you into the price" and the fulfillment. The short version has to come last, after the long one exists.
- **The obvious is invisible to the person who knows it.** "The thing stopping them from teaching it is their expertise." They move fast because they know it, and the steps they skip are the ones a buyer needs to hear.
- **The buyer's words beat yours.** "You have to use their vocabulary, not yours." If your buyers say "aspire," you can't say "want."
- **Articulate the problem, not only the solution.** As one member put it: "It's not actually about your articulation of your solution, it's about your articulation of their problem and how they experience it."

## Where it sits

- `job-understand` researches the reader's words at scale (comments, calls, forums) into `canon/strategy/audience.md`'s language bank. This skill uses the language bank in step 4 when it exists, and works on one offer at a time.
- `setup-brand-tree`'s vehicle layer holds the offers (`canon/vehicle/vehicle.md`). When an offer is on file, start from how it's described there.
- `craft-copylosophy` tests whether a claim holds; this skill finds how to say it at every level. When a rung makes a claim about results, run copylosophy's short version on it before it goes on a page.
- It writes no posts or pages. A rung is a line the person can use; `/post`, `/essay` or a page draft carries it further.
- A rung meant for public selling copy (an ad line, a sales-page line, a profile headline) is a starting line here, not finished copy. Before it goes out, it runs through `skills/craft-sales-copy` as its own piece (copy kind `ad line` or `sales page section`), with that standard's three drafts and its judge.

## What it reads

Before the first question, read what the studio already holds, so you don't ask for it: `canon/vehicle/vehicle.md` (the offers), `canon/strategy/audience.md` (the reader and the language bank), `canon/core-summary.md`, `bank/questions.md` (questions readers asked, in their words), `material/` (sales pages, call transcripts, proposals), and anything in `inbox/` the person points to. Facts about the person, their clients and their results come only from these and from what they say today (`CLAUDE.md` rule 1).

## How to run it

Before step 1, say in two or three sentences what's about to happen, why, what they'll have and how long it takes (`CLAUDE.md` rule 7): about twenty minutes of questions, one at a time, about one offer or method; at the end, a ladder of ways to say it from the most detailed to one line, with each rung matched to where they'd use it.

Ask one question at a time. Wait for the answer. Push back when an answer drifts into abstractions. The person does the talking; your job is to slow them down and write down what they say. The questions follow `skills/craft-interview`, and they ask about real work: the last client, the last call, the last time it went wrong.

### 1. Set up

Find out three things before anything else, skipping whatever the files already answer:

- What are we articulating? An offer, a method, an idea, a body of work.
- Who is it for, as they'd describe the person?
- Where will they use it? Sales calls, a sales page, posts, ads, introductions, a pitch to their own team.

Ask whether they have real buyer language on hand: sales-call transcripts, DMs, emails, reviews, intake forms. If they do, ask them to share it now (paste it, or drop it in `inbox/`). You'll need it in step 4.

### 2. Speak the obvious

Ask them to list what someone needs, step by step, to get the result they help with. Anchor it in a real case: "Take the last client you got there. What did they have to do first?" Tell them it should sound so boring they feel a bit silly saying it.

Case: a man who owns a basketball facility wanted to help trainers go full-time. When pushed, he listed the boring basics: stop one-to-one, move to group training with membership pricing, rent gym space by the hour and then monthly, grow an email list with free clinics, set up a CRM and welcome texts, add leagues and shooting clinics. The coach turned that list into three stages and a price on the spot: "I didn't make anything up. I just extracted what you have."

If they answer with a slogan or a philosophy, ask for the next concrete thing a client actually did.

### 3. Go down: make it complex

Pick one step from the list, ideally the one that carries the most weight. Ask them to explain it the way they would to a peer in their field, or to a student paying full price.

When they simplify for you, stop them. The coach's words to the dancer: "Don't simplify it for me. Make it stupidly complex," the way the most boring professor would. Keep asking what that means, why it happens, and what sits underneath, until you reach a mechanism or a root cause they can name precisely.

Case: the dancer's six-step method had a step called "establish control." Drilling into it went to "external situations and people," then to "at 5 to 10 years old we are conditioned by parents, school and friends to weigh external situations." That bottom rung was the most specific thing she knew, and none of her marketing said it.

You can repeat this for a second step if the first one runs dry. Don't drill every step; one or two deep rungs are enough to build from.

### 4. Collect the buyer's words

Go through the buyer language they shared, and the language bank in `canon/strategy/audience.md` if it exists, and pull out the exact phrases buyers use for their problem and what they want. Keep repetitions; a phrase that comes up twice matters more. Note where each phrase came from.

Case: in a mock sales call, the coach wrote down only the prospect's words ("affluent parents," "aspire," "national level" twice, "top 1% of players in the country") and repeated them back. The prospect said he "felt extremely heard." His team had once pasted barely edited lines from sales-call transcripts into posts, and people replied that it was what they'd been thinking for the last six months.

If they have no buyer language, don't invent any. Say so, and suggest five or six conversations with people squarely in the market; one member's view on the calls was that five or six of those give as much as a survey of 30 to 50. Give them a few open questions to ask, about events ("Tell me about the last time this got in your way"), and mark any buyer-side rung as a draft to test against those conversations. `/understand` can do the wider research later.

### 5. Climb up: make it simple

Starting from the deepest rung, rewrite it in steps, each one simpler than the last, until you reach a single line and then a question the buyer would answer yes to. Every rung has to keep the meaning of the one below it. Use the buyer's words on the lower, simpler rungs.

Case, the dancer's climb:

- At 5 to 10 years old we are conditioned by parents, school and friends to weigh external situations.
- At a young age we were subconsciously shaped by influential people.
- We either control or react.
- How to not care about things outside your control.
- Is control a problem for you?

Case: a coach who helps people recover from narcissistic relationships ended with a detailed version for people who know the field, a "level one" version ("I help people get out of narcissistic relationships"), a sales-call version in the buyer's own language, and a 15-second ad ("Are you in a narcissistic relationship? Keep listening.").

Write the rungs with the person, not for them. Offer a draft rung, ask whether it's true to what they do, and keep their wording when they correct you (`CLAUDE.md` rule 3). Their coined terms stay as they are on every rung.

### 6. Match each rung to where it gets used

Go back to the contexts from step 1 and pick a rung for each. The rule of thumb from the calls: the more the audience already knows, the deeper the rung you can use. Peers and narrow, informed audiences get the complex language; broad or cold audiences get the simple line or the question.

The top of the ladder is the line for a stranger who asked in passing. Someone who can say their work at every level picks the one that fits the person in front of them, and with a stranger that's usually the shortest one.

### 7. Check the ladder

Before handing it back:

- Does the ladder describe the buyer's problem and how they experience it, as well as the solution?
- Does each rung make sense on its own, to someone who hasn't read the others?
- Is every buyer phrase sourced from real buyer language, or clearly marked as a draft to test?
- Did you add anything they didn't say? If so, flag it or cut it.
- Does a rung promise a result the files don't back? Narrow it, or run copylosophy's short version on it.
- Would they say each line out loud to a friend?

## What it writes

`pieces/<yyyy-mm-dd>-ladder-<slug>/ladder.md`, a folder that holds only that file (with no `brief.md`, `/status` doesn't count it as a piece). A short document:

1. **What we articulated** and who it's for.
2. **The obvious list** from step 2, in their words.
3. **The deep rung or rungs** from step 3.
4. **Buyer vocabulary**, each phrase with where it came from.
5. **The ladder**, from most complex to the one-line question.
6. **Where each rung goes:** context, rung, and a sentence on why.
7. **Open questions:** anything to test with real buyers.

Then, for the person's yes (`CLAUDE.md` rule 6), along the route in `skills/setup-brand-tree/SKILL.md` ("Where a picked-up belief goes"): something new is a `draft` entry in its file, with `quote`, `sources` and a `log` line; a change to a `confirmed` entry is a proposal only.

- Buyer phrases from real sources that aren't in the language bank go into that segment's `language_bank` in `canon/strategy/audience.md`, each with its source and a log line naming this ladder. When the segment's entry is `confirmed`, they go to `proposals.md` instead, as one proposal (`kind: change to G<n>`).
- When the obvious list is a method the person hasn't named in the canon, it becomes a `draft` branch entry in `canon/person/branches.md`, in their words, with the ladder as its source.
- When the offer is in `canon/vehicle/vehicle.md` and the person wants the one-line rung there: on a `draft` offer, it goes into that entry's description with a log line; on a `confirmed` offer, it goes to `proposals.md` as `kind: change to O<n>`.

Say back the ladder and where it went in a few lines, then one next step: use a rung this week in the context it was matched to, and tell the studio how it landed (`/capture`).

## How to behave

- Keep it a conversation. One question per turn, short replies, no lectures.
- Stop early simplifying, and ask for the concrete thing when they go abstract.
- Don't flatter the answers. If a rung is vague or sounds like a marketing course, say so plainly and ask again.
- Never invent facts, results, client stories or buyer quotes.
- Treat the steps as a guide. If someone already has a strong deep version, skip ahead; if they have no buyer language, the ladder can still be built, with that gap marked.
- Every line you write follows `skills/craft-house-rules`, with the person's voice guide on top.
