# The Genius of Contradiction (for visual identities)

Method by Josué Valles, the studio's author.

## In the studio

The method is kept whole here. In the studio it runs inside `setup-visual-identity` (after the interviews and the strategy, from the canon) or inside `job-identity` (`/identity`, from the strategy, the material and a short conversation), and its output becomes part of the canon. This section is the one place that says where the brief is saved; the two skills point here:

- **Steps 1 and 2 come from the files, not from questions.** Inside `job-identity`, the canon may be thin: steps 1 and 2 come from the strategy and the material, and the person fills the gaps in the chat. Inside `setup-visual-identity`, what the brand is, who it's for and where it came from: `canon/core-summary.md`, `canon/vehicle/vehicle.md`, `canon/strategy/brand-strategy.md`. The target association is already decided there, with its anti-associations; use it as written. The supporting associations come from the values (`canon/person/values.md`) and the character sheet. Ask the person only for what the canon doesn't hold (what exists visually today, who it sits next to).
- **Step 3's evidence** comes from the stories and proof banks and the person's origin (the thesis's catalyst). A side with no canon evidence behind it is a costume; drop it.
- **Output** goes to `canon/visual/contradiction-brief.md`, person-owned, with `status: draft` until the person says yes to each part, and `source:` naming the skill that ran it (`setup-visual-identity` or `/identity, <date>`). The design system (`canon/visual/design-system.md`: type, color, texture, composition, mark) is derived from the brief's visual codes and clash points, and `setup-figma` applies it to the person's copy of the kit. The designer reads the brief; it never reruns it per piece.

Facts about the person never live in this file. It names the canon files to read.

## Run on its own (`/genius-of-contradiction`)

The whole method, Steps 1 to 8, ending in the Contradiction Brief. Two uses:

- **The person's own brand,** to find the contradiction before committing to a look. Read what the files hold first (the canon files above, `material/`, the strategy if `/strategy` wrote one) and ask only for the gaps. Every side needs evidence from their files or from what they said today, quoted in "Why it's true". When they ask you to fill steps yourself, a side or code you read from their files without them saying it is marked in the brief as your call, with the lines it came from, so they know what to check.
- **A brand they work on for someone else** (a client, a company they advise): the designer-or-strategist case under "Who uses this". The facts come from what they share and tell you, and nothing about that brand goes in the person's canon or banks.

**Saved to** `pieces/<yyyy-mm-dd>-contradiction-<slug>/contradiction-brief.md`, in the format under "The Contradiction Brief (output)". Never to `canon/visual/`: the person's look changes only through `/identity` or `setup-visual-identity`, and either can start from this brief ("use the contradiction brief from <date>") instead of running the method again.

**Next.** For their own brand, `/identity` makes it their look and applies it to the Figma kit. For any brand, `/visual-philosophy` on the contradiction sentence finds the mark the identity carries.


## The idea

The most interesting people, brands and ideas are walking contradictions. They mix opposites that usually don't go together. Jazz is strict structure and chaos at once. Cats love you and ignore you. Napoleon was a short, harmless-looking man who became the most powerful person of his time. Street art is vandalism that ends up in museums.

The reason these work: when the two sides collide, they create a third thing people feel.

- Jazz: structure + chaos = freedom that takes real skill
- Cats: affection + indifference = love you have to earn
- Napoleon: harmless look + enormous power = you can't judge him by what you see
- Streetwear: sidewalk grit + luxury prices = status that comes from taste, not from being proper

A random mix of styles has no meaning when the two sides meet. A good contradiction does. That meaning is what this skill is for.

**The rule that keeps it from being random:** start with the association the brand wants people to have, then find two opposite sides that are already true of the brand and whose collision produces that association. Work backwards from the feeling to the pair. Never pick a pair because it looks cool and then hunt for a reason.

## Who uses this

Two kinds of people run this skill. Figure out which one you're talking to from the first message, and ask if it isn't clear.

- **The brand owner** (founder, solo operator, person building a personal brand). Run it as a guided interview. They know the truth about the brand; you help them see the tension in it.
- **The designer or strategist** working on someone else's brand. They often arrive with a brief, a website, or notes. Read everything they share first, fill in what you can, and only ask about the gaps.

Either way, the output is the same: a Contradiction Brief (format at the bottom) that a designer can work from.

## How to run it

Ask one question at a time. Wait for the answer. Push back on vague answers by asking for a concrete example. If the person shares a website, deck, logo, photos or past work, read or look at all of it before asking anything it already answers.

If the person asks you to skip the questions and fill the steps yourself, do it. Mark in the brief which parts came from them and which are your calls, so they know what to check.

### Step 1. Get the facts

You need:
- What the brand is and what it sells or does
- Who it's for
- Where it came from (founder background, origin, place, history)
- What it looks like now, if anything exists
- Who it competes with or sits next to

### Step 2. Define the associations

Ask what people should think and feel when they see the brand. Collect three to five associations. Then ask which one matters most. That one is the **target association**.

Push for associations that are specific enough to design for. "Trustworthy" and "innovative" are what every brand says. Ask: trustworthy like a family doctor or like a Swiss bank? Those look very different. Keep asking until the association points somewhere.

### Step 3. Find the tensions that are already true

This is the step that stops a brand from faking it. Look for places where the brand is honestly two opposite things. Good places to look:

- **The founder or people:** where they came from vs. what they do now (a former punk who now runs a law firm)
- **The product:** how it's made vs. what it does (handmade tools for a high-tech job)
- **The category:** what everyone in the space looks like vs. what this brand believes
- **The audience:** who they are vs. who they want to become
- **The place or history:** where the brand is from vs. where it's going
- **The channel:** where the brand lives or gets seen vs. what it's made of (something built to last that only shows up in a fast feed)

Generate three to six candidate pairs. For each one, write down the evidence that both sides are true. If you can't point to evidence for a side, drop the pair. A side the brand only wishes were true is a costume, and people can tell.

If the brand has no real tension yet, say so plainly. Two honest options: build one side into the business first, or use the category tension (looking unlike everyone else in the space for a reason the brand actually believes).

### Step 4. Run the collision test

For each candidate pair, write the sentence: **[Side A] + [Side B] = [what people feel]**.

Keep the pairs where the result lands on or near the target association. Drop the rest, even if they're clever.

Then run the **felt vs. worded test**. Some contradictions only work as wordplay ("less is more, but it's also a lot"). Others you feel the moment you see them (a heavy metal can of water). Ask: would a stranger notice this contradiction in two seconds of looking, without anyone explaining it? If not, it's wordplay. Wordplay can live in the copy. It can't carry a visual identity.

Pick one pair. Only one. Two contradictions at once reads as confusion.

### Step 5. Set the ratio

One side is the **base**. The other is the **accent**. Think roughly 80/20, though the exact number matters less than the fact that one side clearly leads.

At 50/50 the two sides cancel out and you get mush. The surprise only works if there's something stable to surprise against.

The base is usually the side that carries the target association and what the audience needs to trust. That leaves two ways the pair can be shaped, and you need to know which one you have, because the accent does a different job in each:

- **Familiar base, surprising accent.** The base looks like the category, and the accent is the twist that makes it stand out. Supreme is mostly skate shop; the art world and luxury are the twist. Mailchimp is mostly a business tool; the odd illustration is the twist.
- **Surprising base, grounding accent.** The base is already unlike the category, and the accent ties it back to what the brand actually is or where it lives, so it reads as belonging there instead of being lost. Liquid Death is mostly heavy metal; the accent is that it's plainly water, which is what makes the metal funny instead of confusing.

Check which shape fits with the person rather than assuming it.

### Step 6. Translate each side into visual codes

For each side, list how it shows up on each of these levers:

- **Type:** serif or sans, weight, how much space, hand-drawn or set
- **Color:** palette, how saturated, how many colors
- **Texture and material:** clean or rough, digital or printed, paper, metal, fabric
- **Imagery:** photography style, illustration style, what's in the frame
- **Composition:** strict grid or loose, crowded or open, centered or off-balance
- **The mark:** shape language of the logo or symbol
- **Motion** (if relevant): fast or slow, smooth or jumpy

Make each list concrete enough that two designers would make similar choices from it. "Elegant" is not a code. "High-contrast serif, lots of white space, one muted color" is.

### Step 7. Decide where they clash

Assign each side to specific layers. The clash should be visible in one or two places, not spread across everything. Examples of clash points:

- Refined serif type over rough photocopy textures
- A strict grid holding loose, hand-drawn marks
- Luxury materials with a crude, blunt message
- Clinical packaging with poetic writing on it

Everywhere else, the base side runs the show. If every element carries both sides, nothing stands out.

### Step 8. Test it

Run these before you hand anything over:

1. **Truth:** Is each side true of the brand, with evidence?
2. **One sentence:** Can you explain the contradiction in one sentence a friend at a bar would get?
3. **Direction:** Does the collision point to the target association, or away from it?
4. **Removal:** Take the accent away and see what the brand loses. With a familiar base, it should lose what makes it stand out and start to look like everyone else in its category. With a surprising base, it should lose its tie to what it is or where it lives and start to look like it belongs somewhere else (a museum, a different industry). If nothing changes either way, the accent isn't doing anything.
5. **Trust:** Does the accent undercut something the audience needs to believe? (A playful bank is fine if the playfulness never touches the part where your money is safe.)
6. **Felt:** Would a stranger notice it without an explanation?

If a test fails, go back to the step it points to. Don't patch it with copy.

## Real brands to learn from

Use these to explain the method. Don't copy them.

- **Liquid Death:** canned water, about the most wholesome product there is, sold with heavy metal and punk visuals. The base is the metal look; the accent is that it's just water. The collision: choosing the healthy option without the preachy wellness feel. A surprising base with a grounding accent.
- **Supreme:** a New York skate shop whose red box logo borrows from the artist Barbara Kruger, and which went on to collaborate with Louis Vuitton. Skate grit as the base, art world and luxury as the accent. The collision: status you get from taste and access, not money alone. A familiar base with a surprising accent.
- **Aesop:** amber apothecary bottles and plain pharmacy-style labels, in shops designed like galleries, with literature quoted around the brand. Clinical as the base, poetic as the accent. The collision: skincare that feels both serious and cultured.
- **Mailchimp (2018 rebrand):** email software for businesses, shown through loose, odd, hand-drawn illustration and a strong yellow. Business tool as the base, quirky art as the accent. The collision: a serious tool that doesn't take itself too seriously.

## Mistakes to watch for

- **The costume:** claiming a side that isn't true. It looks fine in a deck and falls apart the first time a customer meets the real brand.
- **The mush:** 50/50 ratio. Neither side wins, so nothing is surprising.
- **Contradiction everywhere:** every element mixes both sides. The clash should be a few clear moments.
- **Wordplay:** a pairing that only works when you explain it.
- **Picking the pair first:** choosing opposites because they look interesting and then making up the meaning afterwards. Always go from target association to pair.
- **Too many pairs:** one contradiction per identity.
- **The lost outlier:** a surprising base with no grounding accent. It looks striking but nobody can tell what it is or why it's here.

## The Contradiction Brief (output)

Deliver this as a document the person can keep and hand to a designer. If the session offers a document or design artifact type, use it. If the person wants to see it, offer to mock up a few applications (a logo direction, a social post, a packaging face) after the brief is agreed.

1. **Brand in one paragraph:** what it is, who it's for, where it came from.
2. **Target association:** the main one, plus the supporting ones.
3. **The contradiction:** Side A + Side B = what people feel. One sentence.
4. **Why it's true:** the evidence for each side.
5. **Base and accent:** which side leads, roughly how much, and which of the two shapes it is.
6. **Visual codes:** two columns, one per side, across type, color, texture, imagery, composition, mark and motion.
7. **Clash points:** the one or two places the sides meet, described concretely.
8. **What to avoid:** the specific ways this identity could slide into costume, mush or cliché.
9. **Test results:** the six tests from Step 8, each with a short answer.

## How to write everything in this skill

The house rules (`skills/craft-house-rules`) apply to every question you ask and everything you write for the person, including the brief, and supplied copy follows `CLAUDE.md` rule 3. Two more for this method:

- Name the concrete thing in visual terms. "Rough paper texture" beats "organic feel."
- Give principles and treat specific moves as possibilities. A specific code that suits one brand may be wrong for the next.
