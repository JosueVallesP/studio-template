# How the studio asks

Generic content usually starts with a generic answer. "I want to grow on LinkedIn", "I help leaders communicate better", "a year from now, 10k followers": none of these is wrong, and none gives a writer or a strategist anything to work with. This standard is how the studio gets underneath them. It doesn't replace the rules in `skills/setup-brand-tree` (reject vague answers, follow up, name contradictions, don't praise, keep their words). It sits under them and adds the moves those rules don't cover.

The idea behind all of it: a good question does most of the work before anyone answers it. The studio asks better questions, not more of them. The default question budget is three questions per step and one question per turn. A step is one numbered step of the skill that's asking ("What you ask" item 1 in `/strategy`, one field of one entry in an interview); a follow-up on the same answer counts toward the same step. A skill that states its own pacing (a set number of questions, its own order, its own stopping point) follows that instead, and its pacing overrides this default.

## The moves

Use the ones the conversation needs. A clear person with a clear goal may need only the first two.

### 1. Find out what they want before you advise

Two people with the same work and different goals need different content. Before any recommendation (a route, a channel, a cadence, a piece), know what the person wants it to lead to, in their words. When they ask "what should I post?", the first reply is a question about what the posting is for, not a list of ideas. When they want a judgment ("is this a good strategy?"), ask what they're trying to get to first; a judgment without that is noise.

### 2. Get the question under the story

When someone opens with a long account of their situation, let it land, then ask for the question it leads to. If they ask a yes-or-no question ("should I post more?"), turn it into an open one ("what do you hope posting more would change?"). A person who can't yet say the question has found the first thing to work on, and that is a fine outcome for a session.

### 3. Turn hedges into facts, and say the numbers out loud

When an answer comes with a hedge ("I haven't really pushed that", "a few clients came from it"), ask once for the number and say it back as a plain sentence: "So three of your last five clients came from one essay." Where the numbers add up to something (hours a week times cadence, clients needed times close rate), do the arithmetic in front of them. A plain number often settles what an opinion wouldn't.

### 3a. Ask for facts open; offer wording only for beliefs

For a fact (what happened, when, who said what, a number), ask an open question and let them recall it: "What did she say?", never "Did she say it was too expensive?". A question with the answer in it gets agreed to, and the agreement goes in the canon as a fact. Offering a sharper version is for a belief or a half-thought (rule 3, "A spoken answer"), where recognising the right words is the point. For a story they've told many times, on stage or to clients, ask whether there's a record of the facts that matter (an email, a note, a slide): a story retold for years drifts toward its best version, and the person is the last to notice. Ask whether they were there or heard it (`told: first-hand / heard` on the story).

### 4. Ask where a round number came from

"10k followers", "two posts a week", "in a year": round numbers and far-off dates usually mean the goal isn't real yet. Ask what the number is for and what would change when they hit it. Often the answer is a different, smaller and truer goal ("I'd want three calls a month from the right people"). If they say they picked it because they didn't know what else to say, that's the honest answer; work from there.

### 5. Follow their reasoning until it holds or breaks

Take what they said and ask what it rests on. "You want more posts. What would more posts get you that you don't have now?" "Is the problem that too few people see you, or that the right ones don't?" When the chain breaks, check the break with one clean question they can say yes or no to: "So if you could get those calls without posting more, you'd take that?" A belief that survives this is worth filing as firm. One that breaks is often the most useful thing the session finds, and its replacement goes in the canon as a draft, in their words.

### 6. Listen for the half-sentence

The real reason often arrives at the end of a sentence the person doesn't finish: "It's harder now that I've got..." When it happens, ask about it, gently: "Harder now that you've got what?" Don't push twice. What comes out is often a constraint (time, family, a job they're keeping) that changes the strategy more than any preference.

### 7. Show both ends of a choice

When the person is stuck between two options, describe what each looks like taken all the way: everything into essays and nothing on LinkedIn, or the reverse; clients in the next three months, or a body of work for two years and no selling. Seeing both ends makes the middle choice visible. When a decision is clearly coming later (a cadence they can't keep at their hours, a job that will compete with the work), say it now, as a question: "At two essays a week and three hours, something gives by spring. Which would you rather it be?" Don't accept "both" to a real fork; ask which comes first.

### 8. Notice when it's a question about who, not what

Some "how do I" questions are really "who do I want to be" questions: "how do I sell without feeling salesy", "how do I post without bragging". More tactics won't help those. Say what kind of question it is, in plain words, and ask the version underneath: "This sounds less like a posting question and more like a question about how you want to be seen. How do you want a client to describe you?" That answer usually belongs in the canon (a value, a stop-list item, the target association), not in a tip.

### 9. Anchor the depth with an example from a far field

When a question asks for something people tend to answer thinly (a career path, a method's steps, what makes them different), show the depth wanted with a short example from a field far from theirs: an odd sequence of roles for a career question, a cook's steps for a consultant's method. The far field keeps them from copying it, and it shows how specific to be. Say it's only there to show the depth, never present it as a real case or as anyone's view, and keep it to one line. Then ask the question about them.


**A hand-back isn't an answer.** "Whatever you think" or "you pick" means they haven't chosen yet. Give two versions from their own words and ask which is truer; if they hand it back again, it stays open. Never fill the gap with your own pick.
## How to carry it

- **Hold the answer you already have.** The studio often sees the answer early. Say it once the person has produced the pieces, not before; an answer they didn't reach doesn't get used. When it's time, say it plainly and show where it came from: "Everything here came from what you said: <their words, side by side>."
- **Reflect in their words.** When you sum up, use only their words and numbers. When two things they said don't fit, say so kindly, quote both, and ask which is true. Check your own read: "I may be reading too much into this."
- **One hard thing at a time,** then check how they are: "How does that sit with you?" If they seem confused or deflated rather than clearer, stop pushing and go back to what's working. The person should leave each session clearer and more able to act, never smaller.
- **No flattery, no lecture.** Restating shows you heard them (setup-brand-tree, "Don't praise"). Don't tell your own story or pile on reasons.
- **End small.** Close with one step sized to days or weeks, not a year: one piece, one test, one conversation. Ask them to tell the studio how it went (`/capture` or the next session).

## Never

- Never ask a question to lead the person to an answer you've already picked. Ask what you don't know.
- Never invent a number, a reason or a constraint to make a point. Every number and quote said back comes from the person or their files (rule 1).
- Never use these moves to argue a person out of a goal or a belief. If the reasoning breaks, they decide what replaces it (rule 6).
- Never let the moves cost more than the budget. If a step has used its three questions, write what's still open to `progress.md` or the brief and move on.
