# /pricing-tests

Method by Josué Valles, the studio's author, from Blake's Kollege calls (2023), where he priced members' offers live.

People who sell coaching, creative work, therapy, design or advice usually set their price by comparison or by fear. They look at what cheap courses cost, at what they charged last time, or at the most they've ever dared to ask. This skill replaces that with tests that put the price next to the client's real outcome, the client's real alternative and the seller's own needs, then turns the result into a price they can test with real buyers.

The cases below are real moments from those calls; the people are described by what they do. They're here to show each test to the person, not as numbers to copy: Blake's ranges come from his markets. Use them to show the move, and never in a piece (they aren't checkable sources).

## Why it matters

- **Cheap comparisons set the wrong anchor.** A member who ran a whiskey club priced it against a streaming subscription. Blake's answer was that a few meetups a year with the people you admire is a different thing, and that the member had built a mastermind and was charging for it like an app. A music teacher had once paid for music school with the same dream his buyers have, yet priced his own course far below it: "It's because of a lack of belief."
- **The seller's belief sets the buyer's belief.** "You should feel very uncomfortable about it… people… feel comfortable because they know that you're comfortable."
- **Adding stuff to justify a price works backwards.** Piling on content and access "is precisely the opposite of what you need to do." "What would I need to remove in order to justify the price?"
- **Value often sits somewhere other than where the seller thinks.** Blake assumed his own members wanted curriculum and "was dead wrong… all that the value is, is just like a phone call with me."
- **Harder-to-measure outcomes can carry higher prices.** "Every time I've slid the scale to qualitative intangibles… the price has gone up and the effort has gone down."

## What it reads

- `canon/vehicle/vehicle.md`: the offer (`O` entry), its current price, the goal, the constraints.
- `canon/bank/proof.md`: results the person has delivered, which the outcome step leans on.
- `canon/strategy/audience.md`: the buyer's desires and words, for the outcome step.
- `material/` and `inbox/`: sales calls, client emails, past prices.
- `pieces/*-offer-*/offer.md`, when `/offer-designer` has run: its value stack and its price range are the starting point; this skill tests the number in full.

**How it relates.** `/offer-designer` shapes what's in the offer and reads its value against the price; this skill sets the number. `/diagnosis-call` is where the price meets a buyer, and it holds the one description of "separate belief from cash" (its step 7) that step 6 here points to.

## How to run it

One question at a time, short replies. Get real numbers, not guesses. When the person doesn't know a number, write it down as unknown; never estimate it for them. Before starting, say what this is, why, what they'll have at the end and how long it takes (about 30 to 45 minutes), as `CLAUDE.md` rule 7 asks.

### 1. Set up

Find out, from the files first and then by asking:

- What they sell, how it's delivered, and how long it runs.
- Who buys it. Blake split buyers three ways: people hoping to start a business, consumers buying for themselves, and businesses buying for a return. Each supports a different price range.
- The current price, how many they've sold, and how they sold them (warm referrals, a list, ads, calls).
- Their close rate on sales conversations, if they have one.
- What they want the work to give them: time and freedom (a lifestyle business), steady income (cash flow), or something they could sell one day (an asset).
- What they need to earn per month to live well.

### 2. Run the needs math

Take what they need per month, multiply by three for a cushion, and divide by the price to get the number of clients needed each month. Run it at their current price and at a price two or three times higher. Do the arithmetic in front of them (`skills/craft-interview`, move 3).

Case: a couple running a course business did the math on the call. At their low price they needed dozens of sales a month to cover the cushion; at ten times the price they needed a handful. Blake asked which job was harder, and pointed out that a marketer would pick the first and the person who knows the work best would pick the second.

Case: a former professional athlete who wanted to teach worked out that a small group of clients at a yearly price covered his whole income target.

Show both versions side by side and ask which business they'd rather run.

### 3. Name the outcome

Ask what clients actually get, then "why does that matter?" until you reach what it changes in their life or business. Ask it about a real client: "Think of the last client this worked for. What changed for them after?" Separate the measurable outcomes from the felt ones, and keep the person's words.

Case: a bass teacher taught players to read music. Asking "why" led past reading to steady gigs, theater shows and cruise-ship work, feeling like a professional, and respect from peers. The offer moved from "learn how to read music in 60 days" to helping bass players "gain confidence in their playing and land stable, higher-priced gigs."

Case: Blake's music company learned that buyers didn't want to earn money from mastering. One young buyer lit up only at "you'll be able to get the sound you have in your head."

### 4. Run the value tests

Pick the tests that fit the work. A business buyer can be priced against their gain; a hobbyist can't.

- **The real alternative.** What would the client spend to get this outcome another way: a degree, private lessons, a consultant, years of trial and error? The bass teacher compared himself to online courses until Blake pointed him to the music college he had paid far more to attend, and his price moved up to sit near that alternative. A guitar teacher was compared to a year of private lessons. The former athlete was told what speakers of his standing are usually paid. The alternative's price needs a source: what the person paid, or a price they can look up.
- **Their worst moment.** Ask what the client would have paid at the lowest point, before the problem was solved. A coach who helps people leave narcissistic relationships was asked what someone at that lowest point would have paid to get out. The answer: "anything."
- **A share of their gain.** For buyers who earn money from the outcome, price at a share of what they take home. For a program teaching basketball trainers to go full time, Blake priced it at about a third of what a trainer takes home in a year.
- **The $30K test.** Ask what the offer would need to be to justify $30,000. Listen to the answer: more features and content means they're thinking in stuff; access to them means closeness; a changed situation means outcome. Then ask what they could remove, not add.
- **The extremes test.** Imagine the same price paid for only one part of the offer: only content, only group calls, only one to one, only community, only events. Ask which version clients would still pay for. For one member's mastermind, the value sat in the group calls and events. If they can, they ask past clients the same question; their answers are the better evidence.

### 5. Check the story behind the number

When the tests point higher than the current price, ask what makes the higher number feel wrong. Ask about the last time they quoted it: "What happened in your head when you said the price on your last call?" Listen for comparisons ("because I've never sold anything over that," as the bass teacher put it) and for guilt about clients who don't succeed (one member lowered his price for that reason and saw no improvement in results). Say what you heard, plainly, in their words, without diagnosing them. The point is to separate the price from the fear, so they decide on the work's value rather than their comfort (`skills/craft-interview`, move 8: this is often a question about who they want to be, not about a number).

Blake's own stance was "free or very expensive," and he later accepted lower prices for people who want a lifestyle business: "There's nothing wrong with selling a $997, $1,997 thing… We don't need to always scale." Treat the price as a choice that follows from what they want, not a rule.

### 6. Turn the price into a test

The person picks a price from the tests. Then agree how to test it with real buyers:

- **Start with warm clients.** Offer existing or past clients the new version first, with a fair upgrade price, before going to strangers.
- **Make the test honest.** A high monthly price that can be cancelled any time shows quickly whether the value is there. Blake told a coach selling a low monthly membership to try roughly three times the price: "Either you suck or they suck… now you're afraid of the offer, which is good. You actually have to fulfill."
- **Read the close rate.** If most conversations close, the price is probably low. Blake's own habit was to raise prices until about a fifth to a quarter of conversations closed, and to raise again when close rates crept up toward a third. Those are his markets' numbers, a starting point to read against, not a target. With only a handful of sales, treat this as a direction, not a statistic.
- **Separate belief from cash** on the sales call, as `/diagnosis-call` step 7 describes; that skill preps the call.

### 7. Hand back

Write `pieces/<yyyy-mm-dd>-pricing-<slug>/pricing.md`:

1. **The offer and buyer,** in a sentence or two.
2. **Needs math** at the current and proposed prices.
3. **The outcome,** measurable and felt, in the person's and their clients' words.
4. **Test results,** one line per test used, with the number each points to and its source.
5. **The price** the person chose and the reasoning in plain words.
6. **The test plan:** who hears it first, how, and what result would mean raise, hold or rethink.
7. **Unknowns** to find out from real buyers.

Then, for the person's yes (`CLAUDE.md` rule 6), along the route in `skills/setup-brand-tree/SKILL.md` ("Where a picked-up belief goes"). The price goes in only as the number the person said.

- **No offer entry, or a `draft` one:** the price goes on the offer's `O` entry in `canon/vehicle/vehicle.md`, with `status: draft` and a log line naming this pricing document (create the entry as a draft if there isn't one). No proposal.
- **A `confirmed` offer:** the entry stays as it is. The new price goes to `proposals.md` only, as `kind: change to O<n>`, quoting the price and pointing to the test plan.

## How to behave

- Keep it a conversation. One question per turn, short replies.
- Use their numbers. Never invent revenue, results, client quotes or a price.
- Don't flatter. If a price is low out of fear, say so plainly and kindly, with the test that shows it.
- Give the principle, then the possible moves. The ranges in the cases come from Blake's markets and won't transfer directly.
- If they have no sales yet, say the first job is a few real conversations at the proposed price, and that the tests are a starting point until then.
- Everything this skill writes follows the house rules (`skills/craft-house-rules`).
