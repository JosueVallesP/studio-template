# The strategy method

How the studio gets from a person's canon to their personal branding strategy: what they should be known for, who to, on which channels, in what shape, how often, and why. This is the method behind the `strategy-brand` skill.

Sources: methods by Josué Valles, the studio's author (the Network Design method, the personal branding model and the value inventory), and the sources listed: Caleb Ralston's course and talks, Alec Paul across nine interviews, Adam Robinson, Gal Aga, Josh Sanders, Tommy Clark's agency material, Tom Hunt, Natalie Barbu and an unnamed 2026 playbook. In all, 25 transcripts of talks, courses and interviews on LinkedIn and personal branding strategy, read against the studio's design. Where the sources disagree, the method says so and says which goal each side is right for. The five routes, the resolutions of the disagreements and section 8 are the studio's own; the sources supply the parts. The decisions in section 9 are applied throughout.

## 1. What the sources agree on

Strip away the platform tactics and every source runs the same sequence, in the same order, with different names for the steps.

1. **Start from the outcome.** Ralston's Brand Journey Framework asks four questions backwards: what do I want to have happen, what would I have to be known for, what would I have to do, what would I have to learn. Sanders says reverse-engineer the pillars from "a very, very clear why." The personal branding model puts the goal at the top of the vehicle and works down. Nobody picks topics or channels before this.
2. **Decide what to be known for, from evidence.** Ralston calls it the associations and anti-associations. Robinson calls it one or two pillars plus the topics next to the problem you solve. Alec Paul calls it a take. The personal branding model calls it the target association. All of them derive it from what the person has actually done and believes, never from what sounds good. The Network Design method puts it as "Don't tell me what you want to provide, tell me what the evidence says you already do." Robinson's failed year was writing about a pain he didn't share.
3. **Pick the people before publishing.** The Network Design method picks them by hand (reverse audience building). Ralston picks a pond, not a lake. Barbu picks the smallest group with the biggest problem and the willingness to pay. Alec narrows the callout until readers see themselves. The playbook builds a dense cluster of similar people so the algorithm knows who to show the post to.
4. **Settle the credibility position.** Nobody knows a new voice, so early content carries proof. Ralston's split between someone who has done it and a student (the studio calls the first "proven"), Alec's credibility-first arc, Robinson's broker of information for people without big numbers.
5. **Give one channel one job.** Short form and feed posts for awareness, something longer and owned (newsletter, long form, DMs) for trust and conversion, the profile as a landing page. Ralston, Sanders, Alec and Robinson all say LinkedIn is where the buyer gets found and somewhere else is where the relationship deepens.
6. **Set a cadence you can hold for two years, then raise it.** Three strong posts a week is the number most of them land on. Ralston's rule is capacity first; Alec's is twelve to twenty-four months; Robinson's is 30 days to find your voice before judging anything.
7. **Give before you sell, then sell in bursts and sideways.** Six to twelve months of value (Alec, Robinson), bury the product until the last lines (Alec), make the company a character (Robinson), share the knowledge and sell the execution (Ralston), never sell directly and always move in angles (the Network Design method).
8. **Measure what the goal needs, not what the platform shows.** Every source says likes and followers mislead. What replaces them depends on the goal (section 5).
9. **Expect the strategy to evolve.** Alec's seasons, Ralston's expand in layers not leaps, Robinson's pillars that emerge from hits, the personal branding model's reputation check against the target association.

The studio's design already holds most of this as documents (brand strategy, audience, content plan). What it lacked was the reasoning that fills them, which is sections 2 to 6.

## 2. The derivation

The strategy reads the canon and answers seven questions in order. Each answer carries its reason, written down, so the person can push back and so the choice can be revisited when the reputation check comes in.

### 2.1 What is the goal, and what does it need the person to be known for?

Read `vehicle/vehicle.md` (the goal) and `strategy/outcomes.md`. Then run the two-directions test from `skills/setup-brand-tree/references/tests.md`: what the person layer can honestly support against what the goal needs. The overlap is the target association.

The sources add one more test. Ralston: write the anti-associations too, the things the person must never be paired with, because a brand is "an intentional pairing of relevant things done consistently" and pairing with the wrong thing costs more than the exposure gains. Anti-associations are a required section of `brand-strategy.md`, linked to the stop list in the canon.

### 2.2 What kind of goal is it?

The goal sorts into one of five routes, and the route decides most of what follows. Section 4 has them. Pick the route before anything else, because the sources' loudest disagreements (reach or narrow, pillars planned or discovered, personal content or not) are each right for one route and wrong for another.

### 2.3 Who, exactly, and are they reachable?

Three gates, all from the sources, in this order:

1. **Has the person been this reader?** Alec: "if you're selling to heads of sales you better have experience as a revenue leader." Robinson's ecommerce posts failed because he wasn't an ecommerce operator. If the answer is no, the canon has a problem the strategy can't fix, and the strategist says so.
2. **Is this reader on the channel?** Gal Aga's disqualifier. Check with Sales Navigator or by finding creators who already serve them. If not, the strategy goes to route-referrals (section 4).
3. **Will they pay, and does anyone else send them?** Barbu's test: saying they need it isn't evidence, paying is. The Network Design method's players layer adds the horizontal relationships: peers, connectors, and the people around the buyer (authority figures, peers, community voices, cultural layer).

Then narrow until it hurts. Alec: "CMO at a B2B SaaS doing 1 to 10 million" beats "CMO at a B2B company," because the right reader self-identifies and the one below aspires. The Network Design method's version is the four blanks: "I specifically help ___ who are struggling with ___ because they're ___ and want to ___." All four have to be concrete. This fills `strategy/audience.md` and the segments in `brand-strategy.md`.

### 2.4 Where does the person's credibility sit?

Three positions, `proven / student / broker`, and the strategy must name one honestly:

- **Proven**: has done the thing the reader wants, with results to show for it. Content leads with results and the method behind them. Alec's early arc: quantified wins, famous companies, the career path, "it's not bragging if it's true."
- **Student**: hasn't done it yet. Ralston: share the hypotheses and results like a scientist; the student frame gets more traction than faking the proven frame, and lying about which one you are is the only wrong answer.
- **Broker**: no big numbers, but access to people or data. Robinson calls it being a broker of information: "I spoke to 20 CEOs over the past 30 days" earns the hook that a résumé can't. Alec's version for himself: "I spoke to 10 early stage startups about their cold email strategies, here's four things that aren't working and one that is." The value inventory covers this too (network, access and learning as value types).

The credibility position changes the opening months. An unknown voice in the proven position leads with proof for the first stretch, then loosens (Alec, Gal). A student never pretends. A broker builds the interview series that the Network Design method's dual harvest describes, which turns the research itself into content and relationships.

### 2.5 What should the person stand against?

Every source that builds lasting brands names an enemy, and every one of them insists it be a system, a belief or a practice, not a person. Ralston: contrarian, not controversial; a belief said to trigger a reaction is the wrong kind. Robinson's prolific zone sits between mainstream and crazy. The Network Design method's frustration test finds it: what widely accepted practice in the field makes the person angry, because the anger "almost always comes from having seen a better way." Alec calls the advanced version "who you stand against," run like storylines in a TV show.

The canon has this as POVs (what each one rejects) and the thesis. The strategy picks the one or two rejections that the target association rests on and writes them as the enemy in `brand-strategy.md`.

### 2.6 What does each channel do, and what shape does the content take?

Section 3 covers the architecture. The answer gets written per channel into `strategy/channels/<channel>.md`, with the channel's job, formats, cadence, mix and why. Format skills read their channel's file.

### 2.7 How will we know it's working?

Pick two to four signals that would change what the studio does, by route (section 5). Ralston: "if it doesn't change your actions, don't track it." Write them into the content plan with the review dates: weekly for the pieces, quarterly for the plan, once or twice a year for the reputation check.

## 3. Channel architecture

### 3.1 One primary, one or two secondary

Ralston's rule for a solo person: one primary platform that gets the pillar content and every experiment, one secondary that gets repurposed, platform-native versions. Never one platform alone, never all of them. With a team, three primaries at most, one in focus at a time while the others run proven formats.

Which is primary follows from 2.3 (where the reader is) and from the person's natural medium. Ralston: pick the medium that comes most naturally and that you can sustain; if video makes you shudder, write. For a reader who buys in a business context, every source puts LinkedIn first, and Ralston calls it the only platform with real organic reach in 2025.

### 3.2 Each channel's job

| Job | Channel | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Get found, and prove the thesis one leaf at a time | LinkedIn feed posts | Buyers are there, titles earn attention, 2% of users post (Robinson) |
| Hold the reader's attention and go deep | Newsletter or essays | Posts can't go deep; the list survives the algorithm; "a million followers but still not reach a million people" (Sanders) |
| Turn a reader into a conversation | DMs, connection requests, the profile | "DMs are where all the business is generated" (Alec); the profile is a landing page, not a resume |
| Make the relationship, not just the impression | Interview and case study series, collaborations | The Network Design method's dual harvest: offer a platform instead of asking for something; 40%+ response versus 2% for cold pitches |
| Show the vehicle | The site, the offer page | Reached from the profile after several posts, never from the post itself |

A channel without a job in this table doesn't get a file.

### 3.3 The connection strategy is part of the channel, not a tactic

Three sources say the same thing independently. Alec: LinkedIn is the only platform where you can choose your following, so connect with the people you want to see your posts and ignore the rest, "you're a bouncer at a nightclub." Robinson: a list of 100 to 250 best prospects, blank requests, DM a thank-you when they engage, wait 60 days before asking for anything. The playbook: connect in clusters (one role in one city before the next) because engagement from similar people tells the algorithm whom to show the post to. The Network Design method's Dream100 is the list version of this. The channel file carries the list rules and the cadence (10 to 20 requests a day is the common number). No automation; every source warns the account is the asset.

### 3.4 The content mix

Four different ratios appear in the sources, and they describe the same shape from different angles:

- Ralston, two ratios from two talks: 80% the one thing you're known for and 20% other interests; and 75% deep (frameworks, painful problems solved, belief shifts), 20% niche-wide (adjacent topics that still serve the reader), 5% personal. Each is a starting default.
- Sanders: expert pillars (narrow, for the one or two readers who buy) and interest pillars (broad, for reach), weighted by goal.
- The Network Design method, for example for a consultant whose work spans many client cases: 50% universal mechanics (thesis and principles), 30% cross-case bridges proving them, 20% specific tactical teardowns, as a starting default.
- Tommy Clark's agency: every post carries a story, proprietary data or a physical element, because the feed is full of AI text and "generic advice content" is dead.

The layer table in `docs/spec.md` says what each layer of the tree produces (thesis gives the origin piece, values give stories of choices, POVs give opinions that name what they reject, branches give frameworks, proof gives cases). The strategy sets the mix across those layers for the quarter, with the ratio chosen by route (section 4) and by the reader's stage of awareness (`docs/spec.md`). It writes the ratio and the reason into the content plan, and it treats the ratio as a guideline, not a rule. Ralston posts about his motorcycles.

Three story types recur in every founder-facing source and belong in the mix for anyone: the origin story (Clark runs it first with every client; Ralston says never retire it because every post reaches a cold reader), the adversity or failure story with the lesson attached (Ralston: share wins too or you read as "a failure constantly learning"), and the customer or case story.

### 3.5 Cadence

The sources converge on three strong posts a week for most people. The studio sets the default per route (section 4) instead of one number for everyone, and the person's capacity trims it. The qualifications behind the defaults:

- Alec: three excellent beats seven okay, because growth comes from the top 2% of posts; never seven days a week, because stopping for six months costs more than the extra posts gained. Robinson posts three, "probably soon to be four"; Alec would do the arithmetic on whether three reaches the growth target before adding more.
- Ralston: start at a cadence that doesn't feel like a push and raise it on a schedule, because the brand that lasts is the one you can sustain without burning out. Build for the capacity you'll have when business is busier, not for today's.
- Gal Aga: once a week through 2023 produced little; three a week from April 2024 is when it worked.
- Robinson: 16 posts in 30 days as a first commitment, to find the voice, not to get leads.

The one real disagreement: Ralston's accordion (high volume first to learn what the audience calls quality, then compress) against Alec's "every post should be an event." Ralston resolves it himself: volume is for people with no insight into their reader yet; anyone who has met two thousand of them can start with effort per piece. For the studio, the canon and audience research are that insight, so the default is effort over volume, and the accordion is reserved for a person starting from nothing.

Time horizon, written into the plan so nobody judges early: a week or two to set up, then a month or two before the content works in a real way, and nothing judged before then (the author's default for the studio). A business outcome is a separate, longer horizon, and the plan says so, so a slow pipeline isn't read as content that failed: the sources put it at up to 36 months for a meaningful business result (Ralston) and no demos in year one for both founders.

### 3.6 Selling

Give for six to twelve months. Then sell sideways: the product as a character in the story (Robinson), the pain the product fixes rather than the feature (Gal, Alec), a PS line once trust exists, where a rare link outperforms a link in every post (Gal's illustration; he himself links often), campaigns in bursts with a defined window rather than a standing ask (Alec), and "share the knowledge, sell the execution" (Ralston). The Network Design method's rule covers all of them: never sell directly, always move in angles.

### 3.7 Platform-native, not reposted

Ralston: repurposing clips works for the first years and then hits a ceiling; the next level gives each platform its own cut and hook. Alec: posts are intellectual property to be re-exploited, "an episode of Friends doesn't only run one time," so year two writes less and remixes the library. Both fit the studio's fractal model (`docs/spec.md`): a leaf compresses to a fragment for one channel and expands to a branch for another, and the strategy says which direction each channel runs.

## 4. Routes by goal

The goal picks the route. The route sets the defaults below; the person's canon adjusts them.

### route-clients: clients for a service, advisory or coaching practice

The person sells their judgment to a narrow group at a high price. The Network Design method is the fullest method for this route, and Ralston's "trust over virality" agrees with it.

The route has two paces, and the person's priority sets the weighting between them. **Clients soon:** the network design defaults below, run hard. **Body of work first:** a coach or consultant who isn't in a rush and wants the brand built on their frameworks and thesis. Body of work first keeps the hand-picked reader and the no-wide-content rule, but spends most of its pieces on the branches of the tree (frameworks, essays, the origin piece) and runs the Dream100 outreach at a lower rate. Most people sit between the two; the strategy writes the split (say 70/30 toward one, a starting default) into the content plan and doesn't present the person with two systems.

- Known for: one target association derived from the value inventory (two or three of the ten value types the evidence supports) and the uniqueness map (capability, perspective, access, identity). The Network Design method's strategic thesis as the filter: "I'm the only ___ who can provide ___ to ___ by refusing to ___, which allows me to ___."
- Reader: hand-picked. A Dream100, a peers and creators list, an ICP list of one to five thousand, and two or three internal champions per Dream100 target. Weighted 70/20/10 (a starting default) toward founder-led and scale-up companies for results soon, with mid-market and enterprise for long-term value.
- Mix: deep, 75% or more (a starting default). Ralston's warning is the rule here: wide content dilutes the message, brings in people who won't buy, and the buyers tune out. Hyper-personalized pieces for named targets (the prophet, the permissionless apprentice, the inquirer) produce most of the results. Topic-popular posts (HR, workplace, LinkedIn itself) are off the table; Tom Hunt's admission that his attention and his revenue came from different topics is the cautionary case.
- Channels: LinkedIn for proof and the lists, DMs and collaborative series for the relationship, a newsletter or essays for depth. Dual harvest runs the funnel: ten to twenty names at a time, content that demonstrates value, engagement, then an invitation into something collaborative.
- Cadence, clients soon: one or two targeted pieces a week for three months before judging, plus up to three ordinary pieces if the person wants reach too; follow-up rhythm two weeks, then a month. Body of work first: two or three pieces a week, most of them from the branches, with one targeted piece every week or two.
- Signals: inbound referrals, private thank-yous, mentions in rooms the person isn't in, people adopting the frameworks and language, conversation quality. Not followers. The Network Design method: "a pool of a hundred people who need exactly what you do is worth more than ten thousand indifferent spectators."

### route-pipeline: pipeline for a company the person leads

A founder or executive whose company sells to people on LinkedIn. Alec Paul, Adam Robinson and Gal Aga are on this route.

- Known for: a take that differs from accepted best practice in the buyer's world, plus the company as a live case proving it (the personal branding model's vehicle layer and Proof Bridge; Robinson's company as a character). Alec's screen applies: credibility, experience relevant to the buyer, a point of view, willingness to be spicy. A figurehead without unusual ideas runs the account as a distribution channel for announcements instead, which Alec calls "a totally legitimate strategy" and a different route.
- Reader: the buyer, narrowed to title, company size and stage, and connected to on purpose: the Robinson connect campaign or the playbook's clusters, and Alec's five above and ten below the decision maker in an account motion.
- Mix: tangential pain (the problems the buyer has that the product sits next to), credibility stories from the career, the enemy storyline, build-in-public with stakes. Personal posts only where the buyer cares (Clark: "I just got engaged, here's what it taught me about B2B sales" is the failure). Proprietary data as a moat where the company has it.
- Channels: LinkedIn feed as the acquisition channel, the profile as the landing page, DMs after engagement, and Alec's executive go-to-market: the executive's voice in outbound, renewals and reactivation once the brand exists. The sources in this branch also run thought leader ads on posts the ICP already liked; the studio leaves paid out for now.
- Cadence: three a week, four if growth needs it. Robinson averages 36 a month and is the outlier.
- Signals: self-reported LinkedIn attribution at signup (Gal: 60 to 65% of leads), connection accept rate, ICP comments and DMs in the first 90 days, dark social mentions in the next 90, share of pipeline after six months (the playbook's 20% goal), plus the returns nobody puts on the dashboard: recruiting, investors, partners, speaking.

### route-buyers: an audience business

The person sells courses, a community, a book or media to many people at lower prices. Sanders (Chris Donnelly's growth), Ralston's monetization model and Robinson's free course describe it.

- Known for: Sanders's two kinds of pillars, weighted toward interest pillars (broad topics the business touches) with expert pillars underneath. Growth outside the buying audience counts as social proof here, which it doesn't on route-clients.
- Reader: the ideal customer for the offer, "not necessarily the current audience you have" (Ralston), reached through broad topics and pulled into the owned list.
- Mix: educational depth that's actionable within minutes (Sanders: that's why people follow), carousels and cheat sheets for dwell time, the Slack test for shareability (can a reader share this at work; a personal post can't be), 70/20/10 across proven formats, iterations and swings (a starting default) so the format doesn't fatigue.
- Channels: LinkedIn as top of funnel, the newsletter as the trust layer and the real asset, lead magnet posts to move readers over, then Ralston's offer stack (free, lead magnet, low, mid, high ticket), one offer at a time.
- Cadence: four or more a week, higher than the other routes and raised on a schedule; the accordion applies at the start of each new platform (about 12 posts in four weeks to learn).
- Signals: reposts, saves and sends on posts (Sanders: a 20% repost rate in the first hour meant a hit), list growth, then the conversion rate per platform rather than the lead count (Ralston moved his budget to LinkedIn because it converted best, not because it brought most).

### route-role: a career, a seat or capital

The person wants a role, a board seat, investors or partners. Nobody in the sources runs this route as a business, but the Network Design method's Dream100 by goal and Alec's referral-business client cover it.

- Known for: the same target association as any other route, but the proof is the career arc itself. Alec's credibility-first opening applies for longer, and Ralston's turning-points introduction (three to five turning points, each with a lesson) is the first piece.
- Reader: the Network Design method's job-search Dream100: founders of target companies, the person who would be the boss, recruiters, employees who can refer; or for capital, customers who talk about the problem in public, investors, partner CEOs, analysts.
- Mix: deep on the one thing, with the student frame where the person is moving into something new. Low personal share.
- Channels: LinkedIn plus the DMs and real-world network. Alec's real estate client never grew an audience and had his best year because people who already knew him saw him weekly.
- Cadence: lower is fine, once or twice a week held for a year beats a burst. Alec: even once a month beats not starting, because it only gets harder.
- Signals: referrals and introductions, conversations started, who's reading (profile views from the lists). Not followers, not likes.

### route-referrals: the reader isn't on LinkedIn, or the business runs on referrals

Nurses, construction supervisors, local clients, or a business that already runs on word of mouth. Gal Aga's disqualifier and Alec's non go-to-market clients.

- Don't invest heavily, and don't aim for a large audience. Alec: you're early, there's white space, so get targeted with connections, test for a couple of months before committing years, and find the feedback mechanism where the reader actually is (Twitter, newsletters, blogs, podcasts) because "you can't just start from scratch."
- Keep a minimum viable presence anyway (Robinson): recruiting, partners and investors still look.
- Measure differently: referrals from the real-world network, not reach.
- If a different channel holds the reader, this method still applies; only the channel files change.

### Choosing when the goal is mixed

Most people want two of these. The rule from the sources: pick the one the goal needs first and let the second ride on it. Sanders: expert pillars for business growth now, interest pillars to grow a brand; weight, don't choose. The Network Design method's content filter per piece: decide whether this piece is for reach or for relationships and don't try both at once, because they need different content. The content plan carries the weighting for the quarter.

## 5. Signals by route

| Route | Watch | Ignore |
| --- | --- | --- |
| route-clients | Referrals, private thank-yous, language adopted, conversation quality, calls booked | Followers, likes, impressions |
| route-pipeline | Attribution at signup, ICP comments and DMs, dark social mentions, share of pipeline, connection accept rate | Total follower count, viral posts outside the ICP |
| route-buyers | Reposts, saves, sends, list growth, conversion rate per platform | Views alone |
| route-role | Introductions, conversations, who's reading from the lists | Everything else |
| route-referrals | Referrals from the real network | Reach |

Two signals every route keeps: the reputation check once or twice a year (what people say first when they hear the name, against the target association) and the person's own test of whether the strategy still feels true. Ralston's test for any move: will this make the audience trust me more or less. If less, don't.

## 6. Where the sources disagree, and what the studio does

**Pillars planned or discovered.** Robinson says you can't pick pillars on a spreadsheet, they emerge when a post hits and a second angle hits again. Alec refuses a twelve-month plan. The Network Design method and Ralston derive them from the thesis and the enemy before publishing. The studio does both: the canon gives the hypotheses (the branches of the tree), the first quarter tests them, and the content plan is rewritten each quarter from what the audience confirmed. A branch that never lands is a signal about the branch or the reader, not a reason to abandon the canon.

**Reach or narrow.** Sanders grows wide and calls outside-ICP growth social proof. Ralston, Clark and the playbook say wide content dilutes the audience and teaches the algorithm the wrong reader. Both are right for their route: wide for route-buyers, narrow for route-clients and route-pipeline. The studio never lets a route-clients or route-pipeline person chase a topic-popular post for reach.

**Personal content.** Robinson: say what you'd say to your spouse. Sanders: nobody can share a baby announcement in Slack. Clark: only if the ICP cares. Resolved by route and by Ralston's 5%: a text-only poster becomes a face with the occasional personal post (Alec), but the personal post is never the piece that carries the quarter.

**Volume or effort.** Covered in 3.5. Effort by default; volume only for someone with no reader insight.

**Length.** Alec used to say max out 3,000 characters and has since reversed toward medium posts on one idea, because readers have less bandwidth and the feed carries more volume. The playbook still says 400 to 800 words win. The studio treats this as a format question the LinkedIn skill owns and re-tests, not a strategy question.

**Video.** Robinson says video is the deepest trust; Gal won't do it because the return per hour is lower than a fourth text post; Alec says short vertical video doesn't work on LinkedIn, a text platform; Clark says once a week so people hear you. The studio follows Ralston's medium rule: the person's natural medium first, video added when text works and the person wants it.

**AI in the writing.** Alec, Robinson and Ralston all say drafts smoothed by AI do worse and readers now assume dramatic stories are fake; the HFS host uses a custom GPT to sharpen his own drafts and finds it works. The studio's position is already in its design (`CLAUDE.md` rule 1): facts only from the canon, the person's own words kept, nothing invented. The warning the sources add is about voice, which is why the voice guide and the editor exist.

**Ghostwriting.** Alec won't write for clients because "the writing is the actual thinking" and the person has to own it. The studio takes a hybrid position. The person's thinking enters through continuous inputs that are already theirs: calls, their work, the story bank, a journal, existing content and books, plus the strategist's interviews. The studio drafts from that and nothing else, and the person decides. Setup says this to the person plainly so they know what's expected of them: keep the inputs flowing, and make the calls.

## 7. The character sheet

The studio doesn't use brand archetypes (hero, sage, outlaw), and neither does any source. What the sources use instead:

- Ralston's credibility position: proven or student (his word for the first is "expert").
- Alec's two CEO styles: the celebrity distribution channel and the thought leadership builder.
- Finn's two tracks, which Gal Aga has both of: depth almost nobody has (thought leadership) and a phrase that catches on (a unique point of view).
- The personal branding model's three levels of personal branding: egocentric, customer-centric, purpose-centric, aiming for the third.
- The value inventory's ten value types, of which a person provides two or three.
- Robinson's public figure, built the way a PR agency built one fifty years ago, by telling the same story in many ways.

Taken together these make the person's character sheet, which `brand-strategy.md` carries in four lines: the credibility position (2.4), the enemy (2.5), the two or three value types, and the purpose level. The genius-of-contradiction method (`skills/craft-contradiction`) and the personal branding model's inner matrix (worldview, identity, values, mental models) are where it gets richer later.

## 8. What this changes in the studio

- A new skill, `strategy-brand`, runs the derivation in section 2 once the person layer, the vehicle and the audience research are confirmed. It reads the canon and writes `brand-strategy.md` with reasons, one file per channel in `strategy/channels/`, and the first content plan. `setup-brand-tree` hands over to it instead of asking the channel and cadence questions itself.
- `brand-strategy.md` gains: the route and, for route-clients, the pace, with reasons; the character sheet (credibility position, enemy, value types, purpose level); a required anti-associations section linked to the stop list; the cadence for the route; and the time horizon.
- A new template, `strategy/channels/<channel>.md`: job, formats, cadence, mix, connection rules, selling rules, signals, review date. Format skills read their channel's file for purpose and cadence.
- The content plan gains the signals to watch for the quarter and the route's weighting between reach and relationships.
- `setup-start` tells the person what to expect about time at "Setup done" (and at the welcome only if they ask how long it takes): a week or two to set up, then a month or two before the content works in a real way, and that the studio drafts from their inputs and interviews while they keep the inputs flowing and make the calls.
- The interview order already matches the personal branding model: start with concrete tactics and experiences and work up to values, because asked directly people give aspirational answers.

## 9. Decisions

1. No archetypes. The character sheet in section 7 is the model.
2. The network design defaults for route-clients hold, but only when getting clients soon is the person's first priority. Coaches and consultants who aren't in a rush build on their frameworks first (body of work first), and most people get a weighted hybrid without being shown two systems.
3. Cadence is set per route, not one studio default.
4. Anti-associations is the term, and it's a required section.
5. No thought leader ads or other paid for now.
6. The ghostwriting stance is a hybrid: the person's thinking arrives through continuous inputs (calls, work, story bank, journal, existing content, books) and interviews; the studio drafts from them; the person decides.
