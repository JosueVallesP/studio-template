## Post

A head of engineering told me: "I promoted my best engineer and lost two people."

She didn't lose them because he was a bad person. She lost them because nobody told him that his job had changed from writing the best code to making sure other people could.

You could read that as a bad pick and promote someone else next time. I don't think that fixes it. I've coached 140 new engineering managers since 2021, and almost all of them do the same thing in month two: they keep the hardest technical work for themselves "because it's faster." It is faster. For about six weeks. Then you're the bottleneck on every important ticket and your team learns that the interesting work isn't for them.

At one client, we ran my cohort for 9 new managers at once instead of coaching them one by one. It's one company and a small group, so take it as one result. After six months, regretted attrition on their teams was 4%, against 15% for the managers promoted the year before without it. Same company, same pay, same CTO.

We train people for months to become senior engineers. We give them a weekend to become managers.

## Argument check

Claim: when a newly promoted engineer loses people, the cause is that nobody taught them the job changed, not that the wrong person was picked; teaching new managers together is one way to fix it.

Justification and instruction: the reason (nobody told him the job changed) leads to an implied instruction (teach new managers the job). "Job" means the same thing in both: what the manager is responsible for. Holds.

Premises:
- P1: The head of engineering lost two people after promoting her best engineer. Sound (her words, as Dana reported them).
- P2: The cause was that nobody told him his job changed. Weak on its own: one case and Dana's reading of it. P3 and P4 support it.
- P3: Almost all of 140 new managers keep the hardest technical work in month two. Sound as Dana's observation across a sample she names.
- P4 (hidden): if it were a bad pick, it wouldn't show up in almost everyone. Sound: a pattern across 140 points at the role, not the person.
- P5: The cohort group had 4% regretted attrition against 15% the year before. Sound as reported.
- P6 (hidden): the cohort caused the gap. Weak: different year, different people, nine managers. The post states the limit ("one company and a small group, so take it as one result") and no longer claims only one thing changed.
- P7 (hidden): "a weekend" is literal. Weak as fact; it reads as Dana's figure of speech, and it's her line.

Lenses:
- Logic: "almost all" is a strong word, and it's Dana's, backed by her sample of 140.
- Ontology: passes. A cohort is a real, named thing.
- Epistemology: P6 is a small sample and a year-on-year comparison; stated in the post.
- Teleology: passes. The reader wants to keep their people; the post points at what helps that.
- Hermeneutics: "regretted attrition" is a term heads of engineering use; passes for this reader.
- Language: "job" keeps one meaning. Passes.
- Ethics: no pressure or fear. Passes.
- Aesthetics: argument shape fits an argument. Passes.

Research: skipped; the logic and Dana's own numbers settle it for a single post.

Refined thesis (no ID, quick start): New managers lose people when nobody teaches them that the job changed; at one company, training nine of them together came with far fewer regretted leavers.

Belief gate: asked Dana, "Do you believe this, and would you say it to a client without being asked?" She said yes. Not written to `proposals.md` (quick-start pieces never write to the canon; see studio notes).
