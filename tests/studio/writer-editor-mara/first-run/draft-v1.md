# Draft v1

## Copylosophy, short run on the brief

The claim: in a bootstrapped software company, revenue growth can hide a cash problem until it is expensive to fix, and the way it hides is that you hire before the cash can carry the hire and the monthly P&L has no way to show you.

The argument:
- P1: Hiring before the cash can carry it is the failure she sees most in bootstrapped software (P4, confirmed; evidence S1).
- P2 (hidden): The monthly P&L reports the month that already happened and says nothing about the payrolls coming in the next three months. This is what a P&L is, so logic settles it; the brief's "shows it a quarter late" is sharper than anything in the canon, so the draft doesn't use that number.
- P3 (hidden): A 13-week view looks 13 weeks ahead, so a week going red shows up before it arrives. Definitional. The draft does not claim it "would have shown it months earlier" for these two clients; nobody ran one at the time.
- P4: After S1 both clients ran the view and neither has gone below four months since; across 11 clients the median went from 2.8 to 5.1 months (R1). Association, not proof. The draft says so (the brief's "not saying" line asks for it too).
- Conclusion: a founder who checks cash months weekly sees the problem while it is still cheap to fix.

Lenses: logic passes once "would have shown it earlier" is dropped and the 13-week horizon is stated as what it is. Epistemology: "common failure" rests on her confirmed POV, so the draft says it as her view ("the failure I see most"), not as a statistic. Teleology: the reader wants to stop being surprised by cash (D1); checking cash months serves that directly. Hermeneutics: "hired ahead" could be heard as "don't hire", so the draft names where hiring ahead is fine (P4's own wording: the failure is two quarters before the cash can carry it). Language: "cash months" throughout, never "runway" (W2), including when restating R1. Ethics: the story is a near miss told flat; no fear line, no shame for the reader who hasn't looked (D1 WON'T). Aesthetics: story arc fits a two-case story; the lesson after is kept to a few lines.

Refined thesis: same as the brief's one idea, with the mechanism named: two companies growing 60 percent a year hired before the cash could carry it, the monthly P&L couldn't show it, and a weekly 13-week view is what they run now. Traces to T, P1, P4. No change to the brief needed.

## Format stage output

```
HOOK OPTIONS

A. Two of my clients ran below two months of cash in the same quarter of 2024. Both had revenue growing 60 percent a year.
   Mechanism check: time (one quarter of 2024), one concrete event (below two months of cash), a detail that doesn't fit (growth of 60 percent). The reader is in the moment before deciding whether to care, and it leads to a turn.

B. In 2024 one of my clients made payroll because the founder lent the company the money. Revenue was growing 60 percent a year.
   Mechanism check: the founder loan is the exact detail; payroll is the moment; growth is the detail that doesn't fit. Leads somewhere. Loses the "two companies, same quarter" point the brief wants in front.

C. I watched two bootstrapped software companies run below two months of cash in the same quarter. Both were growing revenue 60 percent a year, and both had hired ahead of the cash.
   Mechanism check: speaker present, moment present, detail that doesn't fit present. Gives away the complication (the hires) in the hook, so the body's first beat has less to do.

Chosen: A.

POST

Two of my clients ran below two months of cash in the same quarter of 2024. Both had revenue growing 60 percent a year.

Both had hired ahead of the cash: new people on payroll before the money to carry them was in the bank. Hiring ahead isn't the problem by itself. Hiring two quarters before the cash can carry it is, and in bootstrapped software that's the failure I see most, more than bad product.

One of them made payroll that quarter because the founder lent the company the money.

Nothing in the monthly P&L had warned either of them, and to be honest that isn't the P&L's fault. It reports the month that already happened. It has no line for the payrolls coming in the next three months.

Both now run a 13-week cash view. One page, updated every Monday. Neither has gone below four months of cash since.

Across my 11 clients, the median went from 2.8 months of cash to 5.1 within a year of running the weekly view. I don't claim the view prevents every cash crunch. I claim it shows you the problem while it is still cheap to fix.

Revenue is the story you tell. Cash is the story that's true.

If you run a bootstrapped software company, do one thing before this month closes. Take the cash in the bank and divide it by what leaves the account in a normal month. That's your cash months. Reply with your cash months and I'll tell you what I'd look at first.

CLOSE ALT

Small instruction only, no C1:
If you run a bootstrapped software company, do one thing before this month closes. Take the cash in the bank and divide it by what leaves the account in a normal month. That's your cash months. Write it down, and look at it again next Monday.

PARTS MAP

Hook: lines 1 and 2 ("Two of my clients ... 60 percent a year.")
Setup and complication: paragraph 2 (the hires, with P4's condition)
What happened: paragraph 3 (the founder loan)
Why nobody saw it: paragraph 4 (the P&L)
The turn: "Both now run a 13-week cash view."
Result: "Neither has gone below four months of cash since." plus the R1 paragraph with its limit
The meaning line: "Revenue is the story you tell. Cash is the story that's true."
Close: last paragraph, small instruction (C3) ending on C1 (C4)
```
